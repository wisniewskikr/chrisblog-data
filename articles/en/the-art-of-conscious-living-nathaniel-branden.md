# Content

## Very often a person is highly conscious in one area and not in another: for example work and personal life.

- A person can be very conscious in one area (e.g. work) and barely conscious in another (e.g. personal life).
- Goals can be material, emotional, or spiritual.

## 1. How to live consciously - basic principles

- Conscious living begins with respect for facts and truth.
- Justification is the enemy of consciousness.
- Children need an atmosphere in which they are convinced they live in a rational world: a fact is a fact, and truth is respected.
- To live consciously, one must acquire a "sense of reality", based on three laws:
  - The law of identity: everything is what it is (a car will not turn into an ice cream).
  - The law of causality: everything acts in accordance with its nature.
  - The law of non-contradiction: something cannot be both A and not-A at the same time.
- Because we live in the reality we do, we must understand it and adapt to it.
- It is more worthwhile to correct one's mistakes than to pretend they were never made.
- Consciousness consists of an external and an internal dimension; to act effectively, one must maintain contact with both the world and oneself.
- There is a close connection between rationality and self-esteem.
- Rationality is the ability to perceive relationships: induction (forming general principles from facts), deduction (applying general principles to facts), and integration (relating new knowledge to existing knowledge).
- Reality can be known through knowledge, faith, or feelings, but only knowledge has a built-in self-correction mechanism; making mistakes is part of reality.

## 2. Choice and responsibility

- We can control consciousness through our will.
- Different states of consciousness depend on context — from deep concentration to automatization.
- Success belongs to those who approach life proactively rather than passively, placing independence over dependence.

## 3. Conscious living - I know what I am doing when I am doing it

- The problem arises when people make important life decisions with a low level of consciousness.
- Life consists of pursuing goals; the key question is: What do I need to know in order to achieve my goals?
- There is a connection between conscious living and self-acceptance.
- The impulse to escape is sometimes good, but usually not; it can be mastered by developing a sense of reality and self-discipline.

## 4. Conscious living - I am as attentive as needed

- We have different levels of consciousness toward different people: toward a waiter, only the basic level characteristic of a courteous person is needed; toward a companion, a much higher level is required.
- To know another person, one must first know oneself.
- Relationships differ in character: in a parent–child relationship one side gives more and the other takes more; in a partnership both sides give and take roughly equally.
- Critical thinking is important — it allows one to move beyond ingrained patterns.
- The more conscious we are, the more possibilities we notice, the more options we have to choose from, and the better we adapt.

## 5. Self-awareness: knowing your inner world

- The body must be cared for and looked after.
- Being unaware of one's feelings does not make them disappear — they begin to govern us subconsciously, which is dangerous.
- The normal cycle of emotions: an emotion is experienced, expressed through the body's response, and then discharged.
- Emotions carry an impulse to act, but we do not have to follow it.
- Emotions are a reflection of reality — the inner reality.
- The ability to feel aids rather than hinders thinking, because it provides access to important internal information.
- The art and discipline of continuous self-observation, combined with non-judgmental self-acceptance, is essential.
- Just as a driver who goes into a skid must learn to steer into it, a person who falls into an unwanted emotion must learn the art of "going with it" rather than "resisting it".
- Sometimes emotions are right and sometimes logic is; it is worth allowing emotions in order to have more information.

## 6. Consciousness and self-acceptance

- Self-acceptance means recognising oneself as a person competent to meet life's challenges.
- The six pillars of self-esteem:
  1. Self-acceptance: accepting one's own thoughts and emotions without denying them.
  2. Responsibility: we are the authors of our own decisions.
  3. Assertiveness: willingness to stand up for one's views.
  4. Purposefulness: defining goals and taking actions that lead toward them.
  5. Integrity: consistency between what we know, claim, and do.
- Self-acceptance creates potential, but not the outcome; the outcome arises from the potential.

## 7. Consciousness and spirituality

- Spirituality is the care for consciousness, its needs, and its development.
- The spiritual life consists of seeking an ever clearer picture of reality and one's place in it.
- Spirituality is a way of living, not a set of beliefs; religion can draw one away from reality and therefore from spirituality.
- Spirituality evolves — what was once an exception becomes the norm (e.g. abstract thinking).
- No human trait is more necessary for survival than the ability to discriminate (e.g. food from poison); a mature individual is aware of more distinctions than an immature one.
- At our level of development we need evidence that someone knows something better (e.g. an older brother, an engineer vs. a user); mystics do not provide such evidence.
- We do not always arrive at conclusions rationally, but we always verify them rationally — the reality test.
- It is not kindness, compassion, or selflessness that lift people out of poverty, but the will and ability to act.

## Afterword

- Self-realisation — the fulfilment of the potential existing within a person — is the noblest goal of our existence.
