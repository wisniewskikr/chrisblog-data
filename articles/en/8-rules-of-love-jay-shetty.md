# Content

## Introduction

- The difference between liking and loving: when you like a flower, you pick it; when you love it, you water it every day.
- Love is a daily effort.

## The four ashrams

- The four ashrams are: preparing for love, practising love, protecting love, perfecting love.

**1. Preparing for love**
- Learning to love ourselves.
- Principles: compassion, empathy and patience; analysing past relationships to avoid repeating the same mistakes.

**2. Practising love**
- Not only chemistry and compatibility, but also respect and support.
- Principles: how we know we love someone; how to grow with a partner; priorities.

**3. Protecting love**
- A place of healing after a break-up or a loss of intensity.
- Principles: how to resolve conflicts in order to grow love; the decision to break up.

**4. Perfecting love**
- The ideal: we give love to every person and every moment.
- Principle: we keep learning to love again and again.
- Love is about how we deal with imperfections.

## Part I. Solitude: learning to love yourself

**Rule 1. Allow yourself to be alone**

- Fear of loneliness can impair our ability to make the right decisions.
- By learning to love ourselves we develop compassion, empathy and patience.
- Solitude is the antidote to loneliness; obstacles to achieving it: presence, discomfort, confidence.
- The path: loneliness → presence → discomfort → confidence → solitude.
- Presence: monitoring our own wellbeing and noticing our values.
- Discomfort: feeling bad when alone with ourselves.
- Confidence: a sense of belief in our own strength arising from appreciating our abilities and qualities.
- When we are alone we learn to create space between sensory stimuli and decision-making.
- Self-control and patience allow us not to blindly follow our senses and what attracts us.

## Rule 2. Don't ignore your karma

- In youth, choices are made for us → they become impressions → in adulthood, impressions are used to make choices → they lead to consequences.
- External influences shaping our choices: parents, media, first romantic experiences.
- Parents: gaps in the way we were raised make us look for others to fill them; gifts from upbringing make us seek the same in others. We focus too much on what we received from our parents and too little on what we can do ourselves.
- Media: we are guided by first impressions – we need to be aware of this.
- First romantic experiences: the prefrontal cortex is fully developed only at age 25; before that we are driven by emotions, which is why feelings are so intense.
- Three types of intelligence: (1) you hear fire burns and avoid it; (2) you hear it, try it, then avoid it; (3) you hear it and keep trying anyway.
- Five types that attract us: the rebel (fights the system), the chase (emotionally or physically unavailable), the project (the partner needs to be saved), the fuckboy (someone who sleeps around), the wealthy (types of wealth: knowledge, fame, prosperity, beauty, strength, renunciation). Being attracted to what a partner has or has achieved is not a bad start, but it is not a good ending – abilities and achievements are less important than character and actions.

## Part 2. Compatibility: learning to love others

**Rule 3. Define love before you think about it, feel it, or express it in words**

- "I love you" has a wide range of meanings – from "I want to spend my life with you" to "I want to spend the night with you".
- Love takes time – you need to get to know someone's mind and heart.

**Four stages of love:**

*I. Attraction*
- We feel a spark of interest and explore whether someone is worth our time and effort; we reveal our vulnerable points.
- During dates it is worth establishing: do you like this person's personality, do you respect their values, would you want to help them achieve their goals?
- Questions about personality (what shaped this person): e.g. the best meal they've ever had, favourite holidays, a book or film they've returned to more than once, what occupies their mind most right now.
- Questions about values (who this person is now): e.g. the most fascinating person they've ever met, the greatest difficulty they've overcome, what they're proud of, what they would do if they didn't have to work.
- Questions about goals (how this person sees themselves in the future): e.g. a dream they'd like to fulfil, what they'd like to change in their life, who they consider their greatest teacher.

*II. Dreams*
- When someone attracts us for a longer period, we begin to fantasise about a relationship and start grounding those dreams in reality.
- We need to discard false expectations.
- The secret of a good relationship: attraction by choice, not by need.
- One person should not meet all our needs – it is good to have diverse relationships.
- How we handle our differences is more important than finding our similarities.
- Instead of chasing a dream, take time to get to know the other person; introduce rhythms and routines that build structure.
- In the first month, meet no more than once a week to strengthen the moments spent together.
- A helpful schedule: evenings alone, evenings together, evenings with mutual friends, evenings with your own friends.

*III. Struggles and growth*
- We discover whether we want to put effort into solving problems or living with them.
- Outcome: we part ways or we solve the problems.

*IV. Trust*
- After facing challenges together we learn tolerance, adjustment and adaptation – this turns into trust.
- Trust must start with yourself: what you think, say and do must be consistent.

## Rule 4. Your partner is your guru

- Your partner should be like a mirror in which you see yourself and should broaden your perception.
- It is worth asking your partner: what matters to them, what they need to get there, and whether you can help in any way.

## Rule 5. Purpose is the most important thing

- The four human pursuits: purpose, work and finances, pleasure and connection, spirituality.
- A sense of purpose protects self-worth.
- A relationship consists of three bonds: between the partners, one partner with their purpose, and the other partner with their purpose.
- Passion: if someone could pay you to do anything, what would it be?
- Purpose is where passion meets skill.

## Part 3. Healing: learning to love through facing difficulties

**Rule 6. Win or lose together**

- Every couple argues – or should. Main areas of conflict: money, sex, parenting.
- The loser in an argument should not be one of the partners, but the idea or problem.
- A wave is the issue you disagree on – you must face it together.
- Types of conflict: pointless (ignorance), seeking advantage (passion), productive (kindness).
- Arguing styles: venting anger, fleeing, explosion.
- Planning a fight: choosing the right place and time; carefully choosing words and tone; saying "we" instead of "I" and "you"; replacing "I think" with "I feel"; managing anger; commitment; evolution.

## Rule 7. A break-up won't break you

- Only 15% of relationships manage to overcome infidelity.
- Three perspectives when considering leaving:
  1. Examining the temptation: would you stay with your current partner if you hadn't met this new person? If yes – try to save the relationship.
  2. Examining reality: problems will arise in a new relationship too – are you prepared to work on them?
  3. Examining karma: your new partner may do the same to you; leave when there is no future with your current partner.
- Activities that benefit couples: travelling, volunteering, learning something new together.
- Mild stress can stimulate romantic feelings.
- Ask your partner "why".
- When breaking up: be gentle – the pain you send into the world will come back to you. Talk about differing values and goals, not blame.

## Part 4. Connection: learning to love everyone

**Rule 8. Love again and again**

- We should not give love to only one person, but to many.
- Focus on giving love, not on receiving it.
- First refine love with your partner, then extend it to others.
- It is worthwhile to bring love into the workplace as well.
