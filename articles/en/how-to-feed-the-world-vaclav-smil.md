# Content

## Introduction

- We currently supply approximately 3,000 kcal per person per day.
- We waste approximately 1,000 kcal per person per day.

## 1. What do we owe to agriculture?

- We diverged from other primates 6 million years ago.
- The first Homo appeared 2 million years ago.
- Homo sapiens emerged 300,000 years ago.
- Agriculture is approximately 7,000 years old.
- Hunting and gathering works well for small populations; forager groups numbered between 25 and 500 people.
- At one point the number of Homo sapiens dropped to only 20,000 individuals; the minimum viable population is estimated at 100,000.
- A slow process of domesticating plants and animals followed.
- Earth's population reached 1 billion in 1810 and 8 billion in 2020, meaning roughly 1 hectare feeds 5 people on average.
- Recommended macronutrient ratios: carbohydrates (45–65%), fats (20–35%), proteins (10–35%).
- Cereals and legumes have become the dominant energy source; there is no realistic alternative to the current food system.

## 2. Why do we eat the plants we eat and not others?

- Just 20 plant species account for 75% of global harvests.
- This narrow base reflects the need for crops with high energy density that together supply all three macronutrients.
- Domesticated plants are varieties adapted to human needs – sweeter, larger, etc.
- Sugarcane and maize are used to produce ethanol as a motor fuel.
- Wheat has 7 times the energy density of fruit and 18 times that of vegetables, yet it provides insufficient fat and protein – legumes make up the difference.
- Storage losses for wheat are about 1%, while rice losses in China are about 15%.
- After accounting for harvest losses, storage, and seed, only about 50% of the crop reaches consumption.
- Cassava can be harvested year-round; potatoes only during a specific season.

## 3. The limits of crop productivity

- Photosynthesis is the direct production of phytomass from sunlight, water, macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur) and micronutrients (from boron to zinc).
- Eggs, meat, milk, and mushrooms are examples of indirect production from phytomass.
- Only minerals (from iron to zinc) do not originate from photosynthesis.
- The efficiency of modern engines is 39%, LED bulbs 80–90%, and photosynthesis approximately 1%.
- The low efficiency of photosynthesis stems from plants using only visible light, reflecting green light, and being unable to store excess light – only 7% of solar energy is effectively used.
- After subtracting the energy the plant needs for its own metabolism, the useful efficiency drops to about 4.6%.
- Three photosynthetic pathways: C3 (most common, e.g. rice and wheat, efficiency 4.6%) and C4 (e.g. sugarcane and maize, no photorespiration, efficiency 6%).
- Modern varieties are more productive thanks to shorter stalks, which reduce the proportion of inedible plant material.
- Water-use efficiency: 400 to 1,600 grams of water are needed to fix one new carbon atom; wheat production requires 1,600 tonnes of water per tonne of grain.
- Agriculture consumes 80% of the world's freshwater resources.
- Higher CO₂ concentrations improve yields – a principle applied in greenhouses.
- Three tonnes of wheat require 87 kg of nitrogen but only 11 kg of phosphorus and potassium.

## 4. Why do we eat the animals we eat and not others?

- Vegetarians make up about 5% of the population in wealthy countries.
- History of animal domestication in the area of Turkey, Iran, and Iraq: goats and sheep – c. 11,000 years ago; pigs – c. 10,500 years ago; cattle – c. 10,000 years ago; horses – c. 4,500 years ago.
- The larger the animal, the less energy it uses per kilogram of body mass.
- Beef production is less efficient than pork production.
- Cattle were raised primarily for transport and milk; large amounts of beef are consumed today because horses are no longer needed for transport and there is extensive grazing land available.
- CAFO facilities supply 70% of poultry and more than 50% of pork.
- Feed conversion ratio per kilogram of meat: chickens – 2.7; pork – 9; beef – 30.
- Methane is 28 times more harmful to the climate than CO₂.

## 5. What matters more: food or smartphones?

- Agriculture's official share of GDP is about 1%, but this figure is understated as it excludes transport, processing, and retail.
- The real contribution of the food sector is estimated at about 5% of GDP.
- Agriculture consumes about 20% of a country's energy and 72% of its freshwater.
- Accounting for the entire chain – from farming to retail – the food sector represents 25–30% of global GDP.
- Agriculture occupies 36% of ice-free land.
- Fertilisers cause serious water pollution; agriculture accounts for 34% of greenhouse gas emissions.
- Key environmental problems: deforestation and clearing of rainforests in Brazil; falling groundwater levels in India; heavy-metal contamination of soils in China; nitrogen surplus in the Netherlands (too many animals per capita – the government is introducing livestock limits); drying out of the south-western United States.

## 6. What should we eat to stay healthy?

- Food should supply carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.
- In most developed countries, over 3,000 calories per person per day are available.
- Iron and iodine deficiencies are widespread globally.
- Nigeria, Africa's most populous country, imports 15% of its food.

## 7. Feeding a growing population while reducing environmental consequences – doubtful solutions

- Projections foresee a population increase of about 2 billion between 2020 and 2050; 60% of this growth will occur in Africa.
- Europe's population is projected to decline; the USA's to grow by about 15%; Asia's by about 14%.
- Four unrealistic proposals:
  1. **Universal transition to organic farming** – would reduce yields and fail to meet even 40% of current food demand; requires much more land and double cropping (first leguminous plants to supply nitrogen, then the actual crop); legumes do not, however, supply phosphorus and potassium.
  2. **Large-scale adoption of permaculture and polyculture** – permaculture involves planting perennial crops once and harvesting them for many years, reducing soil exhaustion and labour; polyculture means growing several species simultaneously on one field; both methods are still at the experimental stage.
  3. **Genetically modified varieties of staple crops** – work is under way on plants that fix nitrogen on their own and on crops with more efficient photosynthesis; these are still experimental, and implementation would face serious obstacles.
  4. **Mass production of animal-based food in vitro** – meat grown in reactors requires strictly controlled conditions, making production extremely costly; meeting just 1% of global meat demand would require building approximately 300 facilities.

## 8. Feeding a growing population – what could actually work

- **Legal regulation** – e.g. Dutch rules limiting nitrogen load per unit area; the aim is for the state to enforce efficiency.
- Multiple or multi-species cropping wherever conditions allow.
- Gradual shift from beef to poultry.
- Crop rotation – alternating different species year after year instead of growing maize after maize.
- Modern technology: GPS for planning optimal machinery routes, lasers for levelling field surfaces, sensors for monitoring soil nutrient content.
- Two most important actions:
  1. **Reducing food waste** – in the EU and USA about 100 kg of food per person is wasted annually; cutting losses can deliver enormous benefits.
  2. **Eating meat in moderation** – reducing overall meat consumption and switching from beef to poultry.
- The global population is projected to peak at 9.7 billion around the mid-2060s, then decline to about 8.8 billion by 2100.
