# Content

## Introduction. The little ice age and the general crisis

- In 1618 an uprising broke out in Bohemia, drawing most European countries into conflict.
- That same year in Turkey the so-called Ottoman tragedy began, triggered by the removal of the sultan from power.
- In Asia the Manchus invaded China.

## 1. The bed of the crisis

- Four key factors for people: climate, government, religion, and population size.

### The little ice age

- Weather anomalies occurred.
- Witches were sought as scapegoats.
- Fewer sunspots were observed.
- Volcanic eruptions further lowered temperatures.
- El Niño (monsoon) emerged; it may possibly cause volcanic eruptions.
- The numbers of locusts and rodents increased.
- Price increases were not linear but exponential, as a fixed portion of the harvest had to be set aside for sowing.
- A person needs approximately 2,000 calories a day.
- The world in 1618 was overpopulated.
- The transition in China from the Ming to the Qing dynasty claimed several million lives.

## 2. The general crisis

- In the seventeenth century there were only a few years without war.
- Ships and armies were very expensive; money for the army mattered more than courage on the battlefield.
- Rulers were unconstrained and used religion for their own ends.
- They craved glory and fame; wars also broke out over succession disputes.
- In Asia tanistry was practised – a successor had to prove his worth, which was extremely destructive.
- Kings surrounded themselves with favourites.

## 3. Famine is the greatest enemy

- The areas most exposed to crisis were peripheral agricultural regions, cities, and macro-regions.
- Fires destroyed cities, and the air was foul-smelling.

## 4. Surviving the seventeenth century

- Population declined drastically – sometimes by as much as 50%.
- The most deadly diseases were smallpox and plague; pregnant women (2 in 100 infected) and children were especially at risk.
- Climate and war intensified epidemics.
- In wartime 75% of victims died from the hardships of war rather than in battle.
- Women began marrying at the age of 27–28, which limited the number of children.
- In Europe 40 women per 1,000 died in childbirth (today 0.12).
- One quarter of children died within the first year of life and one half before reaching adulthood.
- 80–90% of patrician daughters became nuns.

## II. Enduring the crisis

### V. The great enterprise in China

- China had two capitals: Beijing and Nanjing.
- The Ming dynasty fell; the country was run by an army of bureaucrats and eunuchs, with the eunuch Wei holding actual power.
- The Ming had trouble collecting taxes, fielded a weak army, and banned the activities of academies.
- The country was first attacked by bandits from the west, then by the Manchus from the north, who founded the Qing dynasty and possessed modern technology.
- Anyone could become an official in China.
- The Qing designated areas within cities where only Tatars could live and ordered the population to shave their heads in the Tatar fashion.
- A revolt of the three feudatories took place.

## VI. The great upheaval: Russia and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, 1618–1686

- In 1618 the tsar concluded a truce with Poland and was required to pay war reparations.
- Russia built the Belgorod Line and the Simbirsk Line to defend itself against Poland, Sweden, and the Tatars.
- A crowd forced the tsar to dismiss Morozov.
- The Zemsky Sobor: landowners became the owners of serfs, and the tsar gained internal peace.
- Poland took Ukraine from Lithuania and distributed vast lands to magnates.
- Khmelnytsky moved from the registered to the unregistered Cossacks; his uprising led to the massacre of Jews, who had previously managed the magnates' estates.
- Poland was successively invaded by Russia and Sweden.
- The Cossack rebel Razin's uprising broke out in Russia.
- Russia emerged from the conflicts as a great power, while Poland never recovered its former position after the Khmelnytsky uprising.

## VII. The Ottoman tragedy, 1618–1683

- Subjects were divided into those who "paid taxes" and those "of the army" (officials).
- The sultan collected boys from Christian countries, converted them to Islam, and made them soldiers or officials.
- Cavalrymen were Turks who held land; spiritual authority rested in the hands of a few families.
- Sultan Osman, who had made enemies of the clergy and lost to Poland, was killed.
- Sabbatai Zevi proclaimed himself the prophet of the Jews.
- Mehmed IV and his grand vizier Fazil Ahmed achieved great successes for Turkey.

## VIII. Devastated lands: Germany and its neighbours, 1618–1688

- Germany had 1,300 local rulers.
- At the top stood seven electors forming one college; two further colleges existed: the ecclesiastical-noble college and the urban college.
- The Defenestration of Prague: the Bohemians chose Ferdinand as king, while the Germans chose Frederick.
- The Thirty Years' War broke out.

## 9. The agony of the Iberian Peninsula, 1618–1689

- Philip IV ruled over Spain, Portugal, and the colonies – an empire on which the sun never set.
- Instead of withdrawing from some wars, the king raised taxes; his most important minister was Olivares.
- Portugal rebelled, and John IV became king.

## 10. France in crisis, 1618–1688

- The Fronde was the greatest rebellion in Europe.
- King Louis XIII lost control of the bureaucracy, as offices could be bought and sold.
- The country had two religions: Catholicism and Protestantism (10% of the population).
- Richelieu became prime minister and sought to crush the Protestants; tax revolts broke out.
- After the deaths of the king and the minister, Anne of Austria governed in the name of Louis XIV with the help of Cardinal Mazarin.
- New taxes imposed on Paris sparked a revolt: the arrest of the judge Broussel raised barricades in the streets – the rebels used slings (frondes).

## 11. The Stuart monarchy: towards civil war, 1603–1642

- James Stuart, King of Scotland, took the English throne after his cousin Elizabeth I Tudor.
- England had 4 million inhabitants, Ireland 2 million, Scotland 1 million; Catholics made up 5% but held disproportionate influence.
- James supported his son-in-law Frederick, Elector Palatine, in war.
- James's son Charles and the Duke of Buckingham persuaded the king to go to war with Spain during a period of bad harvests.
- Charles suffered defeats; Parliament accused him and passed the Petition of Right.
- England alone enjoyed peace and drew large revenues from trade; the courts were compliant with the king.
- The Act of Revocation in Scotland allowed the king to reclaim his lands; an attempt to impose a uniform religion through a prayer book caused conflict.
- The armies of England and Scotland faced each other; Charles was deceived and entered negotiations.
- The Short Parliament focused on the king's faults rather than on taxation; Scotland attacked England.
- The Long Parliament arrested and executed Strafford, the king's adviser.
- An uprising in Ireland, sparked by fears for Catholicism, and Charles's attempt to use force against Parliament (key opponents were warned and escaped) deepened the crisis.
- Charles was inconsistent and indecisive.

## XII. Britain and Ireland from civil war to revolution, 1642–1689

- Parliament constrained Charles I and demanded funds to defend Protestants in Ireland.
- Scotland supported Parliament in exchange for religious privileges; Charles lost the Battle of Marston Moor.
- Fairfax and Cromwell won a further victory and seized the king's personal letters.
- The king fell into Fairfax's hands; soldiers demanded their overdue pay.
- Charles allied himself with the Scots, sparking the second civil war; he was subsequently executed.
- Charles II raised an army and prevailed; in Ireland Protestants first held 40% and then 80% of the land.
- After Charles II died, his brother James succeeded him; James proclaimed religious liberty, which infringed Parliament's rights, and seven bishops refused to read out the declaration.
- Power was offered to James's son-in-law William of Orange; the Glorious Revolution took place and Parliament gained extensive powers.

## III. Overcoming the crisis

- Countries that did not reach the limits of food supply managed to avoid the crisis.

## 13. The Mughals and their neighbours

- The area was equivalent in size to half of Europe and had a population of 100 million, mainly in India.
- The country was divided into fiefdoms; rulers were constantly on the move and never at peace.
- The transfer of power was a serious problem.
- Monsoon failure normally occurred once every 100 years; in the seventeenth century it occurred four times.
- Shah Jahan was a prudent ruler: he supported soup kitchens, reduced taxes, and did not invade other territories; his only failure was against the Uzbeks in Afghanistan.
- Aurangzeb deposed his own father.
- Iran avoided serious demographic problems because women were able to practise contraception.

## 14. The red flag over Italy

- Spain imposed taxes on Italy to finance its wars.
- In Sicily taxes were levied on doors and windows, tobacco, and carriages.
- An uprising also broke out in Naples.

## 15. The Americas, Africa, and Australia

- The climate in America was healthier – people lived longer and in better health – but indigenous Americans died in great numbers from diseases brought by Europeans.
- The Pequot War was fought over maize.
- King Philip, chief of the Wampanoag, attacked New England (King Philip's War); he was defeated partly because the Iroquois sided with New England.
- In New France the Huron accepted tools from Europeans while the Iroquois took weapons.
- In Brazil the Dutch fought the Portuguese.
- Africa: 30,000 people per year were enslaved out of a population of 100 million; most transactions took place within 250 km of the coast.

## 16. On the right track: Japan under the first Tokugawa shoguns

- While the rest of the world was losing population in the seventeenth century, Japan was developing.
- Pre-industrial revolution: time and energy were invested rather than money (self-exploitation).
- Japanese women practised abortion; fields were divided into many plots of varying sizes, creating communities of mutually dependent farms.
- Earlier wars had depopulated the country; power was held by a single centre, the Tokugawa shogunate (not the emperor).
- Peasants could not become samurai and samurai could not become peasants; samurai moved to cities, so the villages were disarmed.
- The Portuguese were expelled; only the Dutch were allowed to remain.
- The response to famines was effective: relief, tax reductions, renunciation of luxury goods, and pursuit of speculators.
- Wars were avoided; extensive censorship was applied, particularly regarding Christianity.

## IV. Confronting the crisis

- Those who rebel are:
- 1. people deprived of their means of subsistence,
- 2. people who break the law to make their lives easier,
- 3. people who follow an authority figure,
- 4. people who lack self-control.

## 17. People deprived of their means of subsistence: parameters of social resistance

- Sources of resistance: failed harvests, the obligation to billet soldiers, rising taxes.
- Women played a large role in protests; it was often assumed that the law did not apply to them.
- People revolted when there was somewhere to flee.
- People revolted on market days, when a gathered crowd did not arouse suspicion.

## 18. Those who seek only change: aristocrats, thinkers, clergy and nameless wicked men

- The group of "powerful people" included: the aristocracy, the clergy and the educated bourgeoisie.
- Well-educated officials and clergymen took part in revolts.
- Rebellion was justified by appeals to a Golden Age.
- Forged documents – purported royal confirmations – were used.

## 19. Those of different opinions who will answer any call: the spread of revolution

- Revolts spread like plagues.
- The host had to be susceptible – composite monarchies were particularly vulnerable.
- Newspapers appeared and information spread very quickly.
- Revolutions often began with a small number of people.

## V. After the crisis

### V. After the crisis

- Around 1700 terrible winters still occurred, but they no longer triggered comparable social upheavals.

## 20. Escaping the crisis

- Mayerne was physician to Cromwell and Princess Elizabeth; he treated them psychologically.
- People sinned to forget their misfortunes and kept records of their good and bad deeds.
- Alcohol, opium, coffee, tea, chocolate and tobacco came into widespread use.
- Outside Europe, peace came through the defeat of enemies: in Japan (Tokugawa), in China (Qing victory over the Ming), in India (Aurangzeb's victory) and in America (the extermination of indigenous peoples).
- In Europe, compromises were reached; wars lasted a long time because no one trusted anyone.
- Structural changes included: a demographic boom, a revival of economic activity and a focus on prosperity.

## 21. The warfare state or the welfare state?

- Many towns and villages recovered quickly and populations grew.
- A second agrarian revolution took place – maize, cassava and tobacco spread widely.
- Quarantine was introduced, as were smallpox inoculations (in China, deliberate infection with a mild strain was practised).
- Public granaries were established and fire insurance was introduced.
- Heyden in Amsterdam invented a pump with a suction hose.
- Amsterdam introduced street lamps and neighbourhood watches.

## 22. The great divergence

- After the crisis, rulers invested in education, yet many universities collapsed for lack of useful courses and staff.
- After 1650, Lord Bacon's New Philosophy (experimental philosophy) flourished.
- In London, the Invisible College held informal gatherings of curious minds.
- Galileo and his telescope, William Harvey and the principles of blood circulation (refuting Aristotle), René Descartes in France.
- In China, Xie wrote the treatise "Five Times on Miscellany".
- Censorship intensified; scientific success depended on money and the flow of knowledge (e.g. through learned societies).
- In England, Hartlib served as a central node for the exchange of knowledge; from this grew the Royal Society in London with its weekly meetings.
- Oldenburg was the Royal Society's first secretary and possessed an extensive network of contacts; scholarly journals began to be published.
- In Poland, Jan Heweliusz – brewer and astronomer.
- Halley discovered that many observed comets were one and the same object.
- Science came to be accepted as the supreme method of knowledge – from the condemnation of Galileo to the knighting of Newton.

## Summary: anatomy of a crisis

- Three great wars: the Thirty Years' War (Western and Central Europe), the Thirteen Years' War (Eastern Europe and Russia) and the Ming–Qing transition (China).

## Epilogue: it's the climate, stupid

- The problem with disasters is passivity; people do not learn unless a disaster recurs with sufficient frequency.
- 2% of scientists of the American Association for the Advancement of Science deny that the Earth is warming (and reject the theory of evolution).
- "Merchants of doubt" are a key problem.
- Losses caused by disasters are growing by 6% per year.
- A human being needs 2,000 calories per day.
- For investments in disaster prevention, the ratio of benefits to costs is 4:1.
