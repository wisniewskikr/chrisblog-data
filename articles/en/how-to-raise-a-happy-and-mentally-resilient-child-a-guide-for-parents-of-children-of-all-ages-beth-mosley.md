# Content

## I. Children's mental health

### 1. What can go wrong?

- 70% increase in mental health problems among children
- A child's brain develops until the age of 24, making them particularly vulnerable during this period

## 2. Responsibilities

- Wellbeing covers seven areas:
- 1. Physical activity
- 2. Connections
- 3. Mindfulness
- 4. Learning
- 5. Giving
- 6. Sleep
- 7. Nutrition

## 3. Screen time – how much is too much?

- How screen time is used matters more than how much of it there is
- Active use (e.g. exchanging messages) is beneficial
- Passive use (e.g. scrolling Instagram) is harmful
- Online spaces are dominated by falsely positive content
- Dopamine is the reward hormone – oriented towards the future
- Serotonin, oxytocin and endorphins are engagement hormones – they act in the here and now
- Balance between them is important

## II. Difficult feelings

### 4. The connection between feelings and behaviour

- The foundation of mental health is regulating one's feelings
- Emotions signal that something is happening in our world and that action is needed
- They serve three functions: protection (quickly identifying threats), motivation (self-motivation and growth) and pleasure (a sense that life is worth living)
- Emotions cause physical changes (e.g. faster heartbeat) and psychological changes (thoughts and feelings)
- Emotions can sometimes interfere with building positive relationships
- The brain has two areas: the lower brain (emotions) and the upper brain (logic), connected by "stairs" that the parent must maintain
- The wise mind is the middle ground between them
- In early childhood, it is the parent who should use the upper brain to calm the child
- The stronger the lower brain's reaction, the weaker the upper brain's reaction

## 5. Connection before correction

- The window of tolerance is the balance between the upper and lower mind; imbalance takes two forms: hyperarousal (too many emotions) or hypoarousal (too few emotions)
- The solution is the principle of "connection before correction": first listen, then look for a solution
- Steps: notice → connect → acknowledge → correct
- Emotions can be regulated through the senses: sight, hearing, smell, touch and taste

## 6. Establishing what matters

- When a child is experiencing difficult emotions, it is not always right to immediately "take them away" and solve the problem

## III. Anxiety

### 7. Fear and the body

- Anxiety is beneficial because it warns us, but it can also be harmful
- One in three children struggles with anxiety
- Anxiety becomes a problem when it is long-lasting or interferes with daily functioning
- There are two automatic responses: the stress response and the relaxation response
- Today, stress is far more common than it was in prehistoric times
- Mastering breathing helps manage stress
- Grounding technique: 5 things you can see; 4 things you can feel by touch; 3 things you can hear; 2 things you can smell; 1 thing you can taste
- Little and often is more effective than rare, intensive sessions

## 8. What to do with worry

- Worry is not bad – it redirects attention to problems
- Worry activates anxiety
- We have approximately 6.5 thoughts per minute
- Evolutionarily, we are more inclined to look for negatives than positives
- The pattern: thoughts → feelings → behaviours
- A self-fulfilling prophecy works like a placebo
- A useful tool: the worry tree
- Instead of asking "How was your day?", it is worth asking "What are you grateful for?"

## 9. Acts of courage

- Anxiety-feeding behaviours (helpful in the short term): avoidance, escape, compensation (e.g. washing hands more often), safety behaviours (e.g. looking at the phone instead of people), seeking reassurance
- The most effective way to overcome fear is to engage with the situation in a safe environment – it turns out to be positive or neutral
- Gradual exposure is helpful
- The goal is to learn to live with anxiety rather than under its command

## IV. Low mood

### 10. The traps of low mood

- Low mood and sadness are natural parts of life – they signal that something is wrong
- They can be triggered externally (loss) or internally (thoughts)
- Areas at risk: relationships, safety, engagement with meaningful activities, the ability to experience pleasure
- There is an energy-saving trap
- Depression is defined by the number and type of symptoms, their duration and their impact on functioning
- Protective factors: diet, physical exercise, meaningful activity, exposure to sunlight, social connections, sleep
- Low mood is greatest in teenagers due to hormonal and social changes and brain remodelling
- Recovery from low mood is supported by: rewards, relationships and reducing rumination

### 11. Doing what matters

- Rewards balance out challenges
- Activities change mood
- Activities can be divided into those that provide a sense of achievement, closeness or joy

## 12. Self-harm

- 17% of teenagers self-harm
- Self-harm stems from difficulties a person cannot cope with; it is a quick way to release bad feelings and a form of soothing
- It can be caused by an excess of feelings or a deficit of feelings

### III. Help

### 13. Building a strong relationship

- As mental health deteriorates, so does the ability to ask for help
- Mood affects relationships, and relationships affect mood
- Tensions arise at the intersection of three needs: achieving what we want; maintaining relationships; preserving self-respect
- Not only what you say matters, but also how you say it
- GIVE skills: Gentle, Interested, Validate, Easy manner
- Six principles of communication: strike while the iron is cold (choose the right moment); use "I" statements (speak about your own feelings, not about the other person); be specific (avoid the words "never" and "always"); put yourself in the other person's shoes; suggest different solutions; don't give up

## 14. Adolescence – a time of risk and opportunity

- In Western societies, adolescence lasts from age 10 to 24
- Adolescence involves risk-taking, sensation-seeking and rebellion against authority
- The goal of adolescence is survival and developing one's potential
- Children shift from parental influence to peer influence
- The lower brain is almost fully developed straight away, while the upper brain develops until the age of 24 – hence the challenges with teenagers
- With young children, adults should act as managers; with teenagers, as coaches
- Teenagers have a strong awareness of status, so it is important to speak to them with respect
- It is worth asking teenagers what their plans are
