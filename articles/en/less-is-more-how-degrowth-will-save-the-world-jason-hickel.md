# Content

## Introduction. Welcome to the Anthropocene

- The number of insects and earthworms is declining; marine animals are disappearing twice as fast as land animals.
- What distinguishes capitalism from other systems is the necessity of continuous growth – about 2–3% per year – which creates an exponential curve linked to resource consumption.
- Fossil fuels must be curtailed and renewable energy prioritised, but this cannot be achieved under continuous growth.
- Instead of GDP, the focus should be on human well-being and ecological balance; the distribution of income and resources matters more than growth itself.
- The fundamental problem is a dualistic belief that humans are superior to other creatures and may exploit them; for most of history, people practised animism – a sense of kinship with other creatures.
- The goal is not limitation but mutual connection.

## 1. Capitalism – a creation story

### I. More means worse

- Capitalism is based on taking more – from nature and human labour – than it gives back; imbalance and accumulation are built into the system.
- We are taught that self-interest and the maximisation of personal gain are part of human nature.
- History of capitalism: lords ruled over peasants; the Black Death disrupted this order and gave peasants the upper hand; 1350–1500 was the golden age of the European proletariat.
- Land then became private property, accumulation followed, and people were forced to work cheaply in factories to survive.
- Colonisation proceeded simultaneously – wealth from colonialism financed capitalism, supported by the Church, the nobility, and capitalists.
- Meeting needs was replaced by intensifying efficiency and maximising production, justified as "improvement" and today called "development".
- Lauderdale's Paradox: private wealth grows while public wealth declines.
- Bacon viewed the earth as a harlot to be tamed by technology; Descartes and Kant later reinforced this view.

## 2. The birth of Moloch

- Normally people trade to achieve use value; capitalism requires exchange value and profit, which must constantly increase.
- Corporations do not seek a stable state but continuous growth; capitalism operates like a virus – its goal is self-replication and infection.
- GDP was invented by Kuznets, who himself acknowledged that it does not account for costs.
- The productivity trap: continuous growth is needed to avoid collapse.
- The debt trap: states finance their needs through bonds, which also require continuous growth.
- During the debt crisis of the 1980s, Western states imposed "structural adjustment programmes" on poorer countries.
- Economies with higher GDP leave a larger environmental footprint; the ecological footprint should be 8 tonnes per person, while developed countries leave 28 tonnes per person.
- Historically, countries of the Global North are responsible for 92% of environmental pollution.
- Growth is perceived positively, but in nature any endless growth is destructive.
- Nine planetary processes must be stabilised: climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, changes in land use, over-fertilisation with phosphorus and nitrogen, depletion of freshwater resources, atmospheric aerosol loading, chemical pollution, ozone layer depletion.

## 3. Will technology save us?

- Green growth theory holds that efficiency improvements and recycling technologies will make growth environmentally friendly.
- Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS): trees are planted, felled, burned in power plants, and the CO₂ is stored – however, its feasibility has not been proven, it requires many capture installations and vast areas of forested land.
- Clean energy is genuinely clean, but the infrastructure needed to produce it is not.
- Solar radiation management (injecting a reflective aerosol into the atmosphere) is very risky.
- Jevons Paradox: efficiency gains produce savings that are reinvested in new technologies, so resource consumption ultimately increases.
- Recycling has limits: about 44% of resources (e.g. food, energy) cannot be reused.

## 4. The secrets of a good life

### II. Less means better

- What matters is not growth itself but how income is distributed and the extent to which it is invested in public services.
- Life expectancy improved not because of capitalism but because of public services: sewage systems, water supply, hospitals, and education.
- GDP contributes to human well-being only up to a certain level – beyond that, the relationship disappears; Portugal has a GDP 65% lower yet a longer life expectancy.
- Public health services are more effective than private ones: Spain spends $2,300 per person and achieves better outcomes than the USA, which spends $9,500.
- The UN identifies thresholds sufficient for a high quality of life: healthcare – $8,000 per person per year, education – $8,700, standard of living – $10,000.
- Beyond a certain level, GDP growth becomes actively harmful, as shown by the GPI (GDP adjusted for social and environmental costs).
- In the USA and the UK, happiness levels have declined despite incomes multiplying many times over.
- Proposed solutions: a global minimum wage, rules against tax avoidance, greater influence for Global South countries in the IMF, debt cancellation, and a requirement for large corporations to allocate 5% of income to purchasing bonds.
- Progress need not be tied to growth – what matters is the use of resources; this was demonstrated by the Second World War.

## 5. Towards a post-capitalist world

- Degrowth is not about limiting GDP but about reducing material and energy consumption.
- Proposed steps:
  - End planned obsolescence: mandatory product life extension, right to repair, leasing model.
  - Curb advertising: limits, bans on psychological manipulation, freeing public spaces from advertising.
  - Shift from ownership to use: shared tools, public transport, bicycles.
  - End food waste (30–50% of food is wasted): dedicated containers and high fees.
  - Restrict ecologically harmful industries: fossil fuels, beef.
- Reducing material and energy consumption will reduce demand for labour; the solution is a shorter statutory working week and a job guarantee.
- Narrow the wage gap between executives and workers to a ratio of 10:1.
- An annual tax of 10% on wealth above one billion dollars.
- De-commodification of basic goods and expansion of the commons.
- Capitalism generates scarcity, not abundance – this keeps the mechanism running; the antidote to growth is abundance.
- Debt cancellation; changing the interest system to dollar-for-dollar accounting or linear rather than exponential interest.
- Steady state: never extract more from the environment than can be replenished, and never produce more waste than ecosystems can safely absorb.
- Democracies are not fully democratic: unlimited political advertising donations allow the wealthy to pressure politicians.
- In the World Bank and the IMF, rich countries hold an enormous advantage over poor ones; in the UK, 3 companies control 70% of the press.
- Democracy and capitalism are in conflict with each other.

## 6. Everything is connected

- The story of the Salgado couple and their ecosystem restoration project as an example of nature's regeneration.
- A need to reverse the narrative: not separation from nature but reconnection with it.
- Baruch Spinoza: the philosopher who opposed Descartes.
