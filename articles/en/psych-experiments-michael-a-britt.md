# Content

## 1. Introduction to psychological experiments

### Classical conditioning

- Involves linking one stimulus with another.
- Experiment: a participant is splashed with water whenever a key word is spoken and flinches; eventually they flinch at the word alone, without the water.
- In life: we associate the sound of a dentist's drill with pain.

## Anchoring

- A number seen before evaluating a product influences willingness to pay – a high number raises the estimated price, a low number lowers it.
- Experiment: participants draw cards showing a number (0–20 or 80–100) and then estimate the price of shown products.
- In life: a salesperson first shows an expensive product (e.g. a car or an online offer), making subsequent options seem cheaper.

## Perception

- Our perception of distance depends on the state of our body – with a heavy backpack a distance seems greater, just as looking down from a tall building feels different from looking up.
- Experiment: ask friends to estimate a distance in a car park – once with a backpack, once without.
- In life: the mind is not independent of the body; physical state influences perception.

## Mnemonic techniques

- Short-term memory holds about 7 items at most; we best remember the first (primacy effect) and last (recency effect) items.
- Finding a pattern in the material allows this limit to be overcome.
- Experiment: ask participants to memorise the digit sequence 248163264128; tell one group that there is a pattern hidden in it.
- In life: recognising a pattern extends and improves retention.

## Eyewitness testimony

- The choice of words in a question influences a witness's description of an event.
- Experiment: after showing a crash video, ask one group how fast the car was going when it "smashed into" the other, and another group how fast it was going when the "collision" occurred.
- In life: physical evidence is more reliable than human memory.

## Encoding and forgetting

- The doorway effect: passing through a doorway into a new room prompts the mind to clear working memory.
- Experiment: participants pack items into a backpack, walk to another room (once through a door, once without), and then say what is in the backpack – answers after passing through a door take longer or contain errors.
- In life: it helps to repeat your purpose aloud before entering a new room.

## Memory – the method of loci

- The method of loci (from Latin/Greek for "places") involves associating items to be remembered with specific locations in one's home.
- Experiment: one group memorises a list of items using loci; another group uses no method.
- In life: information linked to a place has something to "hook onto" and is retained longer.

## Motivation

- Setting a concrete goal is one of the most effective motivational techniques.
- Experiment: two groups solve anagrams; one group is given a goal, the other is not.
- In life: goal-setting is an effective motivational strategy.

## Functional fixedness

- Creativity means applying unconventional solutions to existing objects.
- Experiment: the task of attaching a candle to a wall using only the available materials (including a box).
- In life: creativity is becoming an increasingly important skill.

## Mental set

- Prior experiences create a mental set that makes it harder to view a problem from a different angle.
- Experiment: one group views 10 photos of young women and then an ambiguous illusion; another group views 10 photos of older women and then the same illusion.
- In life: a walk or sleep relaxes the mind and helps approach a problem with fresh eyes.

## Emotion identification

- Facial expressions of emotion are universal across cultures.
- Experiment: one group views photos of happy faces with one angry face; another views angry faces with one happy face – the first group responds faster.
- In life: we identify angry faces more quickly because they may signal a threat.

## Cognitive therapy

- Thoughts we tell ourselves can be untrue and harmful; gaining distance from them is helpful.
- Two ways to deal with negative thoughts: restructuring (carefully reconsidering and rephrasing them) and distancing (realising their absurdity, e.g. by saying them in a funny voice).
- Experiment: one group neutralises negative sentences through restructuring, another through distancing – the distancing method yields better results.
- In life: it is useful to know several ways of coping with unpleasant thoughts.

## Labelling and mental illness

- A mental illness label affects how a person is treated by others.
- Reference to the experiment in which healthy researchers feigned mental illness, were admitted to a psychiatric facility, and behaved normally – staff never noticed the mistake.
- Experiment: one group receives a description of a candidate as someone "with a mental illness", another as "mentally ill" – the second candidate is rated more negatively.
- In life: labels should be used with caution.

## Ergonomics/natural mapping

- Some designs are intuitive to use, others are not.
- Experiment: one group uses a well-designed cooker, another uses a poorly designed one – the first group performs better.
- In life: good design is critically important, for example in an aircraft cockpit.

## Interpersonal attractiveness

- Physical attractiveness is perceived similarly across cultures and stems largely from bodily proportions.
- Attractive people are seen as smarter, funnier, etc. (the halo effect).
- Experiment: participants rate photos of attractive and unattractive people on traits such as kindness and intelligence.
- In life: it is worth being aware of the halo effect and trying not to be guided by a person's appearance.

## Social roles

- People adapt their behaviour to the role assigned to them (cf. the Stanford prison experiment).
- Experiment: a group of four people is asked to list uses for a brick; roles are secretly assigned (leader, timekeeper, devil's advocate, secretary) – participants become deeply immersed in them.
- In life: a person's behaviour often stems from their role rather than their character.

## Cognitive psychology

- Learned helplessness: after a series of failures, a person stops trying even when the chance of success is high.
- Experiment: two groups solve anagrams – one in the order easy–easy–medium, the other hard–hard–medium; those in the second group fail to solve even the medium anagram.
- In life: learned helplessness prevents attempts even under favourable conditions.

## Deindividuation

- Anonymity causes people to behave in ways they would normally avoid.
- Experiment: anonymous and non-anonymous participants comment on three foolish posts – anonymous participants are likely to be more malicious.
- In life: many online platforms do not allow anonymous posts for this reason.

## Mate selection strategies

- A man owning a dog may signal that he would be a good partner and father.
- Experiment: participants rate four photos (a family-man type without and with a dog, a womaniser without and with a dog) – the dog mainly affects the perception of the womaniser.
- In life: when choosing a partner, we consider, among other things, their ability to care for a child.

## Conformity

- When the majority holds a certain opinion, it is difficult to express a different view.
- Experiment: five people (four confederates) state the price of worthless items – the fifth person is likely to give a similar figure.
- In life: it is hard to go against the crowd, but once one person does, it becomes easier for others.

## Happiness/emotions

- Experiential purchases (spending on experiences) provide more satisfaction than material purchases (spending on things).
- Experiment: two groups rate their satisfaction with a recent material and experiential purchase – satisfaction with the experience is typically higher.
- In life: it is worthwhile to spend money on experiences.

## Persuasion

- Satisfied customers leave larger tips; a good mood can be created with a fun riddle.
- Experiment: restaurant simulation – one group of guests receives a fun riddle, the other does not; tips from the first group are higher.
- In life: putting customers in a good mood increases their generosity.

## Supernormal stimuli

- People feel greater attraction to unusual objects – larger or more vivid than ordinary ones.
- Experiment: donations collected for an ordinary cat and a cat with enlarged eyes – the second receives more.
- In life: marketers frequently exploit this mechanism.

## Cognitive dissonance

- We dislike being inconsistent; supporting something and then opposing it causes discomfort.
- Experiment: a participant reviews descriptions of products they recently bought – they look for flaws in negative reviews and endorse positive ones.
- In life: honesty with oneself requires courage and humility to admit a mistake.

## Personality tests/projective tests

- When interpreting ambiguous images, we project elements of our own personality onto them.
- Experiment: participants describe an inkblot; the response is attributed to a fictitious name.
- In life: projective tests are used together with other diagnostic tools.

## Inattentional blindness

- Strong focus on one task causes other stimuli to be ignored.
- Experiment: driving simulator – conversation with a passenger sitting next to the driver vs. a phone call; more errors occur during the phone call.
- In life: talking on the phone while driving is dangerous.

## False memories

- Memories are a mixture of what we actually saw and what we thought we saw.
- Experiment: a participant listens to a list of words and then answers whether a given word was on it.
- In life: one should approach one's own memories critically.

## Change blindness

- People fail to notice a surprisingly large number of changes in their environment – e.g. the substitution of their conversational partner mid-conversation.
- Experiment: the person asking for directions is swapped out during the interaction.
- In life: magicians regularly exploit this mechanism.

## Levels of processing

- To retain information longer, it must be processed deeply – linked to something or reflected upon.
- Experiment: one group counts the letters in words, another uses each word in a sentence – the second group remembers more words.
- In life: deep processing significantly improves memory durability.

## Psychological attachment and embodied cognition

- Warmth plays an important role in everyday human life.
- Experiment: whether a participant holds a warm or cold cup of coffee influences how they describe another person.

## Attractiveness

- The colour red may increase attractiveness.
- Experiment: one group sees a photo of a woman in a red blouse, the other sees her in a blue blouse; the woman in red will likely receive higher ratings.

## Emotions

- Our actions influence our mood.
- Experiment: both groups watch a funny video, but one group holds a pencil in their mouth; that group will likely find the video more amusing.
- The body influences the mind.

## Intelligence/influence

- Writing in complex language makes the author appear less intelligent.
- Experiment: three groups evaluate a cover letter written in easy, medium, or difficult language; the easy version will likely be rated best.
- It is worth trying to write in plain language.

## Persuasion/influence

- The presence of a brain scan image in a scientific article can increase its perceived credibility.
- Experiment: one group reads an article without a brain scan, the other reads the same article with a brain scan illustration; the latter will likely be considered more credible.
- One should evaluate the credibility of articles with a cool head.

## Neuroscience of motivation/persuasion

- Curiosity is a very powerful emotion — it stimulates memory and the reward system.
- Experiment: participants choose between 5 articles with intriguing headlines and 5 with dull ones; they will likely prefer to click on the intriguing ones.
- One should not click on links simply because they sound interesting.

## Behaviourism/operant conditioning

- We behave in certain ways because those behaviours were reinforced in the past.
- Experiment: a participant searches for a hidden object while the experimenter guides them with the words "warm" or "cold".
- External factors influence our behaviour.

## Creativity

- Sometimes we are most creative not when we have complete freedom, but when we face constraints.
- Experiment: two groups create rhymes — one without constraints, the other with constraints; the constrained group will likely show greater creativity.
- Every person is creative.

## Superstitions/critical thinking

- Confirmation bias: when we win, we credit the lucky charm; when we lose, we do not blame it.
- A self-fulfilling prophecy can boost confidence.
- Experiment: one group receives a "lucky ball" before putting it in a hole; they likely perform better.
- Luck has a lot to do with self-confidence.

## Prejudice and discrimination

- Social identity is the identification with a particular group.
- Experiment: in one part of a Memory game participants are divided into groups, in another part they are not; in the first part cheering and name-calling will likely appear, in the second — mutual support.
- Prejudice and discrimination are very easy to create; to overcome them, groups need a common goal.

## Decision-making

- The framing effect: the way information is presented influences the decisions made.
- Experiment: participants choose between options described by different numbers of infected people.
- It is worth thinking carefully about decisions before making them.

## Morality

- At a certain age we develop our own moral standards.
- Experiment: a story about a pharmacist profiting from a medicine is presented to children (who fear punishment), teenagers (who ask what others will do), and adults (who ask what should be done).
- It is worth reflecting carefully on moral issues, for example when serving on a jury.

## Cognitive development, energy conservation

- Stages of children's cognitive development according to Piaget:
  - Sensorimotor (0–2 years): the world is what can be touched.
  - Pre-operational (3–6 years): magical thinking.
  - Concrete operational (7–11 years): basic mathematical problems.
  - Formal operational (teenagers): abstract mathematical problems.
- Experiment: equal amounts of water are poured into two identical glasses, then one is poured into a taller glass and children are asked about the quantity — they will likely say the amount has changed.
- What children can do at each stage of development matters, but so does the context and order of questions.

## Persuasion

- Foot-in-the-door technique: agreeing to a small favour increases the likelihood of later agreeing to a larger one.
- Door-in-the-face technique: a large request is made first in order to then obtain a smaller one.
- Experiment: in a virtual environment participants are first asked for a large favour, then a small one.
- It is worth learning to recognise these techniques.

## Humour

- Some jokes are funny and others are not — this depends partly on feelings of superiority and fear.
- Experiment: two groups rate jokes aimed at younger audiences and scary jokes.
- Jokes are based on mechanisms of superiority and fear.

## Lie detection

- Experiment: a participant draws a scene while either telling the truth or lying depending on a cue word; people who are lying draw less detail and from a bird's-eye perspective.
- This method can be used to try to assess whether someone is telling the truth.

## Embodied cognition

- Can the lightness or heaviness of an object influence the mind?
- Experiment: participants answer questions while holding a light or heavy clipboard.
- These experiments have not been successfully replicated.

## Two-factor theory of job satisfaction and motivation

- "Hygiene factors" (external): salary, relationships, etc.
- "Motivation factors" (internal): achievements, recognition, etc.
- Experiment: participants describe situations when they were satisfied with their work and when they were dissatisfied.
- Knowledge of these factors helps in designing engaging work.

## Persuasion

- It is possible to build resistance to advertisers' practices.
- Experiment: one group is "vaccinated" in advance with knowledge about persuasion techniques, the other is not; the first group will likely be more sceptical.
- It is worth knowing how to defend oneself against persuasion techniques.

## Optical illusions

- Optical illusions show that perception is a combination of what we actually see and what we "should" see.
- Experiment: participants are asked to mark the midpoint of a Müller-Lyer line, which proves difficult.

## Engagement/happiness

- Does using a phone and taking photos reduce the enjoyment of experiences?
- Experiment: one group builds a model of the Eiffel Tower without taking photos, the other with photos; the engagement levels of both groups are similar.
- It is not certain that taking photos diminishes our enjoyment.
