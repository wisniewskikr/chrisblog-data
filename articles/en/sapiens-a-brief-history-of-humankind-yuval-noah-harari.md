# Content

## I. The Cognitive Revolution

### 1. An Insignificant Animal

- Timeline: the Big Bang 14 billion years ago, first atoms 300,000 years later, biology from 4 billion years ago, history (cultures, the Agricultural Revolution, the Scientific Revolution) from 70,000 years ago.
- Species: individuals that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. A horse and a donkey are different species (producing a mule); a spaniel and a bulldog are the same species.
- Genus: species descended from a common ancestor, e.g. lions and tigers. Families are shared by genera.
- Many Homo species lived simultaneously: Homo neanderthalensis (Europe and Asia), Homo soloensis (Java), Homo floresiensis (Flores, the "hobbits"), Homo erectus (Asia), Homo denisova (Siberia), Homo rudolfensis and Homo ergaster (Africa).
- Humans shifted energy from muscles to the brain: the brain (1–2% of body mass) consumes 20% of energy, at the cost of muscle mass.
- Humans are born underdeveloped – raising a child requires an entire tribe; this fosters social skills and great adaptability.
- Humans climbed from the middle of the ecosystem to its top, being completely unprepared for it.
- Fire enabled shorter intestines and thus larger brains; it also provided light, warmth, a weapon, and the ability to digest new foods (wheat, rice, potatoes).
- Homo sapiens appeared 150,000 years ago; 70,000 years ago they set out from Africa to Arabia.
- Two theories about encounters with other Homo species: the interbreeding theory (hybridisation) and the replacement theory (elimination).

## 2. The Tree of Knowledge

- Between 70,000 and 30,000 years ago Homo sapiens invented language, probably as a result of a chance mutation.
- Language initially served for gossip and facilitated cooperation in groups of up to about 150 people.
- Key ability: only humans can talk about fiction, enabling cooperation with strangers. Laws, regulations, and justice are all fiction.
- A great invention: legal persons – only the company's assets are at risk, not private ones.
- Thousands of chimpanzees gathered in a stadium would cause chaos; humans meet regularly in such numbers.

## 3. A Day in the Life of Adam and Eve

- Timeline of human occupations: hunting and gathering for tens of thousands of years, farming and herding for 10,000 years, industrial and office workers for 200 years.
- The tendency to overeat is an effect of life on the sparse savannah.
- Two competing theories about sexual life: the ancient commune (relations with multiple partners) and permanent monogamy.
- Ancient people owned only as many objects as they could carry on their backs.
- Thousands of entirely different cultures probably existed simultaneously.
- Foragers possessed broad general knowledge; modern humans are highly specialised.
- Foragers worked 3–6 hours a day and had fewer domestic burdens.
- If they survived childhood, they could live to 60–80; they ate a varied diet and were less exposed to epidemics (constant movement, no domestic animals).
- Animism: everything has a soul; theism: God exists and there is a hierarchy of beings.
- Very little is known about the lives of foragers – almost no traces remain.

## 4. The Flood

- Before the Cognitive Revolution, humans inhabited only Africa and Eurasia; they had not reached the Americas, New Zealand, or Madagascar.
- Homo sapiens caused massive animal extinctions: large land animals died out, aquatic animals did not; the same pattern repeated on each new landmass reached.
- Causes of extinction: large animals reproduce slowly, they had not developed a fear of humans, humans used burning; climate change was an additional factor.
- Humans crossed Siberia and Alaska, reached the Americas, and settled them by adapting to various climates.

## II. The Agricultural Revolution

### 5. History's Biggest Fraud

- Agriculture began to appear around 9500–8500 BCE in Turkey, Iran, and the Levant; it was complete by around 3500 BCE.
- Order of domestication: wheat and sheep, peas and lentils, olive trees, horses, vines.
- Agriculture arose independently in many parts of the world; not everywhere – some species are unsuitable for cultivation (e.g. truffles).
- The Agricultural Revolution brought: a demographic explosion, indulgent elites, less interesting and more work, and worse, monotonous food.
- Wheat – previously a wild grass – won because it can feed more people: around 13,000 BCE the oasis of Jericho could support 100 healthy and well-fed people; around 8500 BCE it supported a thousand sickly and underfed ones.
- Evolution's goal: copies of DNA; a company's goal: money – not individual well-being.
- Humans adopted agriculture gradually; each improvement worsened their lives, because more grain meant more children and the need for even more grain.
- Luxury becomes a necessity and creates new obligations.

## 6. Building Pyramids

- Foragers did not worry about the future; farmers worried constantly because they could influence it.
- History is the story of what a handful of people did thanks to the surplus, while the majority worked the land.
- There is no single universal truth – both the Code of Hammurabi and the Declaration of Independence are myths.
- Features of the imagined order: it is embedded in the material world, it shapes our desires, and it is intersubjective.
- Types of order: objective (e.g. radiation), subjective (e.g. a child's imaginary friend), intersubjective (e.g. law, money, gods, nations).

## 7. Memory Overload

- Foragers had no need for numbers – hence humans' poor natural mathematical intuition.
- Farmers needed to work with numbers.
- Around 3500–3000 BCE the Sumerians invented writing; it was incomplete – it could not express the full spoken language.
- Sumerian writing evolved into the fully developed cuneiform script.
- Writing alone was not enough – ways of cataloguing and retrieving data also had to be devised.

## 8. History Is Unfair

- Humans were able to create complex societies thanks to imagined orders and writing.
- From a biological point of view nothing is unnatural – it is culture that sets prohibitions.
- A distinction is made between biological sex and cultural gender.
- Theories of male dominance: greater physical strength, greater aggression, woman's dependence on a man during nine months of pregnancy.

## III. The Unification of Humankind

### 9. The Arrow of History

- Culture is a network of artificial instincts.
- Today we try to reconcile freedom and equality.
- The direction of history: simple cultures merge into increasingly complex civilisations; today there is essentially only one culture.
- Cultures integrate through money, empires, and religions.

## 10. The Scent of Money

- Hunter-gatherers were practically self-sufficient and had no need for money; the Agricultural Revolution changed little in this respect.
- Only the growth of cities brought a fundamental change.
- Money connects specialists and allows unwieldy things (land, goats) to be converted into portable ones (coins); it is a system of trust.
- History of money: the Sumerians – one sila of barley (approx. 1 litre); Mesopotamia – the silver shekel; Lydia and King Alyattes – the first coins.
- Money rests on universal convertibility and universal trust.

## 11. Imperial Visions

- Numantia defeated Rome in open battle; only after being surrounded by a wall did it surrender from starvation, and most of its inhabitants committed suicide.
- An empire rules over 20–30 cultures and has fluid borders and an insatiable territorial appetite.
- The Persian king Cyrus the Great was the first to proclaim that he ruled for the benefit of his peoples.

## 12. The Law of Religion

- Today religion is seen as a source of division; in the past it was a source of unity.
- Religion is a system of human norms and values based on a belief in a superhuman order.
- Universal, missionary religions emerged together with the Agricultural Revolution.
- Animism: humans are equal to other animals; polytheism: humans are above animals, the greatest force is not interested in human affairs – lesser deities are.
- Catholics and Protestants both believe in Christ; Catholics additionally believe in rituals and good deeds.
- The problem of monotheistic religions: the existence of evil. The problem of dualistic religions (good vs. evil): who established these principles.
- Religions of natural law: gods, if they exist, are also subject to the laws of nature; the goal is liberation from fleeing pain and pursuing happiness.
- Ideologies such as communism and socialism are also religions, merely called by a different name.

## 13. The Secret of Success

- We study history not to predict the future but to broaden our horizons and become aware of possible consequences.
- History does not confirm that people's lives are getting better.
- Memetics is based on memes: culture as a mind parasite (infection); it draws on game theory and the concept of an arms race.

## IV. The Scientific Revolution

### 14. The Discovery of Ignorance

- The Scientific Revolution began around 1500.
- Feedback loop: skills → resources → research.
- Features of modern knowledge: willingness to admit ignorance, primacy of observation and mathematics, acquisition of new skills.
- Ancient knowledge: everything was contained in sacred books, which held nothing irrelevant.
- Newton, in the "Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy", used mathematics for the first time to support a scientific theory.
- Scottish clergymen Webster and Wallace, together with the mathematician Maclaurin, statistically calculated an insurance scheme for the widows of pastors – the "Scottish Widows" fund was created.
- The goal of science is utility.

## 15. The marriage of science and empire

- Physician James Lind conducted a controlled experiment and discovered that citrus fruits help against scurvy; Captain Cook believed him.
- Europe gained a significant advantage because Europeans had been taught to think scientifically and were able to make use of inventions.
- Cosmographer Waldseemüller published the first map showing America; convinced that the discoverer was Amerigo Vespucci, he named the landmass America. Vespucci was the first to claim that America was a new continent.
- When Neil Armstrong was preparing in the desert for the Moon landing, he met an old Native American who asked him to pass on the words: "Do not trust these people. They came here to take your lands."
- Zheng He was a Chinese explorer.
- Cortés conquered the Aztecs and Pizarro conquered the Incas; both captured their rulers through deception and then formed a coalition against them.
- Rawlinson deciphered an ancient script after finding three translations in the Zagros Mountains.
- The Aryan tribe was the first to use a language from the European language group.
- Science and empires mutually supported each other.

## 16. The capitalist creed

- The ancient economy remained roughly the same size throughout; growth began with the modern economy.
- The modern economy invented a new kind of money: credit – building the present at the expense of the future.
- Credit was known earlier, but a lack of trust in the future and in growth meant that loans were small and highly interest-bearing.
- In the USA, banks can lend 10 dollars for every 1 dollar they physically hold.
- Adam Smith argued that the enrichment of the individual leads to the enrichment of society as a whole – but only when the profits from production are reinvested in the development of production.
- Free-market capitalism is blind to everything else, such as slavery.

## 17. The wheels of industry

- Historically the first machines were bodies – they converted food into movement.
- The next stage was gunpowder.
- Then came steam engines; on 15 September 1830 the first railway line connecting Liverpool and Manchester was opened.
- The next invention was the internal combustion engine.
- Then came electricity.
- During World War I, chemist Haber invented a way to synthesise ammonia, also used poison gases for the first time, and was awarded the Nobel Prize.

## 18. A permanent revolution

- Imagined communities: people think they know each other, even though they actually do not – churches are an example.
- Reasons for the declining likelihood of war:
  - War has become more expensive: nuclear weapons.
  - Lower gains from war: in the past one captured livestock and land, whereas now most value is virtual.
  - Peace has become more lucrative.
  - A cultural shift in the way war is perceived.

## 19. ... And they lived happily ever after

- Money brings happiness only up to a certain level.
- Family and community are of great importance to happiness.
- Happiness is the correlation between subjective expectations and objective conditions – it therefore depends on expectations rather than on health, wealth, or interpersonal relationships.
- The brain averages out the level of happiness, and people are genetically programmed for a certain level of it (5 or 7 on some scale).
- Another definition: happiness is a sense of meaning in life.
- Happiness may consist in aligning one's illusions with the illusions of the surrounding community.
- Buddhism: happiness depends neither on external nor on internal conditions – it depends on ceasing the pursuit and achieving peace.

## 20. The end of Homo sapiens

- Three possibilities for the intelligent redesign of humans:
  - Bioengineering.
  - Cyborgs.
  - Non-organic life: computer programs.
