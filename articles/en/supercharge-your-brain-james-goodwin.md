# Content

## Introduction

- In 1848, a rod pierced Phineas Gage's brain and changed his personality.
- In 2000, intelligence tests taken by almost all Scottish 11-year-olds in 1947 were rediscovered.
- Findings: only 50% of childhood IQ (at age 11) determines adult IQ.
- Only 1/4 of IQ is determined by genetics (DNA); the rest is environment.

## 1. Getting to know the brain

- Since the time of Aristotle and Hippocrates, it was believed that character depends on four humours: black bile, yellow bile, blood, and phlegm.
- In 1543 Vesalius drew the first atlas of human anatomy, based on dissections.
- In 1848 the accident of Phineas Gage took place.
- Brain development: 80% complete at age 2, 100% at age 25.
- The limbic system (the "emotional brain") consists of: the hippocampus, the hypothalamus (hormones, temperature), the amygdala (anger, fear, rage), and the thalamus (signals from inside and outside the body).
- The frontal lobes govern the limbic system and enable planning.
- Humans have achieved so much by working with each other and working on themselves.
- Three core brain functions: executive function, effective interaction with people, and emotional balance (understanding, regulating, and accepting feelings without letting them govern behaviour).
- The brain is influenced by genetics, epigenetics, and environment.
- Challenge your brain and step outside your comfort zone.

## 2. Body and mind

- The last ice age lasted approximately 2.6 million years; the last 10,000 years have brought warming and the development of agriculture.
- NHS physical activity guidelines:
  - Children under 5: physical activity, minimise time spent sitting.
  - Ages 5–18: 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity daily; bone- and muscle-strengthening exercise 3 times a week.
  - Ages 19–64: 150 minutes of moderate activity per week; strength exercises 2–3 times a week.
  - Over 64: movement every day; strength, balance, and flexibility exercises twice a week.
- Prolonged sitting negates the benefits of physical activity – it is therefore possible to have both an active and a sedentary lifestyle simultaneously.
- Combating sitting: avoid sitting, use standing desks, do not sit for more than one hour without a 10-minute break.
- The "ego depletion" principle: willpower is limited; linking a pleasurable reward to an activity helps in performing it regularly.

## 3. The Godzilla brain

- The brain accounts for 2% of body weight but consumes 20% of energy.
- This was made possible by the invention of cooking, which enabled larger brains and shorter intestines.
- Hunter-gatherer diet: hunting provided energy, gathering provided variety.
- The enteric nervous system is our second brain.
- Four basic taste sensations: salty (essential for the electrical functions of the nervous system), sweet (a measure of calories), bitter and sour (warning against toxins).
- Autotrophic organisms: plants (energy from light) and bacteria (energy from chemical compounds, e.g. sulphur) – they create organic particles based on carbon.
- Humans are heterotrophs; they obtain energy from carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.
- The intestines break large particles into small ones (hydrolysis) so that the blood can transport them to cells; enzymes act as catalysts.
- Cells burn molecules using oxygen.
- In 1822, a man named Martin was shot; his stomach wound never fully healed, allowing Dr Beaumont to study the stomach for 10 years.
- A calorie is the amount of energy needed to heat 1 kilogram of water by 1°C.
- Recommended daily calorie intake (ages 19–64): 2,500 kcal (men) and 2,000 kcal (women).
- Free radicals (oxidants): electrically charged molecules that cause oxidative stress.
- Reducing calorie intake by 20–30% is the most effective way to extend lifespan; in practice, 10% is sufficient.
- Overeating (more than 2,000 kcal above the norm) increases the risk of brain disease.
- Fasting causes the body to switch from glucose to ketones.

## 4. Bacteria and the brain

- Bacteria colonise humans from: the birth canal during delivery, breast milk and the nipple, and the surrounding environment.
- Couples who kiss 9 times a day align their bacterial flora.
- Gut bacteria communicate with the brain via: the immune system, hormones (e.g. serotonin), and nerve signals sent through the vagus nerve.
- Chewing gum improves memory.
- A pH below 7 is acidic (unfavourable); above 7 is alkaline.

## 5. Food for thought

- **Vitamin A** – good for memory; accumulates in the hippocampus. Recommended dose: 600 mcg (2,000 IU). Sources: baked sweet potato (960 mcg/100 g), spinach (573 mcg/115 g), carrots (459 mcg/150 g), broccoli (60 mcg/85 g).
- **Choline** – good for the brain. Recommended dose: 550 mg daily for men. Sources: wheat germ (202 mg/85 g), mushrooms (94 mg/85 g), broccoli (63 mg/85 g).
- **Vitamin B6** – anti-stress and antidepressant effects. Dose: 1.4 mg daily for men. Sources: chickpeas (1.1 mg/230 ml), potatoes (0.4 mg/230 g), banana (0.4 mg), white rice (0.1 mg/230 ml), raisins (0.1 mg/115 ml).
- **Vitamin B12** – essential for chemical reactions in the body; frequently deficient in vegans. Dose: 1.5 mcg daily. Sources: Marmite yeast extract (one teaspoon daily).
- **Vitamin C** – enhances cognitive function and protects the brain. Dose: 40 mg daily. Sources: rosehip, berries, peppers, kale, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, citrus fruits, pineapple, cabbage.
- **Vitamin D** – supports brain performance. Dose: 15 mcg daily. Sources: sunlight (below 35° latitude), animal products; vegans should supplement.
- **Vitamin E** – good for skin. Dose: 7–15 mg. Sources: wheat germ oil, vegetable oils, olive oil, nuts, avocado, spinach, broccoli.
- **Vitamin K** – supports cognitive performance. Dose: 65 mcg. Sources: spinach, kale, broccoli, lettuce, soya.
- **Magnesium** – good for memory. Dose: 270–300 mg daily. Sources: almonds, spinach, cashews, peanuts, soy milk, black beans, kidney beans, bananas, raisins, brown rice.
- **Zinc** – supports cell communication. Dose: 11 mg daily for men. Vegan sources: small amounts in beans, chickpeas, nuts, pumpkin seeds. Supplementation without medical advice is not recommended.

## 6. No man is an island

- Intuition is the ability to read what is happening in other people's minds; it is far more developed in humans than in apes.
- Loneliness is the absence of a sense of connection with others; social isolation is the absence of physical contact with other people.
- Principles for combating loneliness:
  - Self-perception: everyone feels lonely sometimes; we tend to perceive ourselves more negatively than others.
  - The surprising power of weak ties: every day try to make contact with someone or start a casual conversation.
  - Care for others.
  - The group matters.
  - Enrich your life.

## 7. The brain – a seat of sex

- Stages of sexual response: excitement (initial arousal), plateau (full arousal), orgasm (climax), resolution (calming after climax).
- What makes a man a good lover (according to surveyed women): 91% – patiently finding out what she enjoys; 89% – being attentive and aware of whether she is enjoying sex; 81% – asking what she likes most.
- Having sex at least once a week is beneficial for health.
- Sexual activity improves erections, and regular intercourse increases testosterone levels and sexual performance.
- Hierarchy of benefits: masturbation → intercourse → intercourse with intimacy; sex without a condom is rated as more beneficial.

## 8. Mind games

- Types of learning: associative (we are not built to learn in isolation), spatial (e.g. navigating a city), procedural (mastering a new skill).
- Children under 12 store languages in one area of the brain; adults store them in several.

## 7. To sleep or not to sleep?

- Why we sleep:
  - New synapses form more easily during sleep.
  - Old synapses are pruned more easily during sleep.
  - Sleep detoxifies the brain.
  - Sleep removes metabolic waste from the brain.

## 10. The importance of good mental well-being

- Ranking of the happiest countries in 2020: Finland, Denmark, Switzerland, Iceland, Norway.
- Happiness: a mental state in which we are content and satisfied with our situation.
- Happiness itself is one thing, but the factors and paths leading to it differ from person to person.
- Components of happiness: physical well-being, psychological well-being (negative emotions balanced by positive ones), potential and ambitions.
- Lack of money causes unhappiness; money raises happiness up to a certain threshold ($75,000), after which the effect levels off.
- If you have money yet feel unhappy, you are spending it poorly — you should spend it on others and on experiences rather than on material goods.
- A good marriage brings happiness; when it ceases to be good, it works in reverse and takes happiness away.
- Children have little impact on the sense of happiness; they can reduce it when they negatively affect professional life, but this can be mitigated by appropriate legal arrangements.
- Complete idleness does not bring happiness — one should live with some purpose.
- Distraction of attention reduces happiness.
- Two systems: the experiencing self (lasts about 3 seconds) and the remembering self (constructs the narrative).
- Emotions are automatic and innate physiological reactions with self-preservation value; they form the basis of feelings and exert a powerful influence on thoughts and actions.
- Types of emotions: hunger, thirst, desire, fear, anger, disgust, sadness, joy.
- Emotions influence perception, but perception also influences emotions (e.g. fear, hunger, desire).
- Emotions are located in the amygdala — the structure between the primitive and the higher brain; it produces hormones: noradrenaline (fear and anger), dopamine (joy), serotonin (mood stabilisation), and GABA (calming and soothing of mood).
- Different emotions can be located in different areas of the brain, so we can experience them simultaneously.
- Emotions travel to the frontal cortex, where feelings are formed; there, emotions, feelings, and logic together help us make decisions.
- Good well-being promotes better functioning of the conscious mind.
- Stress follows a normal curve: up to a certain point it is beneficial; beyond that threshold it is harmful.
- Activities supporting well-being: contact with greenery, yoga, laughter, spending time with animals, art.
- Moderate daily alcohol consumption may support the brain: 2 glasses of wine or 1 litre of beer.

## Afterword

  2. A regular circadian rhythm.
