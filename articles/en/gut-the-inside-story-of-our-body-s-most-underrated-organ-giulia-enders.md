# Content

## 1. The gut in the leading role

- The human body consists of 3 tubes: the circulatory system with the heart, the nervous system with the brain, and the digestive system.

## How do you poo – ... and why is it worth asking?

- We have two sphincters: the external one (controlled consciously) and the internal one (controlled subconsciously).
- The internal sphincter first sends a "trial balloon"; receptors in the space between the sphincters relay a question to the brain, which makes the decision.
- Squatting is the best position for defecation – the bowel opens fully; diverticular disease and haemorrhoids occur much less frequently.

## In the antechamber of the digestive system

- A person produces between 0.7 and 1 litre of saliva per day.
- The glands in the cheeks produce saliva when there is a reason; the glands under the tongue produce it continuously.
- Saliva is filtered blood: red blood cells are filtered out and substances such as calcium are added.
- Saliva contains a painkiller – opiorphin; it relieves throat pain during eating or chewing gum and has antidepressant properties.
- At night we produce no saliva, which promotes throat pain, bad breath, and bacterial growth.
- At the back of the tongue there are papillae that train immune cells and analyse food, drink, and air.
- The tonsils perform a similar function, but their crevices can harbour bacteria; if they are to be removed, it should be done after the age of 7, when the immune system is already trained.

## Structure of the digestive tract

- The oesophagus connects to the stomach not from above but from the right side – this prevents food from coming back up when we walk, laugh, or cough.
- Air collects in the upper part of the stomach and is released when we burp.
- The stomach is crescent-shaped: liquids run down the shorter side quickly into the small intestine, while food rests on the longer side.
- The small intestine is 3 to 6 metres long.
- Proteins react to digestive acids in the same way as to heat treatment – cooking is a kind of outsourcing of digestion.
- At the entrance to the small intestine there is a papilla that sprays active substances from the liver and pancreas onto the food.
- At every stage the small intestine sends the results of its work to the liver, where they are checked for toxins and then passed into the main bloodstream.
- After a meal, blood moves from the brain to the digestive system, causing drowsiness.
- The large intestine breaks down food remnants with the help of bacteria; the appendix in the caecum monitors this process; the energy obtained is sent to the liver and the main bloodstream.
- The large intestine surrounds the small intestine like a frame.

## What do we really eat?

- All living things are made of sugars (which can form chains called carbohydrates), amino acids (which can form chains called proteins), and fat.
- The body likes sugar because it saves the energy needed to break down carbohydrates; however, carbohydrates are healthier because energy is released more slowly.
- Energy reserves: glycogen is stored in the liver; excess is stored as fatty tissue.
- During exercise, glycogen is used first and fat only afterwards – which is why it is worth exercising for more than an hour.
- Lymphatic vessels accompany blood vessels and handle fat, which cannot go directly into the bloodstream (it would float like fat globules in broth).
- The small intestine passes fat to the thoracic duct, from where it reaches the heart and then the liver.
- Bad fat may eventually be neutralised in the liver, but it passes through the heart and other vessels first.
- Good cold-pressed olive oil (extra virgin) should be used, stored in the fridge in a tightly sealed bottle.
- Amino acids are used, among other things, for the production of DNA.
- Vegetarians should ensure they consume plants that contain all amino acids: soy, quinoa, amaranth, spirulina, buckwheat, and chia.

## Allergy, hypersensitivity, and intolerance

- Our ancestors ate up to 500 plant species a year; we eat only 17.

## A few words about poo

- Composition of faeces: 70% water, 10% microbes, 10% indigestible plant fibres, 10% other (medicines, dyes, cholesterol).

## 2. Innervation of the digestive tract

- The digestive tract is surrounded by three layers of smooth muscle that we cannot control consciously.

## How food transport works

- The path of food: eyes → nose → mouth → throat → oesophagus (5–10 seconds) → stomach (about 2 hours; sugars and carbohydrates release energy quickly, fats and proteins more slowly) → small intestine → large intestine.
- Stomach rumbling comes from the small intestine, not the stomach; it signals that there is time for clean-up between meals.
- The large intestine works slowly to give bacteria time to act; water is reclaimed.
- From ingestion to defecation takes on average one day; for faster people 8 hours, for slower people 3.5 days.

## Acid reflux

- Acid reflux is like stumbling – it results from poor nerve function (in this case, the nerves in the stomach).

## Vomiting

- Vomiting is precisely orchestrated: the brain decides, blood drains from the face to the abdomen (we turn pale), and saliva protects the teeth from acid.
- Causes of vomiting: poisoning, bacteria in the digestive tract, motion sickness, stress.

## Constipation

- Constipation occurs in the large intestine when the nerves cannot agree on whether to defecate or not.

## Brain and gut

- The sea squirt, after finding an ideal spot, digests its own brain – hence the theory that the only justification for having a brain is movement.
- The vagus nerve connects the gut to the brain.
- The gut influences mood from childhood, though it does so more discreetly with age.

## 3. The world of microbes

- 99% of bacteria reside in the intestines.
- Almost everything we can smell from living beings is caused by bacteria.
- We have 100 trillion bacteria, weighing about 2 kg.
- One gram of faeces contains more bacteria than there are people in the world.

## Bacteria and the immune system

- 80% of immune cells are located in the intestines.

## Development of gut flora

- In terms of cell count, we are 10% human and 90% bacteria; this is not visible because human cells are much larger.
- At birth we inherit our gut flora.
- Breast milk boosts the baby's immunity.
- After breastfeeding ends, bacteria begin to process simple carbohydrates, such as white rice.
- Gut flora reaches full maturity around the age of 3.
- Children born by caesarean section are deprived of bacteria; however, differences fade from the age of 7.

## Gut bacteria in adults

- From the bacteria's point of view, we reach adulthood at the age of 3.
- Bees are vegetarians; wasps are carnivores.
- The world is divided into: non-living and living (eukaryotes, archaea, bacteria).
- Count Dracula probably had problems with haem production: sensitivity to light, sensitivity to garlic, red urine.
- Bacteria support digestion, produce vitamins, and produce other beneficial substances.

## The significance of gut flora

- Location of bacteria: in humans at the bottom of the tract; in rodents also at the bottom, but they eat their own faeces with the bacteria; in cows bacteria are higher up and are digested when they die off.
- Bacteria can cause weight gain by: breaking down carbohydrates too efficiently (excess energy is retained), causing inflammation (the body stores fat), and influencing appetite by sending signals to the brain.
- Cholesterol destroys bile, which is needed to transport fat; when bile is lacking, fat is excreted.

## The villains – bad bacteria and parasites

- Plastic chopping boards should be used – bacteria have nowhere to hide.
- The bacterium Helicobacter pylori lives in the stomachs of half the world's population; it neutralises acids in its immediate surroundings or slips under the mucosa and tampers with our protective barriers.
- Helicobacter pylori also has beneficial effects, for example it helps with asthma.
- T. gondii are protozoa that cause the host organism to behave riskily – they want to reach a cat.

## On hygiene and good bacteria

- The goal of cleaning should be to reduce the number of bacteria, not to eliminate them entirely.
- Effective methods: dilution, drying (bacteria cannot reproduce), low and high temperatures, cleaning.
- Procalcitonin testing can determine whether the problem is caused by bacteria or viruses.
- The use of antibiotics should be limited.
- Probiotics act as: a massage salon, security services, and counselling and coaching for gut flora.
- Probiotics should be taken for at least 4 weeks.
- Prebiotics support good bacteria.
- Chicory sugar is not harmful.

## 4. The gut-brain axis

- Bacteria influence mood – to a limited extent and after several weeks.
- Certain bacterial strains reduce stress levels.

## 5. A craving for sour

- A sour taste means that good bacteria have been at work – hence people often crave sour foods.
- Fermentation is preliminary digestion by good bacteria; spoilage is preliminary digestion by bad bacteria.
