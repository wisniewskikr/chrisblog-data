# Content

## Prolog. The long shadow of nobility

- From the 15th to the 18th century, the Polish nobility took control of everything, disregarding other estates – the burghers and the peasantry.

## 1. Privilege

- The nobility and the knighthood overlapped.
- In Western Europe the nobility became differentiated; in Poland it became legally equal.
- Noblemen initially identified one another through simple battle cries, then began gathering into clans.
- A Polish clan was not a family but all the noblemen of a given area; hence Germany had thousands of clans while Poland had only a dozen or so.
- When armies grew too large for personal rallying calls, coats of arms came into use.
- Grouping both wealthy and poor neighbours into the same clans fostered the development of legal equality among the nobility.
- Casimir the Great died without heirs and passed power to his cousin Louis, who also had no son. To be able to pass the throne to his daughter, he had to grant the nobility privileges (the Privilege of Košice).
- Jadwiga was married to Jagiełło, who was raised to the throne by election, not by birth.
- After the deaths of Jadwiga and her daughter, Jagiełło remained king solely by the choice of the nobility and granted it further privileges.
- Wishing to secure the future of sons from later marriages, Jagiełło granted the nobility additional privileges.
- The nobility paid non-indexed taxes on peasant land but not on its own land.
- The nobility had guaranteed access to offices and dignities, as well as personal and property inviolability.
- The king could not call a levy en masse on his own, nor conduct a campaign without paying dearly for it.
- Nothing new could be proclaimed without the consent of the Sejm.
- The nobility constituted roughly 5% of the population (peasants c. 70%, burghers c. 20%, clergy c. 1%), though foreign observers estimated it higher, seeing its numerous presence at regional assemblies.
- Towns refused to participate in the Sejm, which ultimately turned against them.
- The nobility obtained the privilege of not paying customs duties, which severely undermined the competitiveness of towns.

## 2. The manor and the demesne

- After the discovery of America, grain prices rose at a dizzying pace: wheat by c. 500%, oats by c. 300%.
- A typical noble family consisted of four people.
- A nobleman earned around 9,000 złoty a year, paying neither tax nor salt excise.
- The ability to avoid work was the quintessence of freedom for a nobleman.
- Demesne farms were at least an hour's journey apart from one another.

## 3. A nation of lords

- The noble estate came first; only later did writers emerge from within it to justify its existence.
- What was supposed to distinguish the nobility from the rest of society was virtue.
- Racism became the official doctrine of nobility.
- The origins of the nobility were traced ever further back – to Adam and Eve, Noah, the Tower of Babel, and to the Sarmatians, a mythical ancient people.
- Noblemen considered themselves the only true Poles; the remaining c. 94% of inhabitants were not.
- New nobility could be granted by the king and the Sejm: nobilitacja meant granting nobility to a commoner, indygenat to a foreigner.
- Nobility could essentially not be lost; tied to birth, it could only be suspended by pursuing a career in a town, but it was recovered upon returning to the countryside.

## 4. Marks of virtue

- People falsely claimed noble status, especially in distant parts; informers received half of the exposed person's estate.
- Farmhands sometimes reported themselves in order to establish their nobility, paying off witnesses.
- The saying "straw is sticking out of your boots" actually referred to the nobility, not to peasants – this was a Polish peculiarity.
- Noblemen were overweight and died young.
- Villagers wore tangled hair, which they feared to cut lest it bring illness or death.
- Noblemen shaved the sides and back of the head, wearing a kind of mohawk, and also wore moustaches and sometimes beards.
- Poles eagerly followed new fashions in dress.
- Noblemen were educated at home and studied Latin; they attended universities but often did not sit examinations (e.g. Jan Kochanowski studied for 14 years and returned home with no degree).
- Noblemen ate and drank copiously, cultivating the custom of urging one another to drink.
- Only the poor buried their dead immediately; the wealthy delayed funerals by up to several months, feasting in the meantime.

## 5. Obedience

- Women were second-class citizens; marriage was contracted for property reasons.
- Widows enjoyed greater freedom.
- The divorce rate was only 1.4%.
- Noblewomen, unlike noblemen, were expected to work; women frequently served as estate managers.

## 6. Self-interest

- After the union with Lithuania, Poles constituted a majority in both the Sejm and the Senate.
- Royal elections were held – free elections in which every vote was equal.
- Pacta conventa were the king's obligations to his voters.
- About 40,000 eligible voters out of some 170,000 noblemen attended the elections.
- Attendance at regional assemblies was likewise low.

## 7. Lords and brothers

- It was possible to rise from the ranks of the ordinary nobility to the magnate class.
- Magnates constituted about 4% of the nobility.
- Poland became increasingly unable to defend itself against enemies.
- After the 17th century the position of the nobility was no longer so secure; the wealthy grew wealthier still.
- By the end of the period studied, 43% of the nobility belonged to the landless gentry.
- The law formally did not change, but noble democracy was replaced by magnate oligarchy.
- Poland had no aristocratic titles, but it had offices; the podkomorzy (sub-chamberlain) settled, among other things, border disputes.

## 8. Rabble-rousing

- The word warchoł originally meant a quarrel, brawl, or row; later it came to denote troublemakers and litigants.
- Mikołaj Rej was a typical quarrelsome litigant.
- Judges did not need legal training, so verdicts depended on personal whim.
- There were no prosecutors, no investigations, no police, and no prisons.
- Even murder required someone to file a lawsuit; court costs were borne by the parties themselves, creating opportunities for abuse.
- Law was enforced through "raids" – private expeditions.
- Townspeople were punished with corporal punishment, noblemen with fines; starostas maintained tower dungeons as prisons.
- Murder was extremely common among the nobility.

## 9. Golden freedom

- Proceedings of the noble Sejm were always poor in quality; as its competences grew, its effectiveness declined.
- The goal of the Sejm was to keep things as they were.
- The nobility resisted the king yet offered no executive power in return.
- Sejm proceedings had no formal procedures, only customs; sessions were not even presided over.

## 10. The levy en masse

- The nobility had many privileges but only one obligation – to defend the homeland.
- Professional armies emerged in other countries but not in Poland.
- Wici (the symbol of punishment for non-appearance) were sent out three times to allow time for preparation, yet mobilisation still took a month.
- The Commonwealth rarely gained advantage from wars: it had good commanders and excellent soldiers but a weak army.
- A sabre cuts on one side only, not two.

## 11. A state with pyres

- Noblemen were religiously tolerant, but mainly to avoid having the Church hold authority over them.

## 12. The long sleep

- Poland lagged behind Europe, yet the nobility did not see this and remained proud of its liberties.
