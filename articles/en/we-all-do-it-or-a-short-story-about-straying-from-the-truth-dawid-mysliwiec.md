# Content

## I. Zenek and castles in the sand

- False memories: not telling the truth does not necessarily mean lying – a person may have false memories, caused by others or by themselves; this is not necessarily a flaw.
- Reconsolidation: when we retrieve a memory, we effectively take it out of the "library", rewrite it and put it back – during rewriting it is easy to add or remove elements.
- The misinformation effect: the memory of an event is distorted by a misleading cue, e.g. using the word "accident" instead of "fender bender" causes people to judge the event as much more serious.
- The flashbulb effect: we place the greatest trust in memories accompanied by a strong emotional experience.
- The Mandela effect: a large group of people collectively remembers facts or details in a way that contradicts reality, e.g. many people were convinced that Mandela died in prison.
- Fuzzy trace theory: memory stores two records of an event – a verbatim trace (literal: words, tone) and a gist trace (meaning, context, emotions). Analogy: RAW file (all information) vs. JPG (general image). The verbatim trace fades quickly, leaving mainly the gist trace.
- DRM paradigm: when people are shown several words associated with a concept (e.g. sleep) without the concept word itself, they often claim it was present.
- Imagination inflation: the more often we imagine an event, the more real it seems to us.

## II. The truth shall set us free, the lie will shape us

- Deception also occurs in animals: as a hunting strategy (e.g. a spider's web laced with pheromones), a defense strategy (toxic coloring in harmless animals), or a reproductive strategy (male spiders give females a snack to avoid being eaten after mating).
- Deception pays off when it is rare, credible and hard to detect. Someone who always lies loses credibility; someone who mixes truth with falsehood is an effective manipulator.
- Organisms strive to occasionally deceive others while being deceived as rarely as possible themselves.
- White lies: prosocial lies serving politeness and the protection of others' feelings; people use them in about 20% of communication.
- Small lies can be polite and altruistic; big lies are usually selfish.
- Research shows that altruism matters more than truthfulness.
- Blue lie: a lie told for the good of society as a whole.
- Theory of mind: the ability to attribute knowledge states and intentions to other beings that differ from one's own – creating a separate "entity" in one's head.
- The Machiavellian hypothesis: it was life in complex societies, not tool use, that led to the development of such sophisticated cognitive abilities.

## 3. How do lie detectors lie?

- Most of our lies are white lies – about 9 in 10.
- The majority of lies in society are told by so-called prolific liars.
- The dark triad: psychopathy, Machiavellianism and narcissism.
- The false consensus effect: we overestimate how widely shared our own opinions, attitudes, values and behaviors are.
- Self-deception: we believe what we tell others, even when we are lying.
- Cognitive dissonance: a discrepancy between what we do and what we claim.
- To relieve cognitive dissonance, many people change their beliefs rather than their behavior.
- Confirmation bias: selectively searching for information that confirms we are right.
- Key question for critical thinking: what would an argument look like that would make me change my mind on issue X? If such an argument proves trustworthy – we change our minds.
- When watching for deception, one should look for changes in a person's behavior, since everyone may lie differently.
- Hindsight bias: after something has happened, we claim we expected it all along – even though we never said so beforehand.
- A polygraph detects lies in at most 70% of cases.

## IV. Who stole more?

- People are not guided purely by logic, e.g. we swallow eaten food without trouble, but spitting it onto a plate and eating it again feels difficult – even though there is no logical difference.
- Appeal to hypocrisy: rejecting someone's moral judgment because the person making it has acted similarly themselves (e.g. a murderer says one should not kill).
- Social proof: people follow the behavior of others, treating it as correct.
- Appeal to authority: we find it easier to accept the opinion of someone we consider an authority.
- Foot-in-the-door: first a small favor is requested, then demands are gradually increased; it works because we want to be consistent.
- Low-ball technique: first agreement is obtained for an action under attractive conditions, then costs are gradually increased and conditions changed.
- Gish gallop: presenting as many poor-quality arguments as possible to overwhelm the opponent.
- Illusory truth effect: something repeated many times very often comes to be accepted as true.
- Mere exposure effect: the more often we are exposed to a given stimulus, the more we trust and like it.
- Overton window: a much worse scenario is first presented, so that a milder one – which was the real goal all along – can eventually be generously agreed upon.
- Primacy effect: we attach greater weight to information that reaches us first.
- Belief regression: correcting false information once is not enough – people forget, so it must be repeated.
- When correcting false information it is better to say how things are rather than how they are not; do not repeat the falsehood, but immediately state the true version.
- The continued influence effect: even after a person learns that something is untrue, they very often do not change their view.
- Availability heuristic: we assess the probability of an event based on how quickly it comes to mind.
- Confirmation bias: we primarily remember information that confirms our thesis.
- Cherry picking: selecting only those pieces of evidence from a text that support one's thesis – like picking the nicest cherries from a basket. Example: citing the survival of only those who prayed fervently as proof of God's existence, while ignoring those who also prayed fervently but perished.
