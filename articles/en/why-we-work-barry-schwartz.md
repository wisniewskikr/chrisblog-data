# Content

## Introduction - the basic question

- Question: why do we work?
- Possible reasons: money, fun, a sense of control (autonomy and proving oneself), growth, contact with others, the feeling of doing something important
- According to Gallup, there are twice as many disengaged workers as engaged ones:
  - 13% engaged
  - 63% neutral
  - 24% hate their work

## 1. False premises

- The dominant premise: to motivate people to work, you have to make it worth their while – the "carrot and stick" principle
- Adam Smith and the "pin factory" – division of labour and wages as the foundation of the work system
- Frederick Taylor and "scientific management" – optimising efficient movements at work
- B.F. Skinner and experiments with rats and pigeons as the basis for thinking about motivation

### The self-fulfilment of false theories

- What people expect from work depends largely on what work offers them
- Human nature is largely created, not discovered – it is up to us how we design it
- This does not apply to other sciences: stars are indifferent to what we think about them

## 2. Good work

- Common beliefs: good work is only for a few; it is the price of living in prosperity

### Hospital cleaning

- Hospital cleaners performed tasks beyond their job description (e.g. re-cleaned a room, paused cleaning to avoid disturbing a patient) – they did so on their own initiative
- Three types of relationship with work:
  - **A job**: work solely for money; you would not recommend it to your children
  - **A career**: a higher level of engagement; the worker likes the work but is mainly concerned with advancement
  - **A calling**: you believe in what you do
- Some jobs are easier to love than others

### Carpet manufacturing

- Interface company and Ray Anderson – he decided to manufacture carpets without harming the environment
- Employees introduced improvements on their own initiative

### Hairdressing

- Hairdressers must be technically skilled but also able to build a relationship with the client; clients leave in a better mood
- Characteristics of a good company: safe and stable employment, decentralised structure, higher wages (employees feel valued), fewer bonuses, opportunities for learning, a clear company mission

### Turning a vicious circle into a virtuous circle

- When a company starts to lose money and cuts costs, people work worse – a vicious circle forms
- Promoting positivity translates into results
- In competitive markets both sides gain: seller and buyer

## 3. How good work turns bad

- Very little is needed to turn bad work into good work – and vice versa
- Good example: a General Motors plant taken over by Toyota – reorganising work cut costs by half
- Bad example: education with rigid lesson scripts – routinisation and excessive supervision damage work; bonuses make things even worse
- **The motivational crowding-out effect**: external motivation (bonuses) crowds out internal motivation (calling)
- Example: a nursery introduces a fine for late pick-up – instead of reducing lateness, the number of late pick-ups increased
- Example: nuclear waste storage in Switzerland – when payment for consent was proposed, the share of those agreeing dropped from 50% to 25%
- When money is offered, we shift from the social domain to the financial domain, which changes how we perceive the situation

## 4. The technology of ideas

### Human nature as a battle of metaphors

- Every science is a conversation between theory and empirical data
- Question: do the media respond to consumers' tastes or do they create them?
- Discoveries vs. inventions:
  - **Discoveries** find something that already exists – they have no moral dimension
  - **Inventions** apply discoveries in practice – they have a moral dimension and can be harmful
- We live in a world of ideas like fish in water; ideas once came from parents, wise people, and sacred texts – now they come from the social sciences

### The technology of ideas

- Ideas matter greatly for humans: when a squirrel is hungry it looks for food; when a human is hungry, they may look for food, pray, organise work, etc.
- The technology of ideas differs from the technology of objects in two ways:
  - It is invisible – it can influence our lives without our knowing it
  - It can be false, yet change our attitude so that the theory becomes true
- A person paid by the hour changes – they start thinking about work even outside working hours
- Mechanisms by which ideology fulfils itself:
  - We change how we think about our own behaviour (e.g. the belief that altruism is hidden self-interest)
  - Self-fulfilling prophecy (e.g. the belief that a certain group is unfit for trade unions)
  - Institutional structures adapt to ideology (e.g. a politician convinced that people are lazy)
- Caring for others is an important human trait, but it is threatened by a focus on self-interest
- We are what society expects of us – if it does not expect much, it will not get much

## 5. The future of work: designing human nature

- When we design social institutions (schools, workplaces), we also shape human nature
- Productivity should be measured not in material units but in wellbeing and satisfaction
- Key questions to ask:
  - **Why?** – what is the purpose of work?
  - **What?** – does the product bring benefits?
  - **How?** – does the worker have the opportunity to use their own initiative?
  - **When?**
