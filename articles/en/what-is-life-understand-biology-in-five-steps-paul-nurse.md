# Content

## 1. The cell

- The cell is the atom of biology – the basic form of life, whose imperative is survival and division.
- Cells vary in size: e.g. a bladder-infection parasite (3,000 in a line make 1 mm) or an egg yolk.

### History
- 1665 – Robert Hooke built a microscope and observed cells in cork; he named them after the Latin word *cella* (cell, small room).
- Leeuwenhoek discovered single-celled organisms.
- 1858 – Virchow discovered that every cell is an individual form of life.
- 1839 – Schleiden and Schwann formulated cell theory: all organisms are made of cells.
- Cell division was discovered.

### Cell structure
- Surrounded by a membrane.
- Contains organelles, also surrounded by membranes, e.g. the nucleus and mitochondria.
- The nucleus stores genetic information encoded in chromosomes.
- Mitochondria act as miniature power supplies.

### Types of organisms
- Eukaryotes: cells have nuclei (e.g. animals, plants, fungi).
- Prokaryotes: cells lack nuclei (e.g. bacteria and archaea).

## 2. The gene

- Genes provide instructions for building proteins, which do most of the work in the cell: constructing it and controlling it.

### History
- Gregor Mendel of Brno conducted experiments on garden peas.
- 1870 – Flemming observed threads (chromosomes) in cells that divide during cell division.
- Beneden observed chromosomes in a parasitic roundworm.
- 1944 – Avery discovered that DNA is responsible for heredity.
- 1953 – Crick and Watson discovered the structure of DNA.
- 1970s – Sanger developed a method for reading genetic information (DNA sequencing).

### Chromosomes and DNA
- The number of chromosomes depends on the organism: pea – 14, human – 46, butterfly – 400.
- Each chromosome contains DNA made up of hundreds or thousands of genes; a human has about 1,300 genes per chromosome, and the DNA stretches to about 8 cm when uncoiled.

### Requirements for DNA as a carrier of life
- Must contain all information necessary for functioning.
- Must replicate with high precision.

### DNA structure
- Four nitrogenous bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C).
- Base pairing: A–T and G–C.

### The genetic code
- Proteins are built from 20 amino acids.
- Three DNA letters encode one amino acid (e.g. GCT = alanine).
- Mutations: one in a billion DNA letters is copied incorrectly.
- A small number of new genetic variants arise at fertilisation.

## 3. Evolution by natural selection

- Natural selection is the cause of the diversity of species.

### History
- Aristotle: animals gain or lose certain body parts over many generations.
- Lamarck (turn of the 18th–19th century): different species are related; the giraffe has a long neck because generations have stretched it upward.
- 1859 – Darwin publishes *On the Origin of Species*.

### Selection
- Artificial (selective) breeding produces organisms with specific, desired traits.
- Conditions for natural selection: reproduction, a system of inheritance, and mutations within that system.

## 4. Life as a chemical process

- Life consists of chemical reactions catalysed by enzymes; taken together they constitute metabolism.
- Vitalism – the historical view that life is driven by a mysterious force flowing exclusively into living creatures.

### History
- Aristotle was among the first to consider the chemical dimension of life.
- 18th century – Lavoisier studied fermentation.
- Pasteur discovered that yeast is responsible for fermentation.

### Enzymes
- Enzymes are protein catalysts that speed up and facilitate chemical reactions, allowing them to occur at the low temperatures and mild conditions inside a cell.
- Proteins are polymers built from 20 amino acids and five elements: carbon (key because it forms 4 bonds), hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and phosphorus.
- Enzymes fold into a three-dimensional shape that fits specific substrates (the lock-and-key principle).
- Enzymes can cooperate: the product of one becomes the substrate of the next, forming a metabolic pathway.

### Cell organisation
- Each cell contains over 40 million proteins engaged in millions of reactions per second.
- Compartmentalisation is the physical or temporal separation of reactions (e.g. tissues, organelles).
- Ribosomes (built from proteins and RNA, numbering in the millions per cell) produce proteins from DNA; production takes about one minute.
- The cell nucleus occupies about 10% of the cell's volume.
- Mitochondria produce energy through cellular respiration (the reverse of photosynthesis); heart cells contain several thousand mitochondria occupying about 40% of the cell's volume. They pump protons across membranes; returning protons pass through protein channels, capturing energy stored as ATP, which breaks down to ADP when the cell uses that energy.
- Chloroplasts (about 100 per plant cell) contain chlorophyll proteins and carry out photosynthesis, producing sugar.

### History of life on Earth
- Life on Earth appeared about 3.5 billion years ago; initial energy came from geothermal sources and there was no oxygen.
- 2–2.4 billion years ago: the Great Oxidation Event – excess oxygen killed most bacteria and archaea.

## 5. Life as an information-processing system

- Every organism collects and uses data about its own state and its surrounding environment.
- When changes occur in either the internal or external world, the organism responds purposefully, aiming to survive and reproduce – that is, to maintain homeostasis.
- This principle applies to whole organisms and to individual cells alike.

### Examples of regulation
- Mechanical: the centrifugal governor of a steam engine.
- Biological: insulin – when blood sugar is too high, the pancreas secretes insulin, which prompts liver, muscle, and fat cells to absorb the excess sugar and convert it to fat.
- Oscillators cause alternating activation and deactivation (e.g. the heart).
- Signalling pathways (e.g. hormones) transmit data throughout the organism.
- Information is stored through chemical marks.
- Epigenetics: a set of chemical reactions that permanently switch genes on or off.

### Gene regulation
- DNA stores information using 4 bases (English uses 24 characters, computers use 2).
- Regulatory proteins – repressors (switch genes off) and activators (switch genes on) – ensure that identical cells form different tissues: skin, heart, kidneys, etc.
- A chemical gradient tells embryonic cells how close they are to the site of fertilisation.

### Theory
- Mathematics is the art of perfection, physics is the art of optimal solutions, and biology is the art of good-enough solutions (thanks to evolution).

## Changing the world

- Antibiotic use should be reduced.
- Cancer monopolises the body's resources because cancer cells can ignore signals telling them to stop dividing.
- Genetic predispositions must not be exploited unethically (e.g. to set higher insurance premiums).
- Gene editing poses the next major ethical and scientific challenge.
- Stem cells retain the ability to divide and generate new cells.
- A future challenge: generating higher crop yields with less environmental degradation.
- Funding applied science at the expense of basic science is like cutting corners on a building's foundations in order to make it taller.

## What is life?

- Life is: the capacity to evolve, spatially bounded physical entities capable of communicating with their environment, and chemical, physical, and information-processing systems.
- Life elsewhere in the universe may be based on silicon, which – like carbon – forms 4 bonds.
- Viruses are alive when they replicate and dead when they do not.

### History of Earth
- 4.5 billion years ago – Earth formed.
- For the first ~0.5 billion years conditions were too hot.
- 3.5 billion years ago – oldest fossils; first organisms based on RNA.
- ~1 billion years ago – emergence of eukaryotes.
- ~600 million years ago – first multicellular organisms.
