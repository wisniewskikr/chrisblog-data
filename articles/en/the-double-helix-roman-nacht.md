# Content

## Chapter 1

- In 1951 Watson arrived in Cambridge, where he met Francis Crick.
- Crick was engaged in photographing haemoglobin crystals and had a booming laugh.

## Chapter 2

- Maurice Wilkins was already working on DNA at the University of London, collaborating with Rosalind Franklin.
- The principle of fair play prevented Crick from conducting the same research.
- Linus Pauling was working on DNA at Caltech in the USA.

## Chapter 3

- Watson was staying in Copenhagen to study biochemistry, but was actually working on bacteriophages (phages) – the simplest viruses, composed half of DNA and half of protein.
- Watson travelled to Naples for a conference.

## Chapter 4

- In Naples, Maurice Wilkins gave a lecture and showed an X-ray photograph of a DNA crystal, which fascinated Watson.

## Chapter 5

- Linus Pauling published a paper on the alpha helix in the USA, which inspired Watson to study crystallography in England.

## Chapter 6

- The Fellowship Board rejected Watson's application to move to England and retrain as a crystallographer.

## Chapter 7

- Watson met Crick and together they adopted the assumption that DNA has a helical structure.

## Chapter 8

- Crick was talkative, which irritated people in Cambridge.
- He was working on a doctorate in biology – earlier the war had interrupted his doctoral work in physics.

## Chapter 9

- Francis Crick married for the second time.
- Crick solved the equation of the alpha helix and sent it to Nature.

## Chapter 10

- Rosalind Franklin gave a lecture on her research, rating the crystallographic approach as good and the chemical model-building approach as bad.

## Chapter 11

- Watson and Crick visited Dorothy Hodgkin in Cambridge, who was working on insulin.

## Chapter 12

- Watson and Crick constructed a three-chain model of DNA.

## Chapter 13

- Watson and Crick presented the model to Wilkins and Franklin; it was a theory of diffraction on helices.
- Franklin demonstrated that the model contained too little water.

## Chapter 14

- Bragg, Watson and Crick's supervisor, forbade them from working on DNA and duplicating the research of King's College London.

## Chapter 15

- Watson spent Christmas 1951 in Carradale.
- After the new year, Watson received a fellowship of 2,000 dollars.

## Chapter 16

- Watson turned to studying tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), which is composed of RNA, not DNA.
- Watson began learning to take X-ray photographs of TMV.

## Chapter 17

- Linus Pauling was due to visit London, but at the airport the FBI confiscated his passport – it was the era of McCarthyism.
- Wilkins was still not interested in building models.

## Chapter 18

- On 24 June, using a new X-ray tube with a rotating anode, Watson confirmed with 100% certainty while studying TMV that DNA has a helical structure.
- Watson and Crick turned their attention to the regularities in DNA observed by Chargaff at Columbia University: the amount of adenine (A) equals the amount of thymine (T), and the amount of guanine (G) equals the amount of cytosine (C).
- Theoretical chemist Griffith provisionally confirmed through calculation that bonds between these bases are possible.

## Chapter 19

- Watson travelled to a conference at the Sorbonne in France; Linus Pauling, whose passport had been returned by the FBI, also attended.
- After the conference, Watson went on holiday in the Alps.

## Chapter 20

- Joshua Lederberg discovered two distinct sexes in bacteria and proposed the theory that half of a chromosome comes from one sex.
- Francis Crick worked on tangled coils and published a paper in Nature.

## Chapter 21

- Watson moved to Clare College in Cambridge.
- Watson adopted the theory: DNA → RNA → protein.
- Peter Pauling, Linus Pauling's son, working on his doctorate in Cambridge, received a letter from his father saying he had solved the mystery of DNA, and showed the letter to Watson.

## Chapter 22

- Linus Pauling sent his son a copy of his paper; Watson spotted an error in it – the model contained hydrogen atoms, meaning the DNA acid was not an acid.

## Chapter 23

- Watson went to London and had an argument with Franklin; Maurice Wilkins came to his rescue and in his office showed Watson Franklin's latest photograph, the so-called form "B".
- Looking at the photograph, Watson became certain that DNA has a helical form.
- Franklin maintained that the bases are on the inside and the sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside.
- On the way back, Watson decided to build a two-chain model rather than a three-chain one.

## Chapter 24

- Watson's sister Elizabeth came to Cambridge and met a handsome Frenchman.
- Wilkins visited Cambridge and said he wanted to postpone model-building until Franklin had left; he reluctantly agreed to let Watson and Crick work on it.

## Chapter 25

- Watson and Crick received Franklin's research results because their supervisor coordinated the London research.
- Watson had an idea: two chains with an identical sequence of bases connected by hydrogen bonds.

## Chapter 26

- The following morning the crystallographers in Cambridge refuted Watson's theory.
- Watson discovered that the pair formed by adenine and thymine joined by two hydrogen bonds has an identical shape to the pair formed by guanine and cytosine.

## Chapter 27

- Watson and Crick built a model that was correct from the perspective of X-ray data and the laws of stereochemistry.
- The next step was to verify whether the whole structure was energetically plausible.

## Chapter 28

- Maurice Wilkins came to Cambridge to view the model.
- Franklin buried the hatchet and supported the theory.

## Chapter 29

- More and more data confirmed the theory.
- Watson and Crick submitted the paper to Nature.
