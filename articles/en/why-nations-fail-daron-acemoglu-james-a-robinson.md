# Content

## I. So close, yet so different

- The city of Nogales is divided into a Mexican part and an American part.

### Spanish colonisation strategy
- Population density: 155 people/km².
- Capture the leader, make him pay tribute, and become the new elite – the goal was exploitation, not work.
- People were divided into encomienda and turned into slaves.
- After independence, no stable system of government was established – Mexico had 52 presidents, only a few of whom took power lawfully.

### English colonisation strategy
- Population density: 0.33 people/km².
- England joined the colonisation race late and was left with the remaining territories.
- Smith realised that workers, not gold-seekers, were needed.
- Settlers received land and the right to vote.
- Attempts to create a feudal structure (lords and serfs) failed – settlers had too many other options.
- Eventually all colonies had: a governor and an assembly of landowners.
- Banks held a monopoly thanks to the support of unelected politicians.

### Industrial Revolution
- Invented in England; textile production first by water mills, then by steam mills.
- Spread to the USA.
- Patents were invented; Edison held 1,093 patents in the USA.
- Banks allowed people to take out loans to start their own businesses.

## II. Theories that don't hold up

- The Industrial Revolution changed the ranking of rich and poor countries.
- The geographical hypothesis: disproved by Singapore and Malaysia; historically, South America was more developed than North America.
- The cultural hypothesis (Protestant ethic): does not explain the differences between the Koreas.
- The ignorance hypothesis: bad or good advisers.
- To achieve prosperity, several fundamental political problems must be solved.

## III. Creating wealth and poverty

- North Korea is 10 times poorer than South Korea.
- After World War II, South Korea had an authoritarian regime that nonetheless supported a market economy.
- Differences between countries stem from institutions, rules, and incentives.
- Key to development: inclusive economic institutions, inviolability of property rights, an impartial legal system, and a level economic playing field.
- Engines of prosperity: technology and education.
- Politics is the process by which society chooses the rules that govern it.
- The state holds a monopoly on the legitimate use of force.
- Inclusive institutions are based on centralisation and pluralism; all others are extractive.
- Technological change is accompanied by creative destruction; fear of it is the source of resistance to inclusive institutions.

## 4. Small differences and critical junctures: the importance of history

- Differences between countries arise from small initial differences and critical junctures.
- The Black Death (1346) killed roughly half the population; by 1600 it had created two different worlds – the east and west of Europe.
- Before the plague, Europe had a feudal system: king, feudal lords, peasants.
- In England the plague transformed the feudal system – peasants won greater freedom.
- Consequences in England: Parliament (1588), which could set taxes and thereby gained power; colonisation created independent merchants in England but not in Spain or France.
- 1588: England defeated the Spanish Armada (bad weather and strategic errors by the commander).
- 1688: Glorious Revolution – establishment of Parliament, patents, one law for all.
- 1775: Watt's steam engine and patent.
- In Eastern Europe, peasants were unable to win freedom – the feudal system strengthened (unpaid labour days rose from 1 to 3 per week).
- Critical junctures very often do not change the type of power, e.g. Africa after independence.

## 5. I have seen the future. It works. Growth under extractive institutions

- Extractive institutions are characterised by limited prosperity and narrow elites.
- Growth under such institutions exploits existing technologies rather than new ones – due to lack of incentives and elite resistance.
- Soviet Union: central planning could not replace the invisible hand of the market.
- Congo: the Lele tribe (poor) and the Bushong (wealthy) were separated only by a river; the Bushong were united in the Kingdom of Kuba.
- Middle East: c. 9500 BC, first domesticated plants in the Hilly Flanks region; the Natufians near Jericho; a sedentary lifestyle enabled food storage.
- The transition to agriculture was driven partly by the Long Summer and the ease of storing grains (e.g. maize); the emergence of elites followed.
- Drawback of agriculture: conflict resolution – one could no longer simply move away.
- After the transition to agriculture, villages grew two to six times larger.
- Aztecs: the greatest power around 800 AD; cacao was used as currency.

## 6. Drifting in different directions

### Venice
- Commenda contract: a staying partner provided capital; a travelling partner carried out trade.
- Doges ruled for life and were elected by the General Assembly.
- Great Council: officials and aristocrats; each year one hundred new members were chosen by four randomly selected officials. The Council elected the Senate and the Council of Forty.
- Fatal change in 1286: new members of the Great Council had to be approved by the Ducal Council, the Doge, and the Council of Forty if their fathers and grandfathers had not previously sat on the council. This produced the Golden Book of aristocracy, and the Commenda was banned.

### Rome
- Officials elected for one year; there were many of them.
- Plebeians had a tribune who could veto laws and propose his own.
- Secession – a plebeian revolt when officials failed to meet their demands.
- Senators grew rich while ordinary citizens grew poorer, as lengthening military campaigns prevented them from working their own land.
- Tiberius Gracchus – an aristocrat and tribune of the people – proposed restoring the 120-hectare land limit; he was murdered.
- After Caesar, the republic never recovered; Augustus restricted the rights of ordinary citizens.
- Many citizens lived on state handouts.

## 7. The turning point

- 1589: William Lee invented the first stocking frame, but monarchs refused him a patent, fearing it would put subjects out of work.
- Steps leading to the Glorious Revolution:
  - Magna Carta: a council of 25 barons confirming taxes.
  - 1265: the first Parliament in England.
  - The Wars of the Roses over the throne.
  - Cromwell and the building of a bureaucracy.
  - The break with the Church.
  - The Peasants' Revolt – power partly in the hands of the people.
  - Charles I, forced by war with Scotland to convene Parliament, entered into open conflict with it.
  - 1688: Glorious Revolution – Parliament invited William of Orange; a constitutional monarchy was established.
- Parliament was elected, though only by about 2% of the population; petitions could be submitted to it.
- Spinning was mechanised (Arkwright) and weaving too (Kay's flying shuttle, Cartwright's power loom).
- Investment in canals and roads; entrepreneurs joined Parliament.

## 8. Not in my backyard – barriers to development

- 1445: Gutenberg's invention.
- The Ottoman Empire banned printing; only about 3% could read, compared with about 60% in Europe.
- Professor Papin of Marburg built the first steamboat – it was destroyed, and Papin died in poverty.
- Ethiopia remained absolutist (rulers traced their lineage to King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba) and was the only country to defend itself against colonisers (the Italians).
- Somalia – no central authority, the country divided into clans.

## 9. Blocking development

### Cloves and nutmeg
- Nutmeg trees grew only on the Banda Islands in Indonesia.
- The Portuguese seized Malacca.
- The Dutch East India Company (second after the English one) then monopolised the trade.
- It killed about 15,000 inhabitants of Banda because they were not organised into a centralised structure.

### Slavery
- Slavery grew when sugar cane production began in the Caribbean.
- Enslaved people were transported across the Sahara and across the Atlantic.
- Virtually any offence could result in enslavement.
- Some states were entirely oriented towards the slave trade, e.g. Congo.
- After slavery was outlawed, Africans began to be exploited on farms in Europe.

### Dual economy
- Two sectors: traditional and modern; the development challenge is moving people from agriculture to industry.
- Cape Town was an excellent place to live for Europeans.
- After the Napoleonic Wars the British took Cape Town from the Dutch; the Dutch, calling themselves Afrikaners, founded states in the interior.
- Diamonds were discovered in Kimberley and gold in Johannesburg; the Boer Wars were fought over these lands.
- When Africans began to prosper, Europeans – needing cheap labour – introduced the Native Land Act (90% of the land went to the 20% European population).
- 1976: Soweto uprising.
- 1994: fall of apartheid.

## 10. The spread of prosperity

### Honour among thieves
- In 1788 Arthur Phillip brought convicts to Australia on 11 ships, arriving on 26 January at Botany Bay, Sydney Cove – the colony of New South Wales.
- First civil case: Henry and Susan Cable and their son had a parcel stolen by a ship's captain; the captain lost in court and had to pay compensation.
- First criminal case: a convict stole bread worth 2 pence from another convict.
- Guards seized everything the convicts produced – the system was inefficient; task-based work was soon introduced, after which convicts could work for themselves.
- The Rum Rebellion – the governor was the captain from the Bounty.
- Another rebellion by an elite of former soldiers, also involving ordinary people.
- The first secret ballot in Australia.

### Breaking down barriers: the French Revolution
- Louis XVI long refused to convene the Estates-General; when he called the Assembly of Notables, it ruled that only the Estates-General could raise taxes.
- On 4 August 1789 the National Assembly decreed equality before the law.
- The rise of the Third Republic, war with Austria and radicalisation of opinion; the Jacobins came to power, followed by Napoleon.

### Exporting the revolution
- France was the first country to introduce mass conscription.
- French conquests swept away the feudal order.

### In search of modernity
- In 1868 Japan was ruled by the Tokugawa clan as shoguns; the emperor was purely ceremonial, the system was feudal, and all foreigners had been expelled.
- The domain of Satsuma in the south was autonomous – Okubo Toshimichi was born there; the domains of Choshu, Tosa and Aki later joined the movement.
- The Meiji Restoration: the shogun abdicated, but a two-year civil war followed.
- Economic backwardness leads to military weakness.

## 11. The virtuous circle

### The Black Act
- After the Glorious Revolution, the Whigs in England tried to impose their own laws.
- Protesters blackened their faces; the Black Act added 50 offences punishable by hanging.
- Courts nevertheless frequently acquitted the accused – pluralism guaranteed equality before the law.

### The slow march of democracy
- In 1832 in England the proportion of men entitled to vote rose from 8 to 16%.
- After World War I the vote was extended to all persons over 21.
- Change came gradually.

### Busting trusts
- In 1916 Rockefeller became the world's first billionaire through the Standard Oil Company trust; Morgan built a similar banking empire.
- The Sherman Act targeted monopolies; the press played an important role.

### Packing the Supreme Court
- Roosevelt and his New Deal – the Supreme Court struck down some laws; the president wanted to change its composition, but Congress and the Senate refused.
- In Argentina Perón packed the Supreme Court and weakened the state.

### The virtuous circle
- Critical junctures are moments when elites can be defeated, but victory is never guaranteed.
- Inclusive political and economic institutions mutually reinforce each other; free media also emerge.

## 12. The vicious circle

### The train to Bo
- The British built a railway in Sierra Leone; after independence it was destroyed because it had strengthened central authority.
- The system of indirect rule and the Trade Control Office – the British built extractive institutions that were taken over after independence.

### From encomienda to land grabbing
- In Guatemala after independence, descendants of the conquistadors held power.

### From slavery to Jim Crow
- In the USA, slavery left the southern states far less developed; Jim Crow segregation laws entrenched inequality; no land redistribution was carried out.

### The iron law of oligarchy
- Ethiopia's emperor was overthrown; the Marxist Derg took power.
- The iron law of oligarchy: one ruling group replaces another.

## 13. Why nations fail today

### How to win the lottery in Zimbabwe
- In Zimbabwe the president won the lottery; in 2008 income was half of its 1980 level; unemployment reached 94%.

### Children's crusade
- Children were conscripted into the army.

### Who is the state
- In Colombia both left-wing and right-wing armed groups operated freely.

### El Corralito
- In Argentina the peso was rigidly pegged to the dollar, making exports expensive, imports cheap, and causing inflation.

### The new absolutism
- Currency devaluation in North Korea.

### King Cotton
- Cotton accounts for 45% of Uzbekistan's exports; every farmer is required to devote 35% of their land to cotton; in September children were sent to harvest it.

### Different rules on the same pitch
- In Egypt politics and business were tightly intertwined.

### Why nations fail
- Nations fail because of extractive institutions.

## 14. Breaking the mould

### Three African chiefs
- Three chiefs travelled to Great Britain to persuade it to protect Botswana from Rhodes; the chiefs were accountable to their tribes.
- After independence Botswana transformed itself from one of the poorest countries into one of the richest.
- Diamonds were discovered and it was decided they belonged to the whole nation, not to individual tribes.

### The end of southern exploitation
- On 1 December 1955 in Montgomery, Rosa Parks refused to move to the section of the bus reserved for Black passengers.
- Martin Luther King organised a bus boycott; the Supreme Court banned racial segregation.

### China's revival
- Mao took power in China; he exiled Deng, then later appointed him Vice-Premier.
- After Mao, the Gang of Four (Mao's wife and three associates) seized power; Hua defeated the Gang, and then Deng defeated Hua.

## 15. Understanding prosperity and poverty

- China's economy is like a bird and the Party's control is like a cage.
- Foreign aid to Afghanistan was largely consumed by bureaucracy.
