# Content

## Introduction. The puzzling riddles of Harry Harlow and Edward Deci

- The first driving force is biological drive: food, drink, sex.
- The second force is rewards and punishments from the external environment.
- There is also intrinsic motivation: the task itself is the reward. It is innate but fragile – it needs the right environment to survive.

## 1. The rise and fall of Motivation 2.0

### I. A new operating system

- Motivation 1.0: biological drive.
- Motivation 2.0: rewards and punishments; Taylor and "scientific management" – work is not enjoyable.
- Motivation 2.1: Maslow challenged model 2.0 – people are different from horses. McGregor brought some of these ideas into the business world (relaxed dress codes, flexible schedules).
- Problems of Motivation 2.1 in three areas:
  - **How we organise what we do:** alongside joint-stock companies, open-source projects and hybrids are emerging.
  - **How we think about what we do:** we are not purely rational beings – losses hurt more than gains please.
  - **How we do what we do:** algorithmic tasks (instructions exist) vs. heuristic tasks (no instructions); carrots and sticks work for algorithmic tasks but kill creativity in heuristic ones.
- Open source is based on intrinsic satisfaction.

## 2. Seven reasons why carrots and sticks (often) don't work ...

- Motivation can only be meaningfully discussed once a fair "baseline reward" is in place.
- Rewards can turn play into work: work is something one has to do; pleasure is something one does not have to.
- Contingent rewards ("if – then") kill intrinsic motivation, strip away autonomy, and work only in the short term.
- Stronger incentives lead to worse performance, especially in motor, creative, and concentration-demanding tasks.
- A reward narrows and flattens the field of vision – the "Candle Problem": with extrinsic motivation, solving it took 3.5 minutes longer.
- If people were paid for donating blood, there would be fewer donors, not more – rewards reduce prosocial behaviour.
- Exception: rewards do not cause harm when they remove obstacles or are intended for others.
- Goals imposed by others can have bad side effects; a reward is addictive – once you start paying, there is no going back.
- Rewards encourage short-term thinking: the goal is reached and not a step further.
- Elements of genuine motivation: autonomy, mastery, purpose.
- 7 flaws of carrots and sticks:
  1. They can extinguish intrinsic motivation.
  2. They can diminish performance.
  3. They can crush creativity.
  4. They can crowd out good behaviour.
  5. They can encourage cheating.
  6. They can become addictive.
  7. They can foster short-term thinking.

## 2a. ... and the specific circumstances when they do work

- Motivation 2.0 works when the task is routine.
- Motivation for routine tasks can be increased by: explaining the rationale, acknowledging that the task is boring, and allowing people to do it their own way.
- When someone is learning something new, external payment for creative tasks is less harmful.
- Rewards for creative tasks should be:
  - Unexpected and given after completion – switching from "if – then" to "now that" (taking care not to let them become routine).
  - Non-tangible: positive feedback.
  - Informational: specific (e.g. "good use of colour"), focused on effort and strategy rather than outcome.
- Summary: for creative tasks, use "now that" rewards combined with praise, positive feedback, and useful information.

## 3. Type I and Type X

- Classical behaviourist theories are based on positive/negative stimuli, incentives, and conflicts.
- Self-determination theory is based on needs: competence, autonomy, and relatedness.
- Interest is a third drive – everyone has it, but some factors nurture it while others undermine it; the main mechanisms of Motivation 2.0 undermine it.

### The power of the alphabet

- Type A: tendency toward competition, elevated risk of heart disease.
- Type B: stable.
- Theory X: people do not want to work and must be coerced.
- Theory Y: interest in work is as natural as play or rest.

### Type I and Type X

- Type I (intrinsic): main motivators are freedom, challenge, and purpose.
- Type X (extrinsic): main motivators are money, fame, and recognition.
- Type I is acquired, not innate – every Type X can become Type I.
- Type I almost always outperforms Type X in the long run (not necessarily in the short run).
- Type I does not disdain money, but once it reaches an adequate level, other things become more important; for Type X, money is always paramount.
- Type I is a renewable resource (like the sun), Type X is exhaustible (like coal); it does not run out as long as autonomy, mastery, and purpose exist.
- Type I promotes better physical and psychological well-being.

## 4. Autonomy

### II. Three elements

- ROWE (Results-Only Work Environment): people work whenever they want; money is only a threshold motivator; employees are partners, not resources.

### Players or pawns?

- Management is an invention, not a natural state – by default, humans are active and engaged.
- Autonomy ≠ independence: autonomy means having a goal and the freedom to choose one's own path toward it.
- Autonomous motivation produces more lasting results – people are more satisfied with their work.

### Four core elements

- Examples of companies using autonomy: Atlassian (24 hours off per quarter for personal projects; results: new products, zero staff turnover), 3M (Post-it Notes), Google (Gmail, Translate).
- Four elements of autonomy: what you do, when you do it, how you do it, with whom you do it.

### The art of using autonomy

- People want to be accountable – it is enough to guarantee them control over task, time, technique, and team.
- People work better in teams that organised themselves.

## 5. Mastery

### From compliance to engagement

- The opposite of autonomy is control – it leads to compliance, not engagement.
- During play we experience flow (autotelic experience): the activity is its own reward; the challenge is neither too easy nor too hard.

### Goldilocks on a cargo ship

- The desire to learn something new is the best predictor of performance.
- A task must be matched (between anxiety and boredom) and allow employees to turn work into play.

### Three laws of mastery

- Flow (short time horizon) and mastery (long time horizon) complement each other.
- Mastery is a mindset: elastic intelligence (like muscles – learning goals) vs. fixed intelligence (like height – performance goals).
- Mastery is pain: determination is perseverance and passion for long-term goals; you do what you love even on days when you do not feel like it.
- Mastery is an asymptote: you can get very close but never fully reach it.

### Oxygen for the soul

- Just two days without flow can produce psychological disturbances.

## 6. Purpose

### The purpose motive

- Purpose provides activation energy for life.
- A person's value lies in their ability to influence someone less privileged; the quality of life between birth and death matters.
- Motivation 2.0 focused on profit; Motivation 3.0 does not ignore profit but also emphasises purpose (e.g. TOMS shoes: buy one pair, another goes to children in need).
- Three domains of organisational life:
  - **Goals:** purpose is the main element; profit is the catalyst.
  - **Words:** an MBA student oath was created; it is worth adding ideals to management: honour, truth, love, justice, and beauty.
  - **Strategies:** how you spend money is at least as important as how much you earn; strategy is the bridge between words and goals (e.g. an employee foregoes a pay rise and donates the equivalent to a charitable organisation).

### The good life

- External goals can be attained, but that is not what you truly need – you may achieve what you value and still feel worse.
- Satisfaction is not about having goals, but about having the right goals.

## III. The Type I toolkit

### Type I for individuals: nine strategies for boosting your motivation

- Type I is based on: autonomy, mastery, and purpose.
- Nine strategies:
  - Give yourself a flow test.
  - Start with the big question: figure out your purpose in life.
  - Then ask the supporting question: was I better today than yesterday?
  - Take a "Sagmeister": once every 7 years, take a year-long break.
  - Conduct your own periodic performance review.
  - Unblock yourself using oblique strategies (cards available to purchase).
  - Take five steps closer to mastery: deliberate practice, repeat repeatedly, continuous critical evaluation, improve weaknesses rather than strengths, prepare for mental and physical discomfort.
  - Apply the Webber idea: what gets you out of bed in the morning?
  - Create your own motivational poster.

## Type I for organizations: nine ways to improve your company, office or group

- Try 20% time.
- Encourage peer-to-peer "now that" rewards.
- Conduct an autonomy audit: tasks, time, team and technique.
- Three steps to giving up control: employees set their own goals; use non-controlling language – replace "you must" and "you should" with "think about" and "consider"; set office hours in the style of universities.
- Play "Whose goal is it anyway?": compare employees' answers to the question "What is our company's purpose?"
- Apply Reich's lock test: check whether employees use the pronoun "we" or "they".
- Build intrinsic motivation into the design: make sure the work environment supports Type I.
- Promote the Goldilocks principle in groups – tasks that are neither too easy nor too hard; groups should be diverse, free of competition; occasionally rotate responsibilities and inspire with purpose rather than motivating with rewards.
- Turn the next team retreat into a FedEx day: one day to work on any project, concluded with a presentation to the team.

## Zen of compensation: paying people in a Type I way

- The goal: removing money from the motivational equation entirely.
- Ensure internal fairness (an employee earns as much as others in the company) and external fairness (earns as much as in comparable companies).
- Pay above the market average – this sidesteps the "if–then" reward trap and increases performance and engagement.
- If you use performance metrics, make them broad, appropriate and hard to game; metrics should be varied and rewards modest.

## Type I for parents and educators: nine ideas for helping our children

- Apply the three-part Type I test to homework: does the assignment offer some autonomy, does it promote mastery, and do students understand its purpose?
- Organise a FedEx day: one full day for a chosen project, ending with a presentation.
- Try "DIY" report cards: students decide at the start what they want to learn, then assess their own progress at the end.
- Give children an allowance and assign them chores, but do not link the two.
- Praise in the right way: praise effort and strategy, not intelligence; praise should be specific, given in private, and only when there is a good reason for it.
- Help children see the big picture – they need to be able to answer the question: "Why am I learning this?"
- Look into five Type I schools (schools in the USA).
- Learn from unschoolers – home education as a source of inspiration.
- Turn students into teachers.
