# Content

## 1. Virus, or the genetic bomb

- Viruses are very simple and small – smaller than bacteria and much smaller than cells.
- A virus attaches to a cell using a special protein; once inside, it can do whatever it wants with the cell.
- Each virus attacks only a specific type of cell.
- A single infected cell can produce up to a million new viruses.
- Retroviruses integrate directly into the DNA network in the cell nucleus.
- Bacteriophages (bacteria eaters): some inject genetic material into the bacterium, force it to replicate the virus, and destroy it; others remain dormant inside the bacterium and replicate only when it weakens.
- Giant viruses were not discovered until the 21st century.
- Viruses are hard to fight because they have learned to use our cells and are not alive outside a host.
- It is possible that the nucleated cell arose when a giant virus attacked a bacterium.
- Genetic information is the sequence of nucleotides on a DNA strand – different for different organisms.
- The genetic code is the rule that translates genetic information into the order of amino acids in a protein – the same for different organisms.

## 2. Life based on heredity

- DNA serves to pass information from generation to generation and to produce proteins.
- Proteins are needed to replenish body fluids, build cells, and produce enzymes, hormones, and antibodies.
- Protein production: the blueprint from the cell nucleus is transported by mRNA to the cytoplasm, where the protein is made.

### Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)

- Mitochondria are responsible for energy production; there are several hundred to several thousand of them in each cell.
- They have their own DNA, separate from the DNA in the cell nucleus.

### A well-regulated machinery

- Every cell contains the genetic blueprint for all proteins, but most cells are specialised and produce only some of them.
- Just a few hours after fertilisation, some genes are activated and others are switched off.

### Linking genes to proteins

- Reading the human genome costs about $1,000.

### Gene modification techniques

- CRISPR/Cas9 technique: exploits the natural behaviour of bacteria defending against viruses – fragments of the virus's DNA are incorporated into the bacterium's genome, and the Cas9 protein can cut DNA.
- Gene silencing by RNA: synthetic RNA causes a gene to temporarily stop producing a protein.
- Gene cloning: a DNA fragment is cut out, introduced into bacteria via viruses, and the bacteria are multiplied.
- Only 2–3% of DNA is used to produce proteins; the rest stores various information.

### The genes of humanity

- We are 98% genetically identical to gorillas.
- Differences between humans and other primates are divided into certain (including FOXP2 and CMAH genes), probable (18), and possible (15).
- We carry approximately 4% Neanderthal DNA.
- We also interbred with Denisovans – their remains were discovered in Denisova Cave in Siberia.

## 3. Curing bad genes

- Types of genetic tests: predictive (detecting mutations in a healthy person who may develop a disease in the future), diagnostic (when a disease is suspected), carrier testing, prenatal (during pregnancy), preimplantation (before IVF), and newborn screening.
- Precision medicine: treatment tailored to a specific individual based on genetic testing.
- It is not advisable to order genetic tests on one's own.
- Minors cannot be tested without their consent – the decision belongs to them.
- Biobanks store genetic material for research purposes.

## 7. The terrible GMO

- GMO (genetically modified organisms): organisms created by genetic engineering methods to obtain a specific trait.
- Genetic modifications do not change the basic composition of food and are 100% safe.
- Genetic modifications are not against nature – humanity has been applying them for centuries.
- Golden rice contains vitamin A needed by poor communities, but it is boycotted by activists.
- Insulin was previously obtained from animals; it is now produced using modified bacteria.
- Growth hormone medication and blood-clotting medication are produced in a similar way.

## 8. The future of the superhuman

- Dr He Jiankui genetically modified two girls – Nana and Lulu – to protect them against HIV, of which their father was a carrier; he was sentenced to prison.
- When considering genetic modifications, one should weigh whether it is better to cure one person for a million dollars or to use those funds to treat a million people.
