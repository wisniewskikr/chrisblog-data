# Content

## I. Two systems

### 1. Meet the characters of the story
- System 1: fast, automatic (e.g., reading a mood); skills are either innate (e.g., focusing attention) or learned (e.g., reading).
- System 2: slow, effort-demanding (e.g., multiplication); has a limited pool of attention that can be devoted to only one complex task at a time.
- System 1 continuously generates suggestions, impressions, hunches, intentions, and emotions for System 2; once endorsed by System 2, these become beliefs and actions.
- System 2 is activated when something surprises us or when we need to solve a problem; it also governs self-control.

## 2. Attention and effort
- System 2 is necessary when multiple tasks must be handled simultaneously – this requires attention, and attention means effort.
- By default, the brain tries to accomplish every task with as little effort as possible.

## 3. The lazy controller
- System 2 operates effectively either through self-control or through such deep absorption in a task that a state of flow arises.
- System 2 exerts weaker control over System 1 when it is busy, when alcohol has been consumed, or when sleep is lacking.
- There are three kinds of effort: physical, emotional (e.g., suppressing reactions while watching a moving film), and cognitive.
- Willpower and self-control are depleted – the second time is harder; the same resource pool serves all types of effort.
- Controlling System 1 requires overcoming one's own laziness.
- System 2 can be divided into an algorithmic part (intelligence tests) and a rational part (the capacity to overcome laziness).

## 4. The associative machinery
- Associations are coherent mental links based on resemblance, contiguity in place and time, or causality.
- Types of connections: cause–effect (virus – cold), object–property (lemon – yellow), object–category (banana – fruit).
- Priming effect: an encountered word or image "primes" access to related associations.
- Priming operates between words and behaviour: reading about old age slows one's gait; walking more slowly makes words related to old age easier to recognise.
- Priming also works through body postures: holding a pencil in the teeth (mimicking a smile) improves mood.

## 5. Cognitive ease
- System 1 assesses whether a task involves cognitive ease or requires cognitive effort.
- The illusion of familiarity arises when we associate something with the past; System 1 triggers a sense of familiarity, and System 2 uses this to judge truth or falsehood.
- To help a message reach its recipient more easily: use good-quality paper, vivid colours, rhymes, and clear language.
- Mere exposure effect: the more often we encounter something, the more we come to like it; this has a biological basis (caution toward novelty is life-preserving).
- Creativity is an efficient associative memory.
- Research shows that the better one's mood, the better one's intuition.
- System 1 dominates under: good mood, intuition, creative thinking, and credulity; System 2 dominates under: sadness, alertness, suspicion, and an analytical approach.

## 6. Norms, surprises, and causes

### Assessing normality
- Normality is our internal assessment of reality; surprise arises when something does not fit that assessment.
- Active surprises: we expect an event that does not occur (e.g., a train that fails to arrive).
- Passive surprises: we do not expect them, but we are not astonished when they appear (high probability of occurrence).
- People share a broadly similar sense of normality – concepts like "table" need no definition.

### Seeing causes and intentions
- Physical causality: one object strikes another and the second moves.
- Intentional causality: I pick up the salt shaker because I want to add salt.

## 7. A machine for jumping to conclusions
- Intuitive responses (engaging only System 1) are justified when they are plausible, the cost of error is acceptable, and they save time and effort.
- System 1 has no doubts, because maintaining doubt requires effort.
- System 1 is credulous and accepts everything as true when System 2 is busy or tired.
- Positive test strategy: we tend to search for confirmations rather than disconfirmations (e.g., the question "Is Sam nice?" directs attention differently from "Is Sam unfriendly?").
- Halo effect: a first impression colours all subsequent judgements.
- To avoid the halo effect, each aspect should be assessed independently and those assessments should not influence one another.
- In group discussions, it is useful to ask participants to write down their conclusions independently first, to limit the disproportionate influence of those who speak first or in an assertive tone.
- System 1 aims for coherence, not truth; it operates by the rule "what you see is all there is."
- A good story is coherent, not necessarily complete.
- System 1 errors: overconfidence; framing effect (the same information framed differently evokes different emotions, e.g., "90% of operations are successful" vs. "the mortality rate is 10%"); neglect of base rates (e.g., believing there are more librarians than farmers).

## 8. How judgements are made

### Basic assessment
- System 1 monitors the environment for threats and, upon encountering a new person, automatically assesses their strength (e.g., a square jaw) and intentions (good/bad).

### Sets and prototypes
- System 1 operates on prototypes or typical examples: it handles averages well but handles sums poorly.
- In emotionally charged contexts, quantity is ignored.

### Intensity matching
- System 1 efficiently processes the quantitative dimension of intensity (something is more or less).

### The mental shotgun
- System 1 has difficulty restricting itself to the single task assigned by System 2 – it fires a "shotgun," hitting both the main target and collateral ones.

## 9. Answering an easier question

### Substituting questions
- System 1 frequently substitutes a difficult question with an easier one – this is heuristics.
- Three-dimensionality heuristic: we judge objects by their spatial appearance.
- Mood heuristic: when asked first about happiness and then about dating, there is no link; when asked about dating first and happiness second, the link is strong (the first question primes the answer to the second via System 1).
- Affect heuristic: System 2 tends to justify System 1's emotions rather than criticise them.

## II. Heuristics and cognitive biases

### 10. The law of small numbers
- Two equivalent definitions: large samples are more accurate than small ones; small samples more often yield extreme results.
- The minimum sample size that limits this law can be calculated statistically, yet very few researchers do so.
- System 1 erroneously assumes that a small sample automatically reflects the larger population.
- We very often perceive patterns where there is only chance.

## 11. Anchors
- Anchoring effect: when we encounter a number before making an estimate, our estimate will be close to that number.
- Anchoring as adjustment (System 2): estimation stops when uncertainty is felt – hence higher estimates when starting from a high anchor and lower estimates from a low anchor.
- Anchoring as a priming effect: suggestions and priming influence anchoring through System 1.
- Anchoring index: in a study using sequoias (anchor of 10 m vs. 100 m), the ratio of the differences was approximately 55%.
- Anchoring makes biological sense: when we know nothing at all, we look for any available cue.
- In negotiations, a high opening price raises the final price; an effective counter-strategy is to threaten to walk away if the price is not reasonable.

## 12. The science of availability
- Availability heuristic: when estimating probability, we automatically retrieve examples from memory, which leads to errors – vivid and dramatic events are more available and therefore overestimated; personal experiences are more available than those of others (e.g., we overestimate our own contribution to household tidiness).
- In an assertiveness study: participants who recalled 6 examples rated their own assertiveness higher than those who recalled 12 (System 1 relies on the speed of retrieval; System 2 relies on the number of examples).

## 13. Availability, emotions, and risk

### Availability and affect
- Our mental picture of the world is distorted by the frequency and emotional intensity of messages we receive.
- Example: people believe accidents kill more people than strokes, whereas strokes are twice as frequent a cause of death.
- The emotional tail wags the rational dog: when we like a technology, we downplay its drawbacks and emphasise its benefits, and vice versa.

### Society and experts
- Availability cascade: the more something is talked about, the more probable it seems, which in turn drives even more talk (e.g., media amplification of events).
- Neglect of probability: people tend to assess risk in extreme terms – either it does not exist or it is high; intermediate probabilities are ignored.

## 14. What does Tom W. study?

### Prediction based on representativeness
- In people, similarity overrides probability.
- Example: a description of a student resembling a computer scientist versus the actual proportion of computer science students at the university.

### Sins of representativeness
- Judging probability by representativeness yields better results than pure chance, but it obscures the underlying statistics.
- Solution: focus mainly on statistics, only slightly adjusting results based on the description.

### Keeping intuition in check
- Bayesian intuition helps keep intuition in check: base rates + informational value.
- Example: the probability that Tom W. is a computer science student = 3% (share of CS students) × adjustment for description → roughly 11% combined.
- Guidelines: the result should always stay close to the base rate; question the informational value of your data.

## 15. Linda: less is more

- System 1 treats a coherent scenario as credible rather than a probable one.
- Example: Linda, described as a feminist, seems more likely to be a bank teller and a feminist than just a bank teller.
- The more coherent a description is (not merely more detailed), the more probable it seems.

### Less is more, sometimes even in joint evaluations
- The "less is more" bias: System 1 relies on averages, not sums.
- Example: people value a set of only good dishes more highly than a larger set of good dishes that includes a few broken ones.

## 16. Causes take priority over statistics

### Causal stereotypes
- System 1 operates on stereotypes – it represents categories as norms and patterns, storing "normal" examples.
- Ignoring a stereotype for an individual is morally correct but statistically wrong.

### Causal situations
- Causal base rate: a base rate embedded in a cause-and-effect story, e.g. the probability of being admitted to a highly selective university.

### Can psychology be taught?
- Diffusion of responsibility: if we believe someone else will help, we do not help ourselves.
- Social pressure: under social pressure, people are capable of inflicting a great deal of pain.
- People automatically assume that they and their acquaintances will behave differently from what statistics suggest.
- To overcome this, one must move from the particular to the general: show specific people who did not help, then ask how many people helped – the estimate then becomes accurate.

## 17. Regression to the mean

- Research indicates that praising good performance is more effective than punishing mistakes.
- Apparent contradiction: after praise, performance declines; after punishment, it improves – this is simply regression to the mean, unrelated to praise or punishment.

### Talent and luck
- Success = talent/effort + luck.
- Because luck varies, an above-average performance on one day should be followed by an expectation of a worse performance – this is regression to the mean.

### Understanding regression to the mean
- Correlation of 1: perfect (e.g. English and metric measures); correlation of 0: none (e.g. phone number and income).
- When correlation is imperfect, regression to the mean occurs.
- Example: exceptionally intelligent women tend to marry men less intelligent than themselves – this is equivalent to saying that the correlation of IQ scores between spouses is not perfect.

## 18. Taming intuitive predictions

### Intuitions that ignore regression to the mean
- We intuitively assess a final outcome the same way we assess a forecast for the future, ignoring regression to the mean.

### Correcting intuitive predictions
- Correction steps: establish the base-rate average → adjust for available data → estimate the correlation → adjust for the correlation.
- Intuition-generated predictions tend to be extreme when input data are extreme, and they are held with excessive confidence because of their apparent coherence.

## III. Overconfidence

### 19. The illusion of understanding
- Narrative fallacy: we form our current views and expectations about the future based on a distorted picture of the past.
- We have incomplete information, much was due to chance, yet we believe we understand what happened. Example: the history of Google.
- We count only what we see; we ignore information that was not clearly presented.
- Coherence: the less we know, the easier it is to construct a coherent story.
- Halo effect: the sentence "Hitler liked children and dogs" disturbs us because it disrupts the halo effect.

#### Social costs of hindsight thinking
- Hindsight bias ("I knew it all along"): people forget their past opinions and suggest they predicted the actual outcome.
- When an outcome is bad, we judge the decision as bad even if it was reasonable at the time (e.g. a standard operation that ended in a patient's death).
- When an outcome is good, we judge the decision as good even if it involved excessive risk (e.g. a reckless investment).

#### Recipes for success
- System 1 makes us perceive reality as more orderly, simple, and predictable than it is.
- The illusion of understanding the past produces the illusion that the future can be predicted and controlled.
- A company's financial performance is largely a matter of chance; a CEO's influence accounts for roughly 30%.

## 20. The illusion of validity

- System 1 draws conclusions from sparse data and is unaware of its own errors – it has an illusion of validity.

### The illusion of skilled stock investing
- Statistically, the least active investors gain the most.
- The illusion is stronger when it is reinforced by a group of like-minded people.

### The illusions of media experts
- Research has shown that experts are wrong more often than if results were chosen at random.
- The more prominent the expert, the more extravagant their predictions.

## 21. Intuition vs. formulas

- Statistics recommends using a simple algorithm rather than expert judgment; experts try to be clever, often overreach, and are susceptible to priming effects (e.g. feeling better after a meal).

### Hostility toward algorithms
- Experts are better at short-term assessments when results are visible almost immediately.
- Algorithms are better at long-term assessments when results emerge years later.

### What I learned from Meehl
- The author learned not to dismiss intuition entirely: collecting solid data, applying an algorithm, and then engaging intuition together yields the best results.

### Do it yourself
- How to use an algorithm: select 6 important, uncorrelated traits; prepare questions scored 1–5; stick to the sequence and the final score.

## 22. Expert intuition – when can we trust it?

- Emotions (hope, fear) are learned quickly and easily (e.g. Pavlov's dogs).
- Acquiring genuine expertise takes considerably longer.

### The environment of skill
- Confidence in one's judgments comes from the ease with which they come to mind and from their coherence.
- Intuition reflects genuine expertise when: regular patterns exist in the environment that allow prediction, and those patterns can be recognised through prolonged practice.

### Feedback and practice
- Experts have an opportunity to learn when they receive clear feedback reasonably quickly.

## 23. The outside view

- Lessons from writing a textbook: the difference between the outside view and the inside view; planning fallacy (choosing the most optimistic scenario); irrational persistence with a decision already made.

### The seductive inside view
- When the inside view and the outside view clash, the outside view has no chance – pale statistics cannot beat subjective feelings.

### The planning fallacy
- Estimates dangerously resemble the realistic scenario rather than drawing on broader statistics.
- Large underestimates stem from elements added to a specific plan.

### The planning fallacy: mitigating the consequences
- How to reduce the planning fallacy: find the base rate, then adjust it for available information if warranted.

### Decisions and errors
- Sunk-cost effect: once resources have been invested in a project, it is hard to withdraw; a crisis may help.

## 24. The engine of capitalism

- Optimistic bias: people have a natural tendency toward optimism.
- Flaws of entrepreneurs: they focus on themselves; they concentrate on what they know rather than what they do not know; they neglect competitors and facts; they do not account for blind luck.
- The advantage of optimists is their perseverance in the face of adversity.

### A partial remedy: the premortem
- Before making a decision, imagine that it is one year later and the project has failed, then collect as many answers as possible to the question: why?

## IV. Choices

### 25. Bernoulli's errors
- Theory of expected utility: economists assumed that people always make rational decisions.
- Prospect theory: describes how people actually behave.

#### Bernoulli's error
- Bernoulli's expected utility theory: people assess risk based on utility, not absolute amounts; the utility of each additional unit diminishes as wealth increases.
- Error 1: the theory ignores the past – having 5 million now after previously having 10 million does not feel as satisfying as having 5 million after previously having 1 million.
- Error 2: the theory ignores the reference point – a person with 4 million and a person with 1 million will react differently to a certain 2 million versus a chance at 4 million; when facing a potential loss, people typically take the risk.

## 26. Prospect theory
- The reference point matters – what you expect.
- The principle of diminishing sensitivity: $100 means something different to a rich person and a poor person.
- Loss aversion: the same amount of loss hurts more than the pleasure of the same amount of gain.

### Loss aversion
- The loss-aversion coefficient for most people is between 1.5 and 2.5 – to risk $100, you need to expect a gain of $150.
- In mixed bets, the loss-aversion coefficient produces risk aversion.
- In bets that are both unfavourable, the principle of diminishing sensitivity produces risk seeking.

### The blinders of prospect theory
- Prospect theory does not account for regret or disappointment.

## 27. The endowment effect
- The standard indifference curve ignores the reference point and loss aversion, which is why we favour the status quo.

### The endowment effect
- The endowment effect is closely linked to the reference point and loss aversion.
- We do not want to change the status quo unless the gain is roughly 2:1.
- This applies only to goods we are attached to, not to goods intended for sale.
- Example 1: sellers price the good at $7, choosers at $3.
- Example 2: students who randomly received either chocolate or a pen and could exchange them – only 10% did so.

### Trader thinking
- The endowment effect is smaller in people accustomed to trading and exchange, in the poor (any change is a loss), and varies across cultures.

## 28. Bad events

### Negativity dominance
- Nature has built us so that bad news is processed faster than good news – this helps us avoid predators.
- Negatives trump positives: one cockroach in a bowl of cherries changes a lot; one cherry in a bowl of cockroaches changes nothing.
- For a marriage to survive, positive interactions must outnumber negative ones by 5 to 1.

### Goals are reference points
- Motivation is greater to avoid a loss than to achieve a gain.

### Defending the status quo
- We defend the status quo because of the desire to avoid losses – this applies to our position, marriage, and job.

### Loss aversion and the law
- Fairness is a reference-dependent phenomenon: raising the price of a snow shovel after a blizzard is judged negatively, while cutting wages in hard times is judged acceptable.
- Our brains are much more willing to punish than to reward.

## 29. The fourfold pattern

### Varying chances
- The expected-value principle assumes people always behave rationally, but two effects undermine it.
- The possibility effect: we assign disproportionately high weight to the difference between 0 and 5%.
- The certainty effect: we assign disproportionately high weight to the difference between 95 and 100%.

### The Allais paradox
- Most people choose a 61% chance of $520 over a 63% chance of $500, and simultaneously a 100% chance of $500 over a 98% chance of $520 – which is not rational.

### Decision weights
- We assign disproportionately large weights to extreme values – 0 or 100%.
- The certainty effect is much stronger than the possibility effect, especially when losses are possible.

### The fourfold pattern
- Highly probable gain: risk aversion.
- Highly probable loss: risk seeking.
- Low-probability gain: risk seeking (lotteries).
- Low-probability loss: risk aversion (insurance).

### Gambling in the shadow of the law
- Yielding to feelings and deviating from probabilities makes sense in single cases – you pay for peace of mind.
- It makes no sense across many cases (e.g. numerous lawsuits) – here probability wins.

## 30. Rare events

### Overestimation of probability and excessive decision weights
- System 1 finds it much easier to imagine one favourable event than many unfavourable ones, so successful people assign too high a probability and weight to events.

### Vivid outcomes
- System 1 is guided by ease of retrieval: a vivid description of an event (e.g. "a large blue envelope on Monday") leads us to assign it a higher probability and weight than a neutral description.

### Vivid probabilities
- People respond more to a concrete description than to percentages: "1 in 100 is innocent" is more compelling than "1% are innocent".

### Decisions based on global impressions
- We assign excessive weight to a rare event when it is accompanied by: obsessive fears, vivid images, concrete representations, or an explicit mention of the possibility.
- When these factors are absent, we are capable of ignoring the event.

## 31. Risk policies
- We should try to control our loss aversion and narrow framing (treating small bets separately rather than together).
- When buying shares, it is better to check them infrequently to avoid unnecessary pain from losses.
- Excessive optimism and loss aversion are strategies that cancel each other out.

## 32. Keeping score
- The sense of responsibility for actions is not the same as for omissions.

### Mental accounts
- People keep mental accounts and prefer to close an account with a gain rather than a loss, which leads to errors.
- The sunk-cost fallacy: even when a project is running at a loss, we continue it because the thought of sunk costs is painful (e.g. a fan who bought a ticket is more likely to attend a game in a snowstorm than one who received a free ticket).
- The principal–agent problem: an agent continues a project that benefits themselves personally but not the firm.

### Regret
- Regret is greater when it concerns unconventional behaviour and action rather than inaction.
- Guilt differs from regret – guilt is compared with normal, conventional behaviour.
- Example: Smith gave a hitchhiker a lift once and was robbed (high regret, low guilt); Brown always gives lifts to hitchhikers and was robbed (low regret, high guilt).

### Responsibility
- Responsibility is closely linked to regret.

## 33. Reversals of preference
- Preference reversal: we choose differently when bets are evaluated together than when they are evaluated separately.
- Individual choices can depend on the context in which they occur.
- Preference reversal occurs only across different categories; within the same category we have no such problem.
- Again, broad framing proves superior to narrow framing.

## 34. Frames and reality
- Framing: the unjustified influence of wording on people's beliefs and preferences.
- Example: the word "losses" sounds worse than "costs"; "cash discount" sounds better than "credit-card surcharge".

## V. Two selves

### 35. Two selves
- Experienced utility: we know what gives us pleasure and what causes us discomfort.
- Decision utility: rational principles of conduct.

### Feelings and memory
- We have two selves: the experiencing self (am I in pain right now?) and the remembering self (how painful was that overall?).
- Research shows the remembering self matters more: it ignores the duration of pain and evaluates the peak intensity and how the experience ended.

### Which self should count?
- This is the same System 1 rule of "less is more": System 1 cannot handle a sum (here: of time) and operates on patterns and averages – just as with the evaluation of sets of dishes.

## 36. Life as a story
- People very often ignore the experiencing self in favour of the remembering self – for example, by taking photographs on holiday.

## 37. Experienced well-being
- Happiness means many activities we want to continue (a state of absorption), few activities we want to end, and few activities during which we are indifferent.
- Happiness is doing things we like in company that gives us pleasure.
- Happiness can most easily be achieved by controlling how we spend our time.
- Lack of money makes us unhappy; wealth increases life satisfaction, but statistically money does not increase well-being beyond a certain level.
- Beyond that level we partly lose the ability to enjoy less costly pleasures.
- Life satisfaction and satisfaction with one's current situation are not the same thing.

## 38. Thinking about life
- When we think about life, System 1 is at work: our current mood (e.g. finding a dime or recalling a recent date) and recent events influence our judgement.
- Statistically, marriage does not affect well-being – it merely swaps one set of joys and sorrows for another.
- A person will be dissatisfied with life if they set themselves high goals in youth.

### Focalism
- Nothing is as important as it seems when you are thinking about it – people get used to most things and stop thinking about them (e.g. a car or the climate).
- People do not adapt to: chronic pain, constant noise, or severe depression (a self-reinforcing cycle of unhappy thoughts).
- Miswanting: we overestimate the consequences for our future well-being of major purchases and changes in life circumstances.

### The problem of time
- The mind handles stories well but handles the concept of time poorly.
- As with other evaluations, it remembers mainly the beginning, the peak, and the end.

## Conclusions

- The book introduces two fictional characters: System 1 and System 2, two kinds of beings: Econs and Humans, and two selves: the Experiencing Self and the Remembering Self.

### Two selves

- The interests of the Experiencing Self and the Remembering Self do not always coincide.
- Rules by which the Remembering Self distorts reality: duration neglect, the peak rule, and the end rule.
- Consequences: people prefer short intense happiness over long moderate happiness; they avoid long periods of happiness that would end badly.

### Econs and Humans

- Econs are not susceptible to: priming, the "what you see is all there is" effect, narrow framing, inside view, or preference reversal.

## Humans are not irrational, only susceptible to:

- The whims of System 1.
- The laziness of System 2.
- The solution is "libertarian paternalism" – the state does not restrict choice but provides incentives.

### Two systems

- System 1 generates assessments quickly: based on associations and based on knowledge.
- System 2 cannot determine whether information from System 1 is based on facts or not.

## Appendix A. Judgment under uncertainty: heuristics and cognitive biases

- Three heuristics used under uncertainty:
  - Representativeness: judging whether object or event A belongs to class B.
  - Availability: estimating the frequency of a class or the likelihood of an outcome.
  - Anchoring value: in numerical predictions when a starting value is already available.

### Representativeness

- People often search for a similar question B for which they know the answer, rather than evaluating the actual probability of A.
- Biases arising from the representativeness heuristic:
  - Base-rate neglect: e.g., when judging Steve's occupation people rely on stereotypes rather than statistics.
  - Sample-size neglect: deviations from the norm are more likely in small samples than large ones, but people ignore this.
  - Misconceptions about randomness: belief that randomness self-corrects (e.g., after four blacks the next must be red).
  - Insensitivity to predictability: people forecast based on descriptions rather than statistics, even when the description comes from a single event.
  - Illusion of validity: overconfidence in one's own predictions without real justification.
  - Misconception about regression to the mean: people do not account for it when forecasting.

### Availability

- People estimate probability based on how easily an example comes to mind.
- Biases arising from this heuristic:
  - Ease of recall: estimates are influenced by frequent media coverage, the vividness of an example, or its recency.
  - Bias in the effectiveness of the search set: words starting with "r" are easier to recall than words with "r" in the middle, so people judge the former as more frequent.
  - Ease of imagination: travel safety is judged by how easily examples come to mind rather than actual probability.
  - Illusory correlation: creation of false associations, e.g., between an illness and a drawing.

### Anchoring and adjustment

- An available baseline value influences subsequent estimates.
- Biases:
  - Insufficient adjustment: estimates remain too close to the anchor (e.g., the height of an American spruce).
  - Biases in evaluating conjunctive and disjunctive events: conjunctive events (repeated occurrence) are overestimated; disjunctive events (at least one occurrence) are underestimated.

## Appendix B. Choices, values, and decision frames

- People respond with risk aversion in the domain of gains and risk seeking in the domain of losses.
- Excessive weight is given to near-certain or near-impossible events, and insufficient weight to moderately probable ones.
- Decisions are made under risk (unknown outcome, e.g., a bet) or without risk (known outcome, e.g., an exchange).

### Framing outcomes

- Given the same data, people make different decisions depending on how the problem is framed: they avoid risk for gains but take risk for losses.

### The psychophysics of chances

- People give excessive weight to boundary probability values: the transition from impossibility to possibility (e.g., lottery) and from uncertainty to certainty (e.g., insurance).

### Framing effects

- People react differently to statements framed as gains (e.g., "lives saved", "cash discount") versus losses (e.g., "lives lost", "surcharge for card payment").

### Transactions and exchanges

- People apply mental accounting to transactions: they will drive across town to save $5 on a $15 purchase but not on a $150 purchase.
- They will not buy a replacement ticket when they lose a $10 ticket they already paid for, but they will buy one when they lose a $10 bill.

### Costs and losses

- Endowment effect: reluctance to give up things already owned.
- People dislike losses and prefer costs.
- Example: people reject a bet with a 10% chance of winning $95 and a 90% chance of losing $5, but accept a $5 lottery ticket offering a 10% chance of winning $100 with a 90% chance of winning nothing.
