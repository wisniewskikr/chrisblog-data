# Content

## Introduction

- In 1918 Great Britain ruled over 1/4 of the world.
- Estimated reparations for slavery amount to 777 trillion dollars.
- 3 out of 10 million enslaved people were transported on English ships.

## I. Why Great Britain?

- Great Britain granted licences to plunder other colonies in exchange for a share of the profits – this was a way of waging low-budget war against Spain.
- Great Britain entered the colonisation race late; before it, Spain had dominated South America and Portugal Asia.
- The goal was gold, sought in Canada, Virginia and Gambia – without success. The decision was made to take it from the Spanish, as Drake did.
- A system of privateering emerged.
- In 1588 the Great Armada was destroyed.
- The English initially lagged behind in navigation and shipbuilding, but caught up quickly and invented the galleon.
- Iron cannon were invented as a replacement for bronze ones – harder to cast, but five times cheaper.
- The Empire began with the theft of gold but grew through the cultivation of sugar; Port Royal in Jamaica played an important role.
- The sugar craze: the empire was built on a sweet tooth – the British consumed ten times more sugar than the French; coffee and tobacco were also addictive, and in the eighteenth century tea was added; about 80% of goods were re-exported; textiles were also imported.
- The Dutch model: in 1579 the Dutch expelled the Spanish; the Netherlands was well governed and had the first central bank; Anglo-Dutch wars broke out at great cost to Great Britain; the Dutch East India Company was founded in 1602.
- The Glorious Revolution of 1688 brought a de facto merger with the Netherlands – the Dutch stadtholder William of Orange was invited to the throne; the English learned financial management.
- The Dutch received Indonesia and the spice trade; the English received textiles and trading posts in India – Fort Madras, Bombay and Calcutta; full power, however, lay with the Mughal emperor in Delhi; civil wars had weakened India.
- Competition with France over territories: the French captured Madras; the British invested in their navy; scientific research began to be supported; Britain defeated the French invasion, partly because it could borrow money; Indian rulers granted the English the right to collect taxes; half the men in the Company were Scottish.
- Great Britain introduced excise duty to repay its war debts.

## II. White plague

- Experiments with colonies began in Ireland and were later extended to America; those colonies were saved by John Smith and based on tobacco.
- America attracted religious fundamentalists – about 1/3 of the Mayflower passengers were Pilgrims; religious tolerance and cheap land were also draws.
- The Native American population fell from 560,000 in 1500 to 280,000 in 1700.
- Most emigrants arrived in America under the indenture system of temporary servitude – usually for 4–5 years.
- The Maroons were formerly enslaved people who were bought off; enslaved people proclaimed independence in Haiti.
- In 1775 the first exchange of fire between the British and Americans took place at Lexington.
- The so-called Tea War arose from a reduction, not an increase, in the duty on tea; the real reason was taxation of Americans without their consent.
- On 4 July 1776 the Declaration of Independence was signed.
- The British won at Bunker Hill and captured New York and Philadelphia, but Washington triumphed at Yorktown.
- American independence was a catastrophe for enslaved people – it delayed abolition by a generation – and for Native Americans.
- Convicts sentenced for minor offences were transported to Australia; Governor Macquarie oversaw the colony; sheep were displacing the Aboriginal people.
- The Durham Report recommended granting self-government and a federal structure to the colonies.

## III. Mission

- The British wished not only to colonise but also to civilise.
- In Freetown, Sierra Leone, enslaved people were freed; Macaulay was the first governor and an expert on slavery.
- The change in attitudes towards slavery began in Clapham; the Quakers were also opposed to slavery.
- David Livingstone – a self-taught prodigy – became a preacher and a doctor and was sent to Africa.
- Livingstone began converting locals, but it proved ineffective, so he turned to exploring new territories.
- He was the first European to cross Africa from coast to coast and discovered Victoria Falls.
- The journalist Stanley found the missing Livingstone.

## 4. The heavenly race

- In India, 900 British officials and 70,000 British soldiers governed 250 million Indians.
- Queen Victoria married her cousin Albert; their favourite residence was Osborne House on the Isle of Wight, across from Portsmouth – at the time the largest port in the world.
- The Royal Navy was the world's greatest military force; 1/3 of merchant ships were British.
- The British suppressed the slave trade but supported the opium trade.
- Sailing ships crossed the Atlantic in 4–6 weeks; steamships cut the journey to 2 weeks and later 10 days; the voyage to Cape Town was reduced from 42 to 19 days.
- Ronalds invented the telegraph, which reduced the time to receive news from India to a matter of hours; railways and maps were further pillars of empire.
- The cartographer George Everest gave his name to the world's highest mountain.
- Emperor Tewodros II of Abyssinia imprisoned British citizens, believing the British had shown him insufficient respect; Napier organised an expedition, freed the hostages and modernised the city of Lucknow.
- The hill station of Simla was a comfortable place to live in India; Kipling was born in Bombay, did not like England where he was sent to school at the age of 5, and on returning to India became a journalist.
- British officials were few; many lower-ranking functionaries were local.
- A rebellion broke out in Jamaica; Eyre brutally suppressed it, but England brought legal proceedings against him.
- A law was introduced in India allowing Indian judges to try white men; this provoked a white backlash and was eventually withdrawn, though in doing so it had united Indians.
- Curzon became Viceroy of India; the aim was to maintain hierarchy, with maharajas kept obedient.
- An attempt to partition Bengal provoked rebellion and was withdrawn; the capital was moved to New Delhi.
- The British extracted only 1% of India's income; the Dutch took 7–10% from Indonesia.

## 5. The power of the Maxim

- 1/3 of African colonies belonged to England.
- Rhodes founded the De Beers diamond company and financed the Rothschild bank; he also created the British South Africa Company.
- He deceived the Zulu warrior king Lobengula and defeated him using the Maxim gun; the conquered territory was named Rhodesia.
- Hiram Maxim developed his invention in the USA but was primarily interested in the British market.
- After Rhodes's death the Rhodes Scholarship was established; the process of colonisation was privatised.
- Goldie created the Royal Niger Company and also made use of the Maxim gun.
- The French built the Suez Canal; the British subsequently occupied Egypt; European powers carved up Africa.
- By 1909 the British Empire controlled 25% of the world's land surface with a defence budget of only 2.5%.
- Chamberlain was the first fully self-conscious imperialist prime minister; Ireland was denied the right to home rule.
- Francis Galton, in Hereditary Genius, pioneered the idea of a hierarchy of races.
- In Sudan the Mahdi challenged British Egypt; at the Battle of Omdurman there was a massacre of the Dervishes – Kitchener commanded and Churchill served as a war correspondent.
- The Boers resisted colonisation and held vast gold deposits; the British were defeated at Spion Kop.
- The Boer Wars were to England what Vietnam was to the USA; the British set up concentration camps for Boers, provoking a domestic backlash.
- Germany became a threat to the empire; England forged an alliance with Germany – it had a navy but no land army.

## VI. Empire for sale

- In 1914 Churchill was First Lord of the Admiralty – the minister responsible for the world's largest navy.
- Britain entered the First World War out of fear of German hegemony and because the government feared crossing to the opposition benches.
- Germany sought an alliance with Turkey, as Istanbul sits on the Bosphorus – the narrow strait separating the Mediterranean from the Black Sea.
- The Gallipoli landings aimed to seize Istanbul quickly but ended in a massacre.
- Lawrence incited the Arabs against Turkey.
- After the First World War England gained new territories but was overall worse off.
- Orwell was born in Bengal as the son of a civil servant in the Opium Department and was educated at Eton; people were gradually losing faith in the empire.
- After the war, countries were indebted; unemployment rose during the Great Depression; the British failed to invest in new technologies.
- Gandhi in India – a lawyer with experience of the Boer Wars; General Dyer ordered troops to fire on a gathered crowd.
- Hitler admired the British Empire.
- The Nanjing Massacre: the Japanese killed between 260,000 and 300,000 people in China.
- The British lost Singapore and were forced to build a railway for the Japanese – the bridge on the River Kwai and the Death Railway.
- In Egypt, Nasser attempted to nationalise the Suez Canal; the British attacked and lost.
- The Cold War strengthened independence movements.

## Conclusion

- The large developmental gap between former colonies and Britain emerged only after they gained independence.
- Nearly half of former colonial countries – 23 – became façade democracies after the empire's collapse.

### New imperialism

- The role of the former empire was to be assumed by the international community or the European Union, but neither institution has the financial means to do so.

### Bearing the burden

- Great Britain produced 8% of total world output; the USA produces 22%.
