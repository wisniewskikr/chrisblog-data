# Content

## Introduction
- The most important thing is a calm atmosphere at home.
- Strategy and consistency are key.
- Building a bond with the child.

## 1. The last shout
- The first step is controlling your own emotions.
- Five ways to stay calm:
  1. Before you react, pause – think about how to achieve a good outcome for everyone.
  2. Don't judge the situation immediately – ask questions.
  3. Postpone the decision – say you need to think about it.
  4. Appreciate a positive reaction – congratulate yourself.

## 2. These are the rules here
- Children need to feel safe in their own home.
- Consistency is a key issue.
- Say: "these are the rules here".
- Five types of consistency: consistent expectations, emotional consistency, verbal consistency, consistent routine, consistent calm.
- Buy time in order to make a good decision.
- Work out a plan together with your partner.
- Take responsibility – don't pass it on to the other parent.
- Be consistent 8 out of 10 times.

## 3. Relationship-based parenting
- The bond with a child must be constantly and mindfully nurtured.
- Build it on daily habits.
- Notice good things and praise them – labels stick.
- Pay attention to small things: e.g. 10 brief praises a day and 3 extended praises a day.
- Four situations for positive comments: acknowledge greater-than-usual effort; a positive moment before school; positive things at mealtimes; positive things at bedtime.
- Show pride in your child – e.g. put photos and notes on the fridge.
- Building positive relationships creates "emotional currency" useful in difficult moments.
- Care for the child rather than paying them.
- Teach the child to feel proud of their own behaviour.

## 4. Counter-intuitive parenting
- Impulsive reactions are the enemy of good parenting – tone down your emotions.
- Traps to avoid: expecting the child to "know how to behave"; keeping a list of wrongdoings; using behaviour points.
- Don't add up positives and negatives – they cannot be weighed against each other.
- Positives and negatives should be kept separate.

## 5. Rules reset
- Rules must be known to everyone and repeated constantly.
- Rules make a child feel safe, even when they resist them.
- There should be three rules – for example: kind, helpful, and calm.

## 6. Positive phrases, positive scenarios
- Use affirming, encouraging, and positive words.
- Example of a good message: "I like that you are usually kind, but your behaviour today leaves a lot to be desired".

## 7. Difficult moments
- When a child is overwhelmed by emotions, your job is not to be swept away by them.
- A child loses control of their behaviour due to a lack of emotional regulation – an overflow of emotions or the dominance of one emotion.
- Don't say "calm down" – it doesn't work.
- Empathy is key: short, simple instructions, focus on feelings rather than logic, e.g. simply being beside the child.
- Ignore secondary behaviours that distract from the original issue (smirking, swearing, etc.).
- "Fogging" technique: acknowledge that you are listening and return to the original issue – e.g. "I understand, but still…" or "I hear you, but still…".
- Say "we" rather than "I".
- Don't accuse; use "I noticed" – e.g. "I noticed you wanted to leave through the cat flap".
- Apparent compromise: when a child doesn't want to go to bed, suggest "How about we compromise?" and offer a small concession, e.g. "Tomorrow I'll let you know earlier when you can play".
- After an outburst, give the child time to cool down – be very careful, as a second outburst is very likely.

## 8. Fair consequences
- Punishments are ineffective and damage the relationship.
- Don't send children into isolation – they need an adult's presence to manage their emotions.
- Counting down (1, 2, 3) is ineffective.
- Punishing through shame is ineffective.
- Look for the mildest consequence that is effective.
- One approach is repairing the harm caused, e.g. cleaning a wall dirtied with paint.
- Prepare a set of around three consequences in advance – ideally agreed with the child.
- A consequence must follow the misbehaviour quickly, especially with young children.
- Possible consequences: going to bed 10 minutes earlier; listening to boring radio in the car; temporary suspension of privileges; screen-time restriction; brief exclusion from play.
- Use the phrases: "this is a reminder" and "this is a warning".
- Have a private conversation between the adult and the child.
- If a consequence has been given, remind the child of their earlier good behaviour.

## 9. Restorative conversations
- A restorative conversation does not only address the current problem but serves to repair the relationship afterwards.
- Apologies should not be automatic.
- The goal is to show the child how their behaviour affected others.
- Six restorative questions:
  1. What happened?
  2. What were you thinking at the time?
  3. What have you thought about since?
  4. Who was affected by the incident and how?
  5. What can you do to make amends?
  6. How can you do things differently in the future?

## 10. The parenting promise
- Change takes time.
- Act in small steps, but regularly.
