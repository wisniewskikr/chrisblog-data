# Content

## Introduction

- Erwin's Law: the diversity of the biological world is much greater than we think.

## 1. Unpleasantly surprised by life

- Evolutionary line: Homo habilis (2.3 million years ago) → Homo erectus → … → Homo sapiens, Neanderthals, Denisovans.
- The total human population worldwide was around 10,000–20,000.
- 14,000 years ago Homo sapiens began adopting a more sedentary lifestyle.
- 7,000 years ago the first cities appeared.
- Around 10,000 BCE there were about 100,000 people; by year zero about 10 million.
- Linnaeus (Swedish, 1707) developed the principles of our classification system: genus (e.g. Homo) and species (e.g. sapiens). The problem: Sweden has few species, while Colombia has 20 times more.
- In the 1970s Erwin went to Panama, applied poison to a single tree and discovered that the number of insect species was far greater than anyone had imagined. Conclusion: the average animal is a fly, wasp or beetle.
- Species living in Earth's crust have generation times ranging from 1,000 to 10 million years; they may account for 20% of all organisms.
- In Germany insect biomass has fallen by 70–80%.

## 2. Urban Galapagos

- Wilson discovered one of nature's most stable laws — the species–area law: the larger the area, the greater the number of species.
- The law has two components: the probability of extinction is higher on smaller islands, and the probability of colonisation is higher on larger islands.
- To preserve biodiversity, half of Earth must be kept wild.
- Speciation (the formation of new species) depends on area: the larger the area, the greater the probability of speciation; and on a species' mobility: the lower the mobility, the greater the probability of speciation.

## 3. The unintentional ark

- Because of climate change, most species will need to move.
- Species have different climatic niches.
- There are corridors through which species migrate.

## 4. The last escape

- The law of escape: an example is escaping from malaria.

## 5. The human niche

- Ideal conditions for humans: warm and dry, an annual average of about 13°C and about 1,000 mm of precipitation per year.
- The higher the temperature, the more quarrels and aggression.
- Deviating from the optimal temperature also negatively affects GDP.
- The desirable climatic zone is shifting towards Canada, Scandinavia and northern Russia.
- By 2080, 3.5 billion people may be outside their climatic niche.

## 6. The intelligence of crows

- Evolution struggles when there is high variability instead of gradual, continuous change.
- The cognitive buffering law: animals with larger brains are able to adapt to new conditions.
- Current changes differ from the past in their speed, magnitude (temperatures this high have not occurred for about 40 million years) and instability (droughts and floods).
- Coping strategies: inventive intelligence and specialisation.

## 7. Opening up to diversity to balance risk

- Genetically uniform crops are not prepared for a variable climate.
- The diversity–stability hypothesis: the greater the diversity, the greater the stability — just as with a stock portfolio: greater diversity means lower risk.

## 8. The law of dependency

- The first caesarean section dates from 300 BCE; today 1/3 of children in the USA are born this way.
- Termites evolved from cockroaches and digest wood thanks to microbes in their guts; microbes are passed to offspring via faeces during moulting, a process resembling being born anew.
- Hoatzins are birds that feed on living leaves; microbes enable them to digest those leaves.
- Children acquire microbes during natural childbirth.

## 9. Humpty Dumpty and the robotic sex bees

- Monoculture crops mean there is a brief feast for bees followed by a desert for the rest of the year.
- In the USA bees are transported from place to place because they are in short supply around monoculture farms.

## 10. Living with evolution

- The MEGA-plate experiment: antibiotics and E. coli bacteria were studied. The bacteria divide every 20 minutes and mutations occur once per billion divisions. Within 48 hours they became resistant to the first antibiotics; they reached the zone with the highest antibiotic concentration after 10 days.
- Bacteria and viruses have an advantage: there are more of them (higher probability of mutation) and they reproduce faster.
- Strategies for fighting bacteria: (1) using diversity — mutation temporarily weakens bacteria, causing them to lose out to other bacteria; (2) growing resistant and non-resistant plants together — bacteria feed on the non-resistant ones; (3) researching and predicting mutations; (4) using solutions developed by nature.

## 11. This is not the end of nature

- Nature is not under threat — mammals are just one small branch of the tree of life.
- Our bodies are adapted to conditions that have prevailed on Earth for 1.9 million years, although those conditions were relatively rare.

## Conclusion

- The average lifespan of a species is 2 million years; Homo sapiens has existed for about 200,000 years.
- Young species are the most at risk of extinction.
