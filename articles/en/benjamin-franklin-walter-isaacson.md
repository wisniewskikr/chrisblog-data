# Content

## 1. Benjamin Franklin and the founding of America

- Franklin's morality: leading a virtuous life leads to salvation.

## 2. The pilgrim's journey

- In the late Middle Ages the surname Franklin denoted free men who held property but no titles.
- The Franklin family carried a gene of dissent, independence, trade, and craftsmanship.
- The line of ancestors: Thomas, Henry, Thomas II, Josiah, and Benjamin – he was the youngest son of youngest sons.
- In 1683 Josiah emigrated to America for religious reasons (he was a Puritan) and economic ones; he brought his wife and three children and went into the candle- and soap-making trade.
- Josiah climbed the Puritan social ladder: he joined a covenant, became a tithingman (keeping order), and then a constable (overseeing tithingmen).
- Josiah was 31 with five children when his first wife Anne died; he married 21-year-old Abiah and they had nine more children together.
- Benjamin Franklin was born on 17 January 1706 in Boston; he was a natural leader and loved to swim.
- He attended the Latin School – he was an excellent pupil and was advanced a year; Harvard and a career in the clergy were planned for him, but his father changed his mind.
- He transferred to a writing-and-reading school, where he failed mathematics.
- At age 10 he worked full-time in his father's workshop; not enjoying it, he was taken by his father to visit other craftsmen's shops.
- At age 12 he began an apprenticeship with his brother James, nine years his senior, at a printing shop, which allowed him to read widely.
- James founded the New England Courant – the first independent newspaper in America.
- Benjamin taught himself a writing style by memorising and reproducing essays; he began publishing them under the pseudonym "Silence Dogood".
- When James was jailed for a few days over the newspaper's content, Benjamin ran the paper; this earned him a document releasing him from his apprenticeship.

## 3. The journeyman

- Benjamin, aged 17, set off for Philadelphia; he cared about how others perceived him.
- He took a job with Samuel Keimer.
- He turned a bump on the head into an anecdote: "Go through life with your head bowed and you will avoid many painful bruises".
- He befriended Pennsylvania's governor William Keith, who wanted a skilled printer in the colony.
- He went to London to buy printing equipment, but the governor failed to provide the promised funds; he worked first at Palmer's printing house, then moved after a year to Watts's.
- In 1726 he returned to Philadelphia with the merchant Denham, who paid his passage in exchange for work in his shop.

## 4. The printer

- In Philadelphia he worked in Denham's shop, but Denham soon died.
- He returned to Keimer's printing house as its manager.
- He opened his own printing shop with Hugh Meredith, funded by Meredith's father; because his partner was not diligent, Ben bought out his share.
- He founded the "Leather Apron Club", also known as the Junto, emphasising mutual assistance and self-improvement.
- He wrote articles under the pseudonym Busy-Body to bring about the downfall of Keimer's newspaper.
- He published a pamphlet on paper money.
- After Keimer went bankrupt, Ben bought his newspaper and began publishing the Pennsylvania Gazette.
- He competed with Bradford's newspaper, which ran stories about sex, violence, and gossip.
- He expanded his printing empire by sending young apprentices to other cities and forming partnerships with them.
- He formed a relationship with Deborah Read, who had lived in her mother's house – where Ben had lodged on first arriving in Philadelphia; Deborah had a husband who was said to have died in the West Indies.
- He had a son out of wedlock – William.
- A second son, Franky, was born but died at age 4.
- He had a plan for self-improvement based on pragmatic humanism; he opposed dogmas.
- He wrote satires under the pseudonym Poor Richard Saunders in almanacs; the saying "Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise" is attributed to him.
- The collected sayings of Poor Richard Saunders were published as a book and for a time were the most famous book in the colonies.

## 5. The public citizen

- Ben believed in organisations and founded, among others: a public library, a fire brigade, a night watch, and a hospital.
- In America two spirits compete: individualism and the communal spirit expressed through numerous associations.
- The Freemasons were founded in London in 1717; Ben joined them and became Grand Master.
- He was not a dogmatist, but printed the books of preacher Whitefield – which made Ben wealthy and the minister famous.
- In his rivalry with publisher Bradford he backed Hamilton for Speaker of the Assembly, became Clerk himself, and stripped Bradford of the postmaster position in Philadelphia, taking it over himself.
- In 1743, eleven years after Franky's birth, Sally Franklin was born.
- He founded the American Philosophical Society, linking the various colonies and devoted to science.
- He organised Militia Associations to defend against the French; he served as a common soldier despite being offered the rank of colonel.
- He preferred to have written of him "he lived usefully" rather than "he died rich".
- At age 42 he retired, handing the firm over to be managed by others.
- Maxim: "He who has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do so again than he whom you once served".

## 6. The scientist and inventor

- Ben began his investigations from curiosity and then sought practical applications; he did not patent his inventions.
- He built the Franklin stove.
- He turned to electricity and introduced the terms battery, charged, neutral, condensation, and conductor.
- He formulated the law of conservation of charge: when you create a positive charge, you simultaneously create a negative charge.
- He noticed the similarity between lightning and electricity; his idea reached France, where the king ordered an experiment, and Franklin in America drew lightning with a kite – thus discovering the principle of the lightning rod.
- For his discoveries he received honorary doctorates from Harvard and Yale.

## 7. The politician

- The purpose of education according to Franklin: to serve humanity, one's country, friends, and family.
- In 1751 he opened the University of Pennsylvania – the first non-denominational college in America.
- He devised a public-private partnership: if the community raised half the funds for a hospital, the Assembly would supply the other half.
- He was initially prejudiced against Black people and owned several Black slaves.
- He became a member of the Assembly; Pennsylvania was a proprietary colony belonging to the Penn family – Ben became an opponent of the Proprietors, and later of the Crown as well, for their refusal to pay taxes.
- He became Deputy Postmaster of the colonies.
- The French began building a chain of forts along the Ohio River connecting Louisiana to Canada; young George Washington attempted to stop them, failed, but won fame and the rank of colonel.
- At a meeting of colonial delegates in Albany, Ben proposed the concept of a federation; the plan was rejected by both the colonial assemblies and London.
- The principle of compromise: one must yield on lesser matters to achieve more important goals.
- He flirted with Catherine Ray – pleasures he both enjoyed and respected.
- London sent the arrogant General Braddock and Governor Morris; Braddock's army was routed, Washington survived, and the Proprietors still refused to pay taxes.
- Ben organised a defence force and this time accepted the rank of colonel; he guarded the frontier against the French.
- He set off for London to obtain permission to tax the Proprietors.

## 8. Troubled waters

- Ben arrived in London with his son William; his landlady was Margaret Stevenson, who looked after him much as a wife would in America; he also formed a close relationship with her daughter Polly.
- He engaged in a dispute with the Penns over the future of Philadelphia; England treated the colonies as a market to be exploited.
- He conducted research into heat and evaporation.
- The Penns refused to negotiate with Ben.
- He made a summer trip to Scotland, where he met Adam Smith and the philosopher Hume.
- William fathered a son, Temple Franklin.
- Ben also travelled to Holland and Flanders.
- George III became king; in America England defeated France and gained Canada.
- After 5 years Ben returned to America.
- William became Governor of New Jersey and married Elizabeth Downes; Ben did not attend his wedding.

## 9. The holiday at home

- Ben made a seven-month tour of postal offices, taking his daughter Sally with him; it confirmed his conviction that America was a single whole.
- The Paxton Boys were killing frontier Indians; this threatened civil war, as some white settlers defended the Indians; Governor Penn agreed to compensate Indian traders, and the Proprietors cooperated with the Paxton Boys.
- Ben was once again sent to London as a representative of the Assembly.

## 10. The agent provocateur

- In London Ben balanced between loyalty to the Crown and American identity; he wanted the king to grant Americans the same rights as Englishmen.
- Colonists accepted external taxes (customs duties) but opposed internal taxes.
- Ben also took care of his grandson Temple.
- The London Parliament passed the Stamp Act (an internal tax) payable on every letter, book, etc.; Ben supported it – this was his greatest political mistake and damaged his reputation.
- He proposed a boycott of British goods and avoidance of stamps; he appeared before Parliament.
- Parliament repealed the Stamp Act.
- Ben went on holiday to France.
- Sally married Richard Bache; Benny Bache was born.
- Polly married physician and lecturer William Hewson; Ben attended her wedding.
- Treasury minister Townshend imposed taxes on glass, porcelain, paper, and dyes; this caused outrage in Boston.
- Ben came to understand that Parliament either could impose all taxes or none at all.
- The Boston Massacre: soldiers from the customs service killed 5 people after one of them was insulted by a young apprentice.

## 11. The rebel

- Ben spent his holidays in Ireland and Scotland.
- Ben began writing his autobiography, *The Life of Benjamin Franklin*.
- Ben became friends with 11-year-old Kitty Shipley.
- Ben met his son-in-law.
- Ben discovered, among other things, that germs cause disease.
- Parliament suspended the tea tax and granted an exclusive concession to the East India Company.
- On 16 December 1773 in Boston, Sam Adams and the Sons of Liberty threw tea into the sea.
- Because of that event and published letters, Ben was interrogated at the Cockpit and ceased to be Postmaster General of America.
- Ben's wife Deborah died.
- The First Continental Congress convened in America and voted to boycott British goods.
- Ben returned to America with his grandson Temple.

## 12. Independence

- On 18 April 1775 the British sought to arrest Sam Adams and John Hancock; the Americans killed or wounded 250 of them.
- The Second Continental Congress convened.
- Ben met his son, but the two stood on opposite sides of the political scene.
- In June the bloody Battle of Bunker Hill and the burning of Charlestown further inflamed hostility between America and England.
- Ben took charge of the postal service and the issuing of new currency.
- Ben went on a mission to Quebec.
- Thomas Paine published the pamphlet *Common Sense*, arguing that the king is nothing special.
- William was arrested by the Americans.
- The Committee of Five for the Declaration of Independence comprised: Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Roger Sherman, and Robert Livingston.
- On 2 July the Continental Congress voted for independence.
- The Articles of Confederation were created.
- Ben was sent to France on a diplomatic mission, taking his grandsons Temple and Benny with him.

## 13. The ladies' man

- Ben arrived in Paris, where he was very famous; he wore a beaver-fur cap and spectacles.
- Ben settled in the village of Passy; Chaumont was the owner of the estate and supplied the Americans.
- Bancroft, the delegation's secretary, was a spy for England.
- France provided aid to America, but in secret.
- The British captured Philadelphia.
- The British lost at Saratoga.
- The French agreed to support America on condition that Spain, their ally, did so as well.
- The British sought to make peace with America.
- France signed treaties with America: a treaty of friendship, a commercial treaty, and a military treaty.

## 14. Bon vivant

- Adams arrived in France.
- Ben met Voltaire, also an elderly Enlightenment philosopher.
- Ben joined the Masonic Lodge of the Nine Sisters.
- Ben became friends with Madame Brillon.
- Ben became friends with Madame Helvétius.
- Ben enjoyed playing chess.
- Benny was taken to school in Geneva.
- Temple worked at the legation but was more interested in having fun.

## 15. The negotiator

- Ben became America's sole plenipotentiary in France.
- John Paul Jones became a naval commander and captured the ship *Serapis*.
- Benedict Arnold, an American general, defected to the British.
- At Yorktown, Washington together with the French defeated Cornwallis.
- Franklin began peace negotiations with the British.
- Essential conditions: recognition of the USA, withdrawal of troops, recognition of borders, fishing rights off Canada.
- Desired conditions: reparations for destruction, British admission of guilt, free-trade agreement, Canada for America.
- In 1782 England and America signed an agreement.
- Ben brought Benny to Paris.
- Temple became involved with a married woman, Caillot; they had a child, Theodore, who died of smallpox.
- On 21 November 1783 the Montgolfier brothers made the first balloon flight.
- Ben wrote the second part of his autobiography and invented bifocal spectacles.
- Jefferson arrived in France; together they drafted treaties with other countries.
- Temple went to London to meet his father; Temple resembled Ben in appearance, while Benny resembled him in mind.
- After 8 years Ben began his return to America; in England he met his son.

## 16. The sage

- During the voyage Ben conducted experiments on the Gulf Stream; crowds welcomed him in America.
- Benny enrolled at the University of Pennsylvania and later became a printer.
- Ben expanded his house.
- Ben became Governor of Pennsylvania.
- In 1787 work on the constitution took place in Philadelphia: two chambers were established — a house with proportional representation and a senate with equal representation.
- Ben was the only person to sign all 4 founding documents.
- Ben continued writing his autobiography.
- Washington became president.
- Toward the end of his life Ben committed himself to the abolition of slavery.
- Ben died on 17 April 1790 at the age of 84.

## 17. Epilogue

- William Franklin: received nothing in the will.
- Temple Franklin: inherited a substantial portion of the estate and his grandfather's notes; he delayed publishing them and much was lost; he fathered another illegitimate child.
- Sally and Richard Bache: inherited the bulk of the estate; they visited England.
- Benjamin Bache: published a newspaper, fell out with his parents, married Margaret Markoe; died at the age of 29 from yellow fever.
- Ben established funds in Boston and Philadelphia to support poor tradesmen; these funds later gave rise to institutions of higher education.

## 18. Conclusions

- Saying: "It is hard to make an empty sack stand upright."
- Ben freed morality from theology.
- Ben took the lightning bolt from the gods and the sceptre from tyrants.
