# Content

## Introduction

- Four basic questions: What are we doing? Why are we doing it? Could we do something better? Why would that be better?
- The Big Three ethical theories: virtue ethics, deontology, utilitarianism

## Part I

### 1. Should I punch my friend in the face for no reason?

- Name of theory: Aristotle's virtue ethics
- Key question: what kind of person should I be?
- Three pillars of the theory: nature, habit, learning; keywords: flourishing, virtues, moderation
- The goal of life is happiness/flourishing, achieved by possessing virtues in the right amounts and proportions
- Virtues are good character traits (e.g. courage, temperance) that make us want to befriend a person
- A person is born without virtues but with the capacity to acquire them; natural abilities, when cultivated, become skills
- We develop virtues by acting virtuously: justice through just actions, courage through courageous actions
- Strong emphasis on wise teachers and wise friends
- Virtues must be held in the right proportions: be generous, but not too generous; be brave, but not too brave
- The closer we are to the golden mean in one trait, the closer we are to the golden mean in others (accumulation)
- Cruelty should top the list of vices as a transgression against other beings
- Shklar: knowledge makes a person gentle, ignorance hardens the heart

## 2. The trolley problem

- Name of theory: consequentialism, specifically utilitarianism (Bentham and Mill)
- Key question: what should I do?
- The best action is the one that makes the greatest number of people happy
- Practical application: e.g. raising large sums of money by appealing to donors' egos
- Seven criteria for evaluating pleasure: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (how soon it will occur), fecundity (what other pleasures it may lead to), purity (how little pain it causes), extent (how many people it benefits)
- Utilitarianism creates problems for the integrity of the person taking the action
- Thought experiments: trolley problem with a lever, trolley problem with a large person, a doctor killing a janitor for organs to save 5 patients, an electrician electrocuted at a transformer during a final watched by millions

## 3. Should I lie and tell my friend I like her blouse?

- Name of theory: deontology (Kant)
- Key question: what should I do?
- The categorical imperative: act only according to a maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law
- Before acting, one must consider what would happen if everyone acted the same way
- The complete opposite of utilitarianism: happiness has no significance
- Second formulation of the categorical imperative: do not use people to get what you want
- Thought experiment: a murderer asks about your brother

## 4. Should I return the shopping cart?

- Reasonableness: when I disagree with the other side, I am able to restrain or modify my demands in order to reach a common goal
- Contractualism: only those principles that reasonable people agree to are ethical
- Contractualism is an ethical minimum; if we can, it is worth doing more
- Ubuntu: a person becomes a person through other people — contractualism with a turbocharger; we not only owe others something, but we exist through them
- The individual does not exist alone: whatever happens to the individual happens to the group, and whatever happens to the group happens to the individual
- Example: returning a shopping cart

## Part II

### 5. Should I save people from a burning building?

- Happiness pump: a person driven not by a self-preservation instinct but by an other-preservation instinct — wants to make others happy
- Aristotle's philosophy assumes that vices are acceptable because they keep us in the middle (of the golden mean)

## 6. Selflessness

- Moral luck: if we perform good deeds, we should be rewarded for them
- Mindfulness: according to Thich Nhat Hanh, it is the practice of continuously returning to the present moment
- Pragmatism: according to William James, it focuses on the practical difference, fruits, and facts; a mediator and reconciler that removes rigidity from our claims

## 7. On a car accident

- Guilt: the internal sense of having done something wrong
- Shame: humiliation for who we are, reflected in us through the judgment of others from the outside; goal: for the wrongdoer to stop, or for others to know what was done
- Whataboutism: a defensive strategy — when you have done something wrong, you throw sand in the accuser's eyes, without addressing the substance of the matter

## 8. Accumulating good deeds

- Moral exhaustion allows a brief rest from the rules when: the rules do not cause obvious harm to others, and when we must accept that we are not perfect
- The Overton window: something that was once unacceptable becomes law
- Example: fare-dodging on a crowded tram

## Part III

### 9. Buying a new iPhone

- Opportunity cost: something we could have done but did not
- Ultra-utilitarianism (philosopher Singer): one can always do more
- Ultra-utilitarianism strips us of integrity — of being a complete and indivisible person — in the name of an unspecified "human happiness"
- The ethical minimum grows with one's capacity to act: a millionaire can do more than a busy single mother
- Ethical action starts at Scanlon (minimum) and drifts toward Singer (what more can I do)
- Example: Bill Gates donated $30 billion but kept $54 billion

## 10. The sandwich

- "It has always been this way" is the last line of defense of the true ignorant — we make a virtue of failing to seek better solutions
- When we do something problematic, two things must be held in mind: "I love this, it is part of my identity and integrity" and "the person who created it is troubling"
- One must try and draw a line somewhere; if we are wrong, draw it again
- Examples: a fast-food chain owner made homophobic remarks — should one stop buying his sandwiches?; Woody Allen and his marriage to his stepdaughter

## 11. Should we make difficult decisions?

- Existentialism (founder Sartre): we are born from nothing, we decide for ourselves who we are and what we do, then we die; and at the same time we are a model for others
- Camus was an ultra-existentialist — nothing in life matters; he proposed three ways out: suicide, finding meaning in some structure (religion, family, etc.) — which only covers up the meaninglessness of life, or living with acceptance of that meaninglessness

## 12. Context

- We all have potential, but sometimes circumstances prevent us from turning it into virtues — for example, poverty
- One cannot apply a single ethics to everyone
- Saying: some people were born with a silver spoon and think they made it themselves
- The veil of ignorance (creator Rawls): one child divides, the other chooses — and that is how social rules should be set up

## 13. Apologizing

- Apologies border on ethics: there is an attempt, a failure, and an apology; they help heal wounds
- A lie: you know the truth but deliberately say the opposite
- Bullshit: the truth is irrelevant to you; you only want to present yourself to your interlocutor in a favorable light
