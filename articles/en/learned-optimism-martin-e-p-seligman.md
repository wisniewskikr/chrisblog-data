# Content

## I. The search

### 1. Two ways of looking at life

- Pessimists give up more easily and fall into depression: they believe bad events will last a long time, will affect all their actions, and are their own fault.
- Optimists treat failure as a temporary and isolated setback caused by unfavourable circumstances, and see problems as challenges.
- At the root of pessimism lies helplessness; as we grow older we gain control over our environment (arm and leg movements at around 3–4 months, intentional crying, walking, speech).
- Traditional formula: success = talent + desire; today optimism must be added to that.
- Pessimism/optimism affects depression, success, and physical health.
- Non-negative thinking is important.
- Key concepts of control theory: learned helplessness and explanatory style.

## 2. Learning helplessness

- Behaviourism was the dominant school of thought – it assumed that behaviour is determined by punishments and rewards.
- Dog experiment: dogs that received shocks they could not stop never learned to escape when moved to a cage that allowed escape – unlike dogs that could stop the shocks or those that received no shocks at all.
- Cognitivism emerged alongside behaviourism.
- The epicycle analogy: just as astronomers who assumed geocentrism kept correcting their model with new epicycles, behaviourists had to increasingly patch their theories.

## 3. Explaining misfortune

- Explanatory style has three dimensions: permanence (is an event permanent or temporary), pervasiveness (does failure in one area carry over to others), and personalisation (is the cause internal or external).
- Optimism can become a problem when it leads to shifting responsibility onto external factors and avoiding accountability for one's own actions.

## 4. Deep pessimism

- Depression is pessimism raised to the power of two.
- Three types of depression: ordinary (arises from sadness and passes), bipolar (alternating manic and depressive phases, strong hereditary burden, well treated with lithium carbonate), and unipolar.
- Depression manifests as changes in thinking, mood, behaviour, and physiological reactions.

## 5. How you think, how you feel

- Women fall into depression more often than men because they "ruminate" on thoughts; men more often take action.
- Depression = pessimism (intensified by rumination) + failure.
- Drug treatment does not change thinking and involves a high relapse rate; cognitive treatment changes thinking and results in fewer relapses.
- Cognitive therapy includes: recognising and controlling automatic thoughts, creating new explanations, freeing oneself from depressing thoughts, and identifying faulty assumptions.

## II. Areas of life

### 6. Success at work

- Pessimists are sadder but have a more realistic picture of reality.
- Alternating between optimism and pessimism is healthy.
- Pessimism is largely a legacy from our ancestors and requires conscious control.

## 7. Children and parents: the origins of optimism

- Explanatory style is shaped by three sources: inheriting it from one's mother, critical feedback received at school, and life crises.

## 8. School

- Women are far more susceptible to depression than men, but in childhood the reverse is true: boys are far more susceptible.

## 9. Sport

- Optimism also applies in sport, including team games.

## 10. Health

- Experiment on rats with cancer (50% baseline rate): rats able to stop electric shocks recovered at 70%, those unable to stop them at 20%, and those not subjected to shocks at 50%.
- Chain of events: loss → pessimism → depression → reduced catecholamines → increased endorphins → immune impairment → illness.

## 11. Politics, religion, and culture: a new psychohistory

- In America, the more optimistic a candidate, the greater their chances of winning an election.

## III. Transformation: from pessimism to optimism

### 12. An optimistic life

- Optimism should guide us when the cost of failure is low; for example, a pilot should not be guided by optimism.
- The ABC model: Adversity (describe the obstacle), Belief (what you think happened), Consequences (how it affected you).
- Distraction techniques: a rubber band on the wrist, writing down the thought, scheduling a specific later time to deal with the thought.
- Disputation: arguing against one's own negative thoughts by examining evidence, alternatives, implications, and the usefulness of the belief.
- Extended model ABCDE: Adversity, Belief, Consequences, Disputation, Energisation.
- Externalising voices: a friend or spouse voices your pessimistic thoughts aloud while you respond to them aloud.

## 13. How to help a child avoid pessimism

- Techniques helpful for children: ABC, ABCDE, and externalising voices.

## 14. The optimistic organisation

- Optimism is the difference between helplessness and resourcefulness.

## 15. Flexible optimism

- Today the individual is the maximum unit of value; in the past the individual was the minimum unit – more attention was paid to duties and less to feelings and sensations.
- Taking care of the body: e.g. jogging is tiring but has positive effects in the long run.
- Taking care of emotions: caring for the public good instead of personal gain is tiring but positive in the long run; suggested actions include donating money to help others (ideally overseeing the entire process), giving up a personal pleasure in favour of community activity, supporting the homeless, and actively participating in community organisations.
