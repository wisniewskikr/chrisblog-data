# Content

## Introduction

- People tend to return to what is comfortable and familiar, even if it is not what is best for them.

## 1. Self-care – the art of survival

- Self-care means mindfully dedicating time to things that make it easier to get through daily life.
- True self-care is about long-term health and well-being, not momentary pleasure.
- You need to take care of yourself first in order to be able to take care of others.
- The problem arises when you too often return to activities that improve how you feel in the moment but make you feel worse in the long run.
- Types of self-care: physical, mental, emotional, spiritual, recreational, financial, and professional.
- Physical self-care: actions that support your physical health and well-being, e.g. medical check-ups and dental visits; eating and sleep are the most important.
- Mental self-care: activities that stimulate your intellect and creativity, e.g. learning new skills, reading books, or taking an online course.
- Emotional self-care: learning to identify and express what you feel; naming emotions can weaken their hold over you.
- Spiritual self-care: actions that deepen your connection with something greater than yourself, e.g. volunteering, meditation, or spending time in nature.
- Recreational self-care: finding time for activities and hobbies that bring you pleasure and satisfaction.
- Financial self-care: developing healthy spending and saving habits.
- Professional self-care: actions that help you feel supported in your role and career path, e.g. attending professional development workshops.
- You cannot control everything in the world – focus on controlling what you can.
- If you are struggling to start a task, try setting a timer for 5 minutes.
- You can also tackle a difficult task in the company of another person.
- Cheer yourself on: you cannot control external acceptance, but you can give it to yourself.

## 2. Meeting the inner critic

- Negativity bias: the brain focuses more on bad things than good ones – historically this served survival.
- Thoughts turn into words, words into actions, and actions into habits.
- Cognitive distortions:
- Catastrophising: the brain jumps to the worst possible scenario; the line between what is possible and what is probable becomes blurred.
- All-or-nothing thinking: one small bad thing ruins the entire day; everything is either bad or good.
- Emotional reasoning: treating your own emotions as facts.
- The fairness fallacy: believing that everything in life should be fair and equal.
- The reward fallacy: believing that sacrifice and hard work must be rewarded.
- Personalisation: taking everything personally, even when it has nothing to do with you.
- Overgeneralisation: drawing unwarranted conclusions from limited information and applying them to all future situations.
- Blaming: attributing responsibility for negative outcomes or your own emotional pain to everyone and everything outside yourself.
- The need to be right: being prepared to do anything to prove that your opinions and decisions are correct.
- The control illusion: feeling you have no control over anything, or conversely feeling responsible for everything and everyone.
- Jumping to conclusions: making assumptions without supporting evidence (mind-reading or fortune-telling).
- Mental filtering: fixating on the negative aspects of a situation while ignoring the positive ones.
- Instead of building self-worth through comparisons (a vicious cycle – there will always be someone better), it is worth practising self-compassion: treating yourself with kindness despite your shortcomings, failures, and mistakes.

## 3. The mind-body connection

- The brain is primarily concerned with our safety.
- The brain is not equipped to warn us about threats without a clear endpoint, such as climate change.
- The brain has a "ground floor" and an "upper floor".
- The ground floor includes the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.
- Sympathetic nervous system: responsible for acute responses to threats (physical or psychological) – fight, flight, freeze, or fawn.
- Parasympathetic nervous system: the rest-and-digest system; responsible for renewal, growth, and recovery.
- Window of tolerance: the space between hyperarousal and shutdown.
- After trauma, the "upper floor" may struggle to have a say.
- Techniques that support regulation: grounding, conscious breathing, visualisation, power posing, contact with nature.

## 4. Navigating the full range of feelings

- Emotions are neither good nor bad – they are information.
- Not all emotions reflect the truth.
- If you want to get out of a hole, you have to stop digging.
- It helps to name an emotion and add "and that is okay".
- It is worth reflecting on what a given emotion is trying to tell you.

## 5. Healthy boundaries are tough

- Boundaries are limits that protect well-being.
- Types of boundaries: porous (no boundaries), rigid (keeping others at a distance), and healthy (like a fence with a gate).
- Emotions signal when a boundary needs to be set.
- Types of communication: passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, and assertive.

## 6. We need other people

- Oprah Winfrey: "Many people will ride the limousine with you. Seek those who will take the bus with you."
- It is worth consciously deciding who to invest your time and energy in.
- Talk to strangers and look for common ground.
- Criteria for trust: honesty, transparency, accountability, ethical behaviour, and proof of alliance.
- Four attachment styles: secure, anxious, avoidant, and disorganised.
- Love languages: acts of service, words of affirmation, physical touch, quality time, and thoughtful gifts.
- When conflict arises in a relationship, fight fair.
- Do not follow "I'm sorry" with "but".

## 7. Everyone grieves something

- Grief is a response to the loss of something you value.
- Types of grief: anticipatory, delayed, cumulative, disenfranchised, traumatic, and complicated.
- Pain is a reaction to loss; suffering occurs when others deny us our right to that pain.
- The goal of grief is to honour the pain and ease the suffering.

## 8. In search of meaning

- The ability to sit with discomfort is important.
- It is better to seek meaning than happiness.
- Four pillars of meaning: belonging, purpose (how you can be of service to the world), transcendence (flow – engagement and intense focus), and storytelling (reflecting on your life and creating a meaningful narrative from your experiences).
- It is worth asking yourself: what would you like more of in your life? What would you like less of?

## 9. Taking care of mental health in the long term

- Choose your therapist carefully; you can change therapists after some time if they are not the right fit, but it is worth letting them know first that something is not working – they may be able to fix it.
