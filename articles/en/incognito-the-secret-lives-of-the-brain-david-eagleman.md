# Content

## 1. Someone is sitting in my head, but it's not me

### Powerful magic

- Consciousness is only a small cog in the brain – most processes happen on autopilot.
- Consciousness is like a newspaper: it reports the most important things that have already happened.

## 2. The testimony of the senses: what are experiences, really?

- About one-third of the brain is devoted to processing vision.
- We receive only a small fraction of the data from our eyes – the rest we model and infer from context.
- The brain doesn't need to know everything; it just needs to know where to get the data.
- Only focused attention delivers specific data, e.g. how much pressure the left versus the right shoe exerts.
- Eyes act like agents – they gather data without the involvement of consciousness.
- The brain doesn't just interpret data, it also fabricates it: the large blind spot in each eye is filled in by the brain.
- Seeing has to be learned – the example of a blind person who regained sight after many years.
- The brain only needs to be given information and it will learn to use it; blind people can "see" via impulses on their back or tongue (Wicab BrainPort device, used by Erik Weihenmayer).
- The visual cortex sends the thalamus the signals it expects; the thalamus compares them with incoming signals and returns only the difference.
- Signals reaching consciousness are like a time-delayed talk show – e.g. signals from the eyes and ears travel at different speeds, but the brain makes them seem synchronised.

## 3. Gaps in reasoning

- Procedural memory encompasses skills to which we have no direct conscious access.
- There can be an enormous gap between what we know and what we are aware of – examples: chicken sexers or aircraft spotters.
- People often fall in love with reflections of themselves in others – e.g. people whose names begin with the same letter marry each other more often.
- Priming: an earlier stimulus steers the mind onto a particular track of thought.
- Mere exposure effect: things we have seen before appeal to us more – hence the effectiveness of advertising.
- Illusory truth: a statement heard before is more readily accepted as true.
- Intuition: test subjects consciously identified which card decks were favourable after 25 cards, but the subconscious knew after just 13; people with brain damage kept choosing badly even when they intellectually knew the answer.
- The role of consciousness: it sets goals and trains the subconscious – after that, the rest is left to the subconscious.
- What distinguishes humans is flexibility – the ability to adapt the nervous system to a specific task.
- Advantages of automation: faster decisions and energy savings.

## 4. The kinds of thoughts that can be thought

- Nothing is tasty or disgusting in itself – palatability is an indicator of usefulness; e.g. baby koalas eat their mothers' droppings to obtain bacteria that break down eucalyptus.
- We see only one ten-trillionth of the light spectrum.
- Synesthesia: the ability to perceive simultaneously through multiple senses (e.g. hearing colours); affects one in a hundred people.
- Homo sapiens was a hunter-gatherer for 99% of its existence.
- The brain is a set of programs that ended up there because they solved an evolutionary problem.
- Infants come into the world equipped with ready-made programs for: properties of objects, physical cause-and-effect relationships, numbers, the organic world, the beliefs and goals of others, and social interactions.
- Instincts are strong, innate behavioural patterns – they operate like learned tasks, without conscious access; they include: sexual fascination, fear of the dark, compassion, quarrelsomeness, jealousy, seeking justice, problem-solving, incest avoidance, and reading mood from facial expressions.
- Signals of beauty: in women – full lips, shapely buttocks, narrow waist (signs of fertility and oestrogen); in men – a strong jawline, facial hair, broad chest (signs of testosterone).
- A woman's assessment of a man's attractiveness may change depending on the phase of her menstrual cycle.
- When we see someone only briefly, they seem more attractive than when we observe them at length – the system does not want to miss an opportunity.
- Vasopressin makes voles monogamous; in men, the more copies of the RS3 334 variant, the weaker the effect of vasopressin and the greater the likelihood of remaining single or having marital problems.
- We are programmed to lose interest in a partner after about 4 years – the time it takes to raise a child through basic dependency.

## 5. The brain is a group of rivals

- The mind consists of many simple, unconscious subsystems.
- These subsystems partially overlap and compete to determine a decision, e.g. whether or not to take a piece of cake.
- Two main systems: the emotional (monitors internal state) and the rational (monitors external state).
- The emotional network is essential for setting priorities – without it we would not know what to do when the external world does not change.
- People prefer quick gains over deferred benefits.
- A virtuous person is someone who can resist the temptation of an immediate reward.
- Ulysses contract: a decision tied to a future condition, e.g. "I'll eat the cake, but tomorrow I'll go to the gym".
- The brain's hemispheres complement each other – a child who has one hemisphere removed before the age of 8 can function normally.
- We have two independent memory systems: the hippocampus (ordinary memory) and the amygdala (traumatic memory).
- Evolution equipped us with many overlapping systems – when one fails, another can be used.
- Hidden programs execute behaviour while the conscious mind tries to justify it; consciousness does not intervene when a task is handed over to zombie programs.
- The role of consciousness: it enables learning and supervises the zombie programs.
- Consciousness is probably graded rather than binary, and depends on the number of zombie programs.
- Keeping a secret is unhealthy for the brain.

## 6. Why legal responsibility cannot be spoken of?

- The amygdala is responsible for guilt and aggression.
- Free will does not exist – everything is conditioned, so the legal system should not focus on punishment but on the future.
- The frontal lobes are not fully developed until around the age of 25 – only then does a person begin to operate on the principle of think first, then act.
- Proposed solution: frontal lobe training in prisons.
- The term "legal responsibility" should be replaced with "potential for improvement".
- Punishment still serves the functions of retribution and deterrence.

## 7. After dethroning the king

- Temporal lobe epilepsy causes auditory hallucinations – Joan of Arc probably suffered from it.
- Our behaviour is influenced by: drugs, neurotransmitters, viruses, and genes.
- Our behaviour is a combination of genes and environment: bad genes + good environment = good person; good genes + bad environment = good person; bad genes + bad environment = bad person.
- Extensions of the brain include the hormonal system and the immune system.
