# Content

## Introduction

- Science is not a collection of facts about the world – a collection of facts about the world is called "knowledge".
- Science is a way of thinking about the world and making sense of it, which can result in new knowledge.
- Besides science, there are other paths to knowledge: art, religion, debate, and reflection.
- Scientific theories must be falsifiable – the author of a theory must indicate a way to refute it (e.g. white swans).
- Scientific theories must be testable, verifiable, and self-correcting.

## 1. Something is either true or it is not

- Context matters.

## 2. It is not that simple

- Occam's razor – the simplest explanation is the best.
- The simplest explanation is not always the correct one – example: creationism versus Darwin's theory.

## 3. Puzzles should be enjoyed, but also solved

- The laws of nature are logical and comprehensible.
- Scientific thinking is built into our DNA – it manifests as a child's curiosity.

## 4. If you do not understand something, it does not mean you will not understand it if you try

- If you do not understand something, you most likely have not put enough effort into understanding it.
- We do not need to know everything – we can stand on the shoulders of giants and draw on their knowledge.
- Today we must choose what deserves our time and attention.

## 5. Do not place opinion above evidence

- Easy access to information means that many people express views with great confidence – one must be able to separate facts from opinions.
- Good evidence should come from a reliable source, be consistent, and not be susceptible to arbitrary interpretation.
- No evidence can refute a conspiracy theory.
- In science, we try everything we can to disprove a theory.

## 6. Recognise your own cognitive biases before judging the views of others

- Confirmation bias: arises when we surround ourselves with people who hold the same views.
- Illusory superiority: every person overestimates their own competence while failing to notice their own weaknesses.
- The Dunning-Kruger effect: if you are incompetent, you cannot know that you are incompetent – recognising a lack of qualifications in a given field requires qualifications in that field.
- The problem with social media: the incompetent shout the loudest, because the competent are aware of the gaps in their own reasoning.
- Correlation does not imply causation – e.g. a higher number of churches is associated with a higher number of crimes, but this results from a larger population.
- We must ask ourselves why we believe something to be true.

## 7. Do not be afraid to change your mind

- Cognitive dissonance: arises when an important belief is challenged by new information.
- Uncertainty is a lack of certainty, not ignorance – in science, nothing is accepted as an absolute truth.

## 8. Take the side of reality

- A "confidence index" can be assigned to every source of information.
