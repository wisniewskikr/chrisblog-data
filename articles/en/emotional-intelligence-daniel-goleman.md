# Content

## Emotional intelligence is innate. But emotional competencies are not.

- The ability to control impulses is the foundation of will and character.
- Two morally important attitudes today: self-control and compassion.
- The skill of directing oneself toward the right person, to the right degree, at the right moment, for the right purpose, and in the right way.

## I. The emotional brain

### 1. What are emotions for?

- Emotions guide us when we face challenges too important to be left to reason alone.
- Emotions are impulses to action.
- Types of emotions: anger, fear, happiness, tenderness, surprise, disgust, sadness.
- Emotions are shaped by life experience and culture (e.g., different ways of expressing grief).
- Our mind consists of two parts: emotions (the stronger they are, the more power they have) and reason.
- The brain consists of the limbic system (basic emotions) and the neocortex (consciousness).

## 2. The autonomy of emotional hijacking

- Emotional hijacking occurs when the limbic system takes control of the body.
- The source of feelings is the amygdala – located just above the brainstem, near the base of the limbic ring; there are two such bodies.
- When the amygdalae are destroyed, we stop: feeling, remembering feelings, recognising those closest to us, feeling fear, competing, and cooperating.
- The amygdala acts as the brain's watchdog – it examines every incoming impression asking: is this something I hate? is this something that causes pain? is this something I fear? If the answer to any question is yes, it triggers an alarm.
- The thalamus receives signals from vision, translates them into the brain's language, and then sends them simultaneously along two pathways: to the amygdala (a shortcut, with its own unconscious memory operating by association, contacting the adrenal gland and triggering adrenaline) and to the neocortex (which manages emotions).
- Because the amygdala is faster, we react to its signals more quickly.
- The hippocampus stores dry facts, while the amygdala retains their emotional context and causes emotionally charged events to be remembered with particular intensity.
- Operating by association can cause errors, e.g., a loud bang in peacetime triggers a wartime reaction.
- The amygdala develops fastest in the child's brain, which can give rise to early emotional problems.
- The frontal lobe: the right part contains fear and aggression, the left keeps the right in check.
- The connection between the amygdala and the cortex means we cannot think clearly when overwhelmed by strong emotions; yet without this connection we make poor decisions – everything becomes equally indifferent.
- Feelings are essential for making good decisions: they provide direction, eliminate bad choices, and highlight good ones.
- Reason determines probability; feelings determine value.
- Feelings and reason are equal partners – we must strive for their balance.

## II. The nature of emotional intelligence

### 3. When the smart are foolish

- Intelligence accounts for only about 20% of life success; the remaining factors include motivation, impulse control and deferral of gratification, mood regulation, empathy, and an optimistic outlook.
- School should help children find the field that best matches their talents.
- Seven basic intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial (e.g., architects), kinaesthetic (fluency of movement), musical, interpersonal, and intrapersonal (brilliant concepts).
- Types of emotional intelligence: knowledge of one's own emotions, managing emotions (calming down), the ability to motivate oneself (deferring desires), recognising emotions in others, initiating and maintaining relationships with others.

## 4. Know yourself

- Self-awareness is the examination of experience together with emotions – it is the first step of emotional self-control; one can act in accordance with an emotion or not.
- Types of responses to emotions: aware of their feelings, overwhelmed by emotions, accepting their emotions (but doing nothing to change them).
- Alexithymia: lacking words for what one feels – even though the emotions are genuinely present.
- There are two levels of emotions: conscious and unconscious.

## 5. Slave to passion

- Virtue is emotional balance, not the suppression of emotions.
- Mood is short-lived; general well-being is long-lasting.
- The art of calming oneself is a fundamental life skill.
- We have no control over when emotions arise or what kind they are; we only have control over how long they last.
- Rage is the most relentless emotion – it is a prolongation of anger.
- Anger is the hardest to control; the best strategy is to reframe the situation as quickly as possible (later it no longer works). It operates on both a short-term level (immediate fight or flight) and a long-term level (we remain on edge and quickly fall into anger again).
- Ways of dealing with anger: reframing the situation; waiting it out in a calm environment without dwelling on negative thoughts, ideally with an engaging distraction.
- Venting anger almost never reduces it – it only makes us feel better momentarily.
- Worry heightens alertness, but prolonged worry that brings us no closer to a solution can turn into a phobia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, or panic attacks.
- To cope with worry, one must catch such a thought as quickly as possible, calm down, and then logically reason through the low probability of the feared outcome.
- Sadness causes us to stop seeking new situations in order to adjust to the present one; types: sadness, melancholy, depression.
- The theory of cathartic sadness is false – it only prolongs sadness.
- Effective actions against sadness: entertainment and exercise, a small success (a long-postponed task), comparison with someone worse off, social work, suppression of feelings (though the cost is unknown).

## 6. The master aptitude

### Anxiety

- Anxiety can significantly disrupt reasoning ability – it impairs working memory in relation to the current task.
- A feeling becomes pathological when we pay no attention to anything else.
- Sometimes, however, anxiety provides motivation.
- Anxiety must be neither too low (no preparation) nor too high (paralysis).

### Motivation

- The way emotions amplify or disrupt our ability to think and plan defines our limits.
- The fundamental psychological ability is the capacity to resist an impulse (emotions move us – hence the etymology of the word); example: four-year-olds who could wait for a second marshmallow.
- Laughter enables more open-minded thinking.

### Hope

- People with higher levels of hope set themselves more difficult goals and know how to work to achieve them.
- Hope is the belief that one has the will and the means to achieve one's goals – this is what distinguishes it from wishing.
- Characteristics of hope: motivation, keeping one's spirits up, flexibility when a task is too difficult, breaking a task into smaller parts.

### Optimism

- Optimism is the expectation that, despite setbacks, things will ultimately work out well.
- Realistic optimism is good; naive optimism is bad.
- Optimists treat failure as something that can be changed and do not blame themselves but rather some external factor; pessimists do not.

### Self-confidence

- It underlies both hope and optimism; it can be strengthened by acquiring a new skill.

### Flow

- A person merges with the task, being at the peak of their abilities, with emotions working in their favour.
- It produces a sense of elation, which is why we gladly return to it.
- We think neither of success nor of failure – pure pleasure in the activity is the motivation.
- Conditions for achieving flow: full concentration and a task in which one is skilled.
- This can be used in school, building on the child's profile; learning must be neither too easy nor too difficult.

## 7. The roots of empathy

- Empathy stems from self-awareness – the more open we are to our own feelings, the more skilfully we read the feelings of others.
- We most often express emotions non-verbally, which is why empathy is so useful.
- Development of empathy: up to age 3 – motor mimicry (the child behaves as though the misfortune had happened to itself); after age 3 – it comforts or disengages.
- For a child to learn to comfort, reprimands must include showing the harm done to another person; imitation of others' behaviour is also important.
- Emotions are shaped by the attunement between mother and child – this happens without words.
- Attunement differs from imitation: one must re-enact the feeling in a different way. It emerges around the 8th month of life, when the child becomes aware of being a separate being and that others share its feelings.
- Empathy requires calm.
- Psychopaths feel neither anxiety nor fear nor empathy for their victims.

## 8. The art of getting along

- The ability to manage other people's emotions is the essence of forming and maintaining social relationships.
- The art of getting along requires self-control and empathy.
- Rules for expressing feelings depend on culture: minimisation (Japan), exaggeration, or substitution (e.g. in Asia, making false promises instead of saying "no").
- Emotions are contagious – we often unconsciously mirror the emotions of others; synchronisation and engagement are key.
- A person who is liked is one whose emotional skills make others feel good in their presence.
- An emotionally skilled person can attune to others' moods and impose their own mood.
- Components of interpersonal intelligence: organising groups, negotiating solutions, forming personal relationships, conducting social analyses.
- Without understanding one's own feelings, a person becomes merely a chameleon.
- Two basic mistakes when joining a new group: attempting to take the lead and failing to adapt to the situational dynamics.

## III. Applied emotional intelligence

### 9. Intimate enemies

- In marital problems: she wants engagement and conversation about feelings, he wants to withdraw.
- Gender differences: girls receive more information about emotions, boys mainly about anger.
- Girls play in small, cooperation-oriented groups and stop playing when someone gets hurt; relationships and connections matter.
- Boys play in large, competition-oriented groups and do not stop playing when someone gets hurt; independence and autonomy matter.
- The key to a marriage surviving is working out how to express disagreement and discuss painful issues.
- One should speak freely, not harshly, about what bothers them; attacking a person rather than their actions is a mistake.
- Personal criticism, contempt, and disgust increase the risk of breakdown; a man's wall of silence destroys the bond.
- Women should criticise behaviour, not the person, and avoid contempt.
- Men should not avoid conflict or cut off discussion by offering practical solutions – women want conversation and respect for their feelings.
- Recommendations: stick to the topic, show empathy, reduce tension, take breaks during conversation.
- The XYZ method: "When you did X, I felt Y, and I would prefer you to do Z."

## 10. Managing with heart

- Leadership is not dominance but the art of persuading others to achieve a common goal.
- Key elements of leadership: constructive criticism (praise, do not postpone), supporting diversity, establishing interpersonal connections.
- The art of criticism: be specific (in both criticism and praise), propose a solution, be present (ideally one-on-one), be sensitive.
- Receiving criticism: do not treat it as a personal attack, do not adopt a defensive posture, treat it as guidance for working together.
- Prejudices arise in childhood and the beliefs that justify them appear later; we easily remember confirming examples and ignore disconfirming ones.
- Training should be conducted and discrimination condemned.
- Group work is more important than ever due to specialisation.
- In a group, the collective IQ matters less; emotional intelligence quotient matters more; overly enthusiastic or disengaged people are obstacles; harmonious cooperation is key.
- Each of us has a network of conversation partners, a network of experts, and a network of trust.

## 11. Mind and medicine

- Our sense of well-being rests on the illusory feeling that nothing can happen to us.
- The threshold of medical effectiveness can be raised by taking care of one's emotional state.
- The immune system is the "brain of the body": it recognises what belongs to it and what does not.
- Hormones limit the activity of immune cells.
- The emotion most harmful to the heart is anger.
- Anger should neither be suppressed nor fuelled; what matters more is whether it persists constantly rather than whether we express it; the antidote to anger is a trusting heart.
- Anxiety makes it easier to catch viruses; it is especially dangerous for women.
- Depression increases the risk of illness.

## IV. Windows of opportunity

### 12. The family crucible

- The way parents treat a child has consequences for the child's emotional life.
- Parental mistakes: dismissing a child's feelings, excessive indulgence, contempt for a child's feelings.
- Parents should teach children to recognise feelings, manage them, and show empathy.
- Qualities children should develop: self-confidence, curiosity, intentionality, self-control, sociability, communication skills, and the ability to cooperate.
- "Basic trust" is important; neglect has a more devastating impact than outright brutal behaviour.
- The first 3–4 years of life are especially important.
- Aggressive parents raise aggressive children who in turn become aggressive parents.
- Abused children do not show compassion.

## 13. Trauma and emotional relearning

- Post-traumatic stress is a dangerous lowering of the alarm threshold.
- Helplessness is key: stress caused by factors beyond our control leaves people unable to learn to react differently.
- Adults are paralysed by trauma; children re-enact the traumatic situation.
- Stages of healing: gaining a sense of safety, recalling the situation, grieving the loss, returning to normality.
- In a patient, periods of reliving the trauma in detail alternate with periods of amnesia.
- Once the amygdala has learned something, it never forgets; therapy teaches the cortex how to control it.

## 14. Temperament is not destiny

- Temperament is our innate disposition; four types are distinguished: shy, bold, cheerful, melancholic.
- Shyness probably arises from a genetically easier excitability of the amygdala.
- Genetic predispositions can be suppressed through upbringing: exposing children to difficulties helps overcome shyness; setting clear boundaries and firmly forbidding dangerous things are important.
- The self-control centre develops until the age of 16–17.
- Our behaviour is shaped up to the age of 17; after that it is hard to change, but it is possible.
- We cannot shed behaviours learned in childhood, but we can learn to control them.
- Post-traumatic stress is a disruption of the brain's functioning; therapy is the repair of the brain.

## V. Emotional literacy

### 15. The costs of emotional illiteracy

- Children in depression do not learn social interactions and have much to catch up on later.
- It appears that children can be psychologically vaccinated against depression.
- Eating disorders: anorexia (inability to eat) and bulimia (binge eating followed by vomiting); those affected probably cannot distinguish between anxiety, anger, and hunger.
- Outcasts at school: they either burst with anger and perceive hostility where there is none, or they are shy.
- Alcoholism serves to overcome anxiety and boredom.

## XVI. Educating emotions

- Everyone needs help observing themselves when upset.
- Self-knowledge is important.
- Teachers for emotional education must be chosen carefully – not everyone is suited.
- A virtuous life is based on self-control; self-motivation is also important.

## Appendix A. What is an emotion?

- Basic emotions: anger, sadness, fear, happiness, love, surprise, disgust, shame.
- Emotions form circles (families of related feelings).
