## 1. A deeply religious non-believer
- An atheist holds that there is nothing beyond the purely physical world, that the soul does not outlast the body, and that miracles do not exist — unexplained phenomena are simply things we have not yet figured out.
- A theist believes in a God who, after creating the world, continues to act within it.
- A deist (e.g. Voltaire, Diderot) holds that God created the world but no longer acts in it.
- A pantheist (e.g. Spinoza) rejects God — Nature takes his place.
- There is a widespread conviction that religious beliefs are a special domain deserving respect — e.g. members of one religious community are the only people in the USA legally allowed to take drugs, while others, even cancer patients, are prosecuted.

## 2. The God hypothesis
- The God hypothesis comes in many versions: animism, polytheism, monotheism (first Judaism, then Paul of Tarsus created Christianity, and Muhammad created Islam).
- A paradox: the United States, founded as a secular state, is today highly religious — possibly because religion works like a product and aggressive marketing reaches the uneducated. England, with an official church, sits at the opposite pole.
- Sagan's quote on extraterrestrial life: when asked what one feels in one's gut, the answer is — I try not to think with the door.
- Temporary agnosticism: at present there is no evidence.
- Permanent agnosticism: there is no answer regardless of how much evidence we gather (e.g. do you see red the same way I do?).
- The greatest mistake of agnosticism: treating both possibilities (God exists / does not exist) as equally probable, without considering likelihood.
- It is the theist who must provide proof — just as someone who claims a small red teapot orbits between Earth and Mars must prove it. Most people would be agnostics, but with a strong probability that the teapot does not exist.
- When asked about atheism, one can reply: you too disbelieve in Zeus, Thor, and other deities — I simply went one god further.
- Bierce's definition of prayer: petitioning for the suspension of the laws of nature on behalf of a person who admits he is unworthy of such a favour.
- The Great Prayer Experiment: three groups of patients — group I was not prayed for; group II was prayed for and knew it; group III was prayed for but did not know it. Result: no significant difference; group II had more complications, possibly due to the pressure of knowing.

## 3. Arguments for God's existence
- Arguments fall into two categories: a priori (thought experiments) and a posteriori (examining the existing world, e.g. Thomas Aquinas).
- Omnipotence and omniscience are mutually exclusive: if you know everything, you know what you will change in the future, so you cannot change your mind, which means you are not omnipotent.
- Aquinas's first argument: something must have existed at the beginning when there was nothing.
- Aquinas's second argument (from perfection): since degrees of perfection exist, there must be something most perfect.
- Aquinas's third argument (from design): things look designed, so someone must have designed them — evolutionary theory contradicts this.
- The ontological argument of St Anselm of Canterbury: if we can conceive of something, then if it exists it must be more perfect. Kant identified the slippery assumption that existence is more perfect than non-existence; Malcolm noted it is nonsensical to say a house is better if it exists than if it did not exist at all.
- The argument from beauty: beauty (Beethoven, Shakespeare) exists regardless of whether God exists.
- The argument from personal experience: it does not differ from madness; the brain processes stimuli and can play various tricks.
- The argument from scripture: the Bible contains thousands of inconsistencies.
- The argument from admired religious scholars: there is a strong negative correlation between education and religiosity.
- Pascal's Wager assumes it is better to believe than not, but also assumes either that one can talk oneself into faith or that one can deceive God.
- The argument from Bayes's theorem: the calculated probability rests on the author's private values and assessments.

## 4. Why there almost certainly is no God
- Astronomy makes us aware of our smallness; geology and Darwinism — of the vastness of time.
- Three possibilities for the origin of complex organisms: chance (highly improbable), a designer (but who designed the designer?), natural selection (works on a "warmer–colder" principle).
- The worship of gaps: areas where science has gaps are by definition handed over to God.
- Earth must meet many conditions (the Goldilocks zone, a not-too-elliptical orbit, protection by Jupiter, stabilisation by the Moon, the Sun not belonging to a binary star system) for life to be possible.
- Life arose only once; evolution is a continuous process.
- Three highly improbable events: the origin of life, the origin of the eukaryotic cell, and the origin of consciousness.
- The strength of nuclear interactions is 0.007. If it were 0.006, only hydrogen would exist; if 0.008, only heavy elements would exist and there would be no water.
- Universes may exist in parallel, one after another, or may reproduce through black holes.

## 5. The roots of religion
- Religion provides comfort and consolation, binds groups together, and satisfies the desire to know the purpose of human existence.
- Natural selection is ruthless utilitarianism (even the peacock's tail). Religion consumes time and resources.
- Direct benefits of religion: faith calms (like alcohol) and benefits the upper classes.
- Group selection: a tribe that believes martyrdom leads to paradise fights more bravely, but the less heroic warrior survives, and that tendency grows from generation to generation.
- Religion as a by-product: e.g. a moth dying in a flame is a by-product of navigating by the moon.
- The childhood hypothesis: children learn through unconditional obedience to elders (beneficial for survival), but when they learn something wrong (e.g. religion) they carry it into adulthood and pass it on.
- Humans are natural dualists (separating body and mind) and natural creationists (everything must have a purpose).
- Humans analyse reality in three ways: physical (how exactly does it work), design stance (what it does and how to use it, without knowing the mechanism), intentional stance (what are its intentions, without knowing the mechanism — useful, but leads to errors such as attributing intentions to weather or stones).
- Religion as a by-product of falling in love and of "constructive irrationality" (sometimes irrational behaviour is safer, e.g. caution while hunting).
- Genetic drift: something changes by chance, not because it confers an evolutionary advantage (analogy: Romance languages from Latin).
- Natural selection favours more faithfully replicating objects (memes).
- Cargo cults: islanders began to worship cargo, imitating white men shuffling papers. Conclusions: cults arise rapidly, their origins are quickly forgotten, the independent emergence of similar cults suggests a specific component of our psyche; there were probably many such cults and only the strongest survived.

## 6. The roots of morality: why are people good?
- Natural selection is the complete opposite of chance and has a purpose.
- The gene is selfish, but it is in its interest for the organism to be sometimes selfish and sometimes altruistic.
- Kin altruism: we are kind to genetic relatives because our genes are there too.
- Reciprocal altruism: one good turn deserves another; strategies include "always be nasty" and "tit for tat".
- Altruism for good reputation and altruism as advertising (you show that you are better).
- Ancient times and small groups shaped both altruistic and xenophobic behaviours in us.
- Hauser demonstrated that people make similar moral decisions but are usually unable to justify them.
- The trolley experiment: Kant explained that we have a built-in conviction that a rational being may never be used as a means to an end, even a higher one.
- Conclusion: no difference in moral behaviour between believers and non-believers has been demonstrated.
- When a believer asks why one should be good without God, one should ask whether he does good only because someone is watching — that is not goodness, it is calculation.
- In red states (religious) the crime rate is 62%; in blue states (non-religious) it is 38%.

## 7. "The Good Book" and the changing spirit of the times

- A holy book can be a source of morality through rules or examples.

**The Old Testament**
- There must be some criterion by which we choose what is good in the Bible and what is not – therefore the Bible is not a source of morality.
- God spared Noah.
- God spared Lot from Sodom and Gomorrah.
- Abraham presented his wife as his sister and gave her to Pharaoh, and then tried to sacrifice his son.

**Is the New Testament better?**
- Jesus was the first to depart from strict observance of the Bible.
- The problem of atonement for original sin: if Adam and Eve are symbols, where does the sin come from? And why could God not simply forgive sins?

**Love thy neighbour**
- Many examples suggest that the commandment "Thou shalt not kill" applied only to Jews.
- Religion divides through labelling children, segregating children, and prohibiting mixed marriages.

**Zeitgeist and morality**
- Zeitgeist means the Spirit of the Age – something that was once moral no longer is, e.g. slavery.

**What about Hitler and Stalin?**
- The assumption that they did evil because they were atheists is false – one might just as well claim they did evil because they had moustaches.
- It is not certain whether Hitler was an atheist; most likely he was not.
- Seneca the Younger: religion is regarded by the common people as true, by the wise as false, and by rulers as useful.
- Causes of wars: greed, political ambition, ethnic and racial prejudice, hatred or revenge, belief in destiny, belief in religion.

## 8. What's wrong with religion? Why such hostility?

**Fundamentalism and the undermining of science**
- A fundamentalist believes something is true because it follows from a holy book, not from reasoning.

**The dark side of absolutism**
- Absolutism stemming from religion causes people not to follow the Spirit of the Age.
- Example: the death penalty for blasphemy in Pakistan or Saudi Arabia.

**Religion and homosexuality**
- Alan Turing, who cracked the Enigma code, committed suicide because he faced either imprisonment or compulsory hormone treatment.

**The great Beethoven fallacy**
- The argument "if the embryo had been removed, there would be no Beethoven" applies equally to Hitler or Stalin.

**How moderate faith fuels fanaticism**
- Voltaire: those who can make you believe absurdities can also make you commit atrocities.
- Faith does not automatically deserve respect – otherwise we are supporting Osama bin Laden and terrorists.
- In the Quran, later verses override earlier ones; the earlier ones, written in Mecca, speak of peace, while the later ones, written in Medina, speak of war – so Islam was a religion of peace for only 13 years.
- Moderate religion paves the way for fanaticism.
- Teaching children that faith is a virtue is harmful.

## 9. Childhood, abuse and escape from religion

- Victor Hugo: in every village there is a torch – the teacher, and an extinguisher – the priest.
- The abduction of Edgardo Mortara: in 19th-century Italy, the papal police abducted a child and took him to a monastery because he had been secretly baptised by a Catholic nanny.

**Physical and mental abuse**
- Instilling fear of hellfire in children.

**In defence of children**
- Freedom of speech is sacred, except when it comes to the moral and religious education of children.

**The educational scandal**
- In the United Kingdom, a local businessman contributes 10% of the funds while the government provides 90% – and yet that businessman has influence over the school. This is how faith schools came about.

**On raising awareness again**
- Do not say "a Christian child", but "a child of Christian parents".

**Religious education as part of cultural heritage**
- The Bible can be read as a literary work.

## 9. The necessary gap

- It is said that the Bible fills some gap – but perhaps that gap would be filled by something else, something better?

**The inner voice**
- A voice in children's heads that persists into adulthood.

**Comfort and consolation**
- Maturity means giving life as much meaning as we ourselves choose.
- Infantility: placing responsibility for our own happiness on someone else, e.g. God.

**Inspiration**
- Our brains are built to operate on the savannah – dealing with small objects moving at low speeds; other things are hard for us to imagine.
- We do not see reality itself, but a model of it – and that model differs across animals: humans, moles, birds.
