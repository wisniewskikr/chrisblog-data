# Content

## Introduction

- Change is rapid: long periods of sameness interrupted by sudden shifts.
- Persuasion is changing someone's mind without coercion.
- The key question before attempting persuasion: why do I want to bring about a change of mind?

## 1. Post-truth

- Truthers are seekers of truth who disagree with the official account of 9/11.
- The information deficit model assumes that someone thinks differently because they lack knowledge – access to it should eliminate the difference.
- Post-truth: facts persuade no one.

## 2. Deep canvassing

- Deep canvassing can change a person's mind in about 20 minutes.
- Radical hospitality: creating an atmosphere reminiscent of a family gathering.
- Important questions: where the interlocutor first heard about the issue (sermons, family, etc.) and whether they know someone directly affected by it.
- Facts don't work – the interlocutor must talk through the topic themselves and change their own way of thinking; the tools are questioning and listening.
- The goal: to get the interlocutor to reflect on their own thinking.
- The candle metaphor: trust is the candle; the thin layer of wax is our story, the thick layer is theirs.
- Questions are keys on a keyring – one of them may unlock the interlocutor's personal story.
- Battling over the interpretation of facts doesn't work; you need to reach the source of the opinion so the interlocutor can consider whether anything has changed.
- When interlocutors explain themselves, they supply their own counterarguments.
- Effectiveness: a change of mind in about 1 in 10 people.
- Connection to System 1 and System 2: people default to System 1; only a request to put themselves in someone else's shoes activates System 2.

## 3. Socks and crocs

- The dress dispute: a mother photographed a dress before her sister's wedding and sent the photo; her daughter and fiancé disagreed about the colours; friends asked to weigh in also disagreed – the photo went viral.
- Our experience of reality is not a perfect reflection of the world.
- Every animal perceives only a slice of reality – for example, a tick cannot hear but can detect lactic acid.
- The brain works like a general in a bunker over a scale model: scouts bring information and the general moves troops; bad information means an outdated model.
- The brain automatically compensates for lighting conditions and reinterprets colours.
- When the truth is uncertain, the brain constructs the most probable reality based on existing knowledge.
- Stages of learning: description → explanation → prediction → creation.
- People have different prior probabilities.

## 4. Imbalance

- The brain processes in sequence: shapes, sounds and colours, then language, then writing.
- The brain interacts with the world through the senses or with itself through thought.
- Chemical reactions and electrical activity alter the arrangement of molecules and atoms in the brain.
- The brain burns traces into neurons to reflect patterns, then changes behaviour based on those changes.
- The tool is dopamine: an increase provides motivation, a decrease removes it.
- When reality matches expectations, dopamine levels remain stable; when it exceeds them – they rise; when it falls short – they drop.
- The brain continuously updates its model when it encounters something unfamiliar or interesting.
- Levels of processing: at the bottom, sensory impressions (shapes, colours, sounds); in the middle, concrete constructs (e.g. cockroaches and stones); at the top, abstractions (e.g. humility and floods).
- Two cognitive processes: assimilation (integrating new information into existing structures) and accommodation (changing or building new structures); the shift to accommodation occurs after cognitive dissonance, at around 30% inconsistency.
- Truth and belief are two different things.
- Epistemology is the study of knowledge itself and the determination of what is true.
- Types of knowledge: declarative ("that" – e.g. pudding exists) and procedural ("how" – e.g. how to change a tyre).
- Science is an empirical epistemological method: hypotheses → theories → models → experiments.
- A perceptual crisis leads to a shock of realisation.
- The assumptive world is usually inherited within a culture.

## 5. Westboro

- Westboro is a hate group operating in America.
- One member left the movement because his back hurt and the group refused to let him see a doctor.
- Children's negative emotional states end sooner if they are allowed to pass through them.

## 6. Truth is tribal

- Conspiracy theories appeal to the ego: the believer feels they belong to an elite.
- When controversial views are challenged, the body enters fight-or-flight mode; with neutral topics, it does not.
- The brain's primary task is protection – physical and mental.
- Groups form without a shared founding characteristic, and hostility toward other groups follows.
- We are ultra-social: willing to sacrifice ourselves and others for the group.
- Social death is more terrifying than physical death.
- For scientists, openness to change is part of their group identity and a drive toward precision.
- Resistance to facts is rational when social security is absent.
- Reason is a slave to emotions.

## 7. Discussions

- When we are motivated to accept a theory, we seek only confirmation, not refutation.
- A balance between trust and action is necessary.
- Group discussion helps bypass the bottleneck of trust when new information is too radical.
- We cannot decompose our own justifications on our own.
- Our reasoning is one-sided and lazy – which is why we need discussion and groups.

## 8. Persuasion

- Beliefs are statements we hold to be true.
- An attitude is a spectrum of feelings.
- Beliefs and attitudes together form our values.
- Communication model: who? what? to whom? through what channel? with what result?
- The message and the context matter – the same information can be received differently by different people.
- Other factors of effectiveness: motivation and the ability to absorb information.
- Central route: a motivated person and strong arguments; peripheral route: an unmotivated person, weak arguments, quantity matters more than quality.

## 9. Street epistemology

- Conversation framework: claim → reasons → method of evaluating reasons; then focus on the method itself.
- The goal: to live in a world where people believe what is true.
- Do not ask who is right, but why we perceive the issue differently.

## 10. Social change

- The Ice Age drove human development.
- Culture shaped genes, and genes shaped culture.
- Contact changes minds, but only on equal terms.
- We are experiencing a crisis not of post-truth but of post-trust.
- The network effect: innovator → early adopter → mainstream.
- We are what we know.
- It is not genius but persistence and a little luck that change minds.
