# Content

## Prolog
- Serf peasants were slaves.
- Originally, the word "chłop" (peasant) meant a slave.
- Earlier, peasants were referred to as "śmiecie" (trash/refuse).
- The nobility made up around 6% of Poland's population, peasants around 70%.

## I. Degradation
- In the Middle Ages, peasants were required to work for their lord only a few or a dozen or so days per year.
- Already in the Middle Ages, lords refused to allow peasants to leave.
- Peasants worked the most on church estates.
- Poland did not escape the plague, though it claimed fewer lives there than elsewhere.
- The influx of gold from the Americas raised grain prices.
- Production for export began, leading to the maximisation of efficiency and the expansion of serfdom.
- 60% of land belonged to the nobility.
- Peasant farms steadily shrank.

## II. Brutalisation
- In Poland, the 16th century was a period of prosperity, while the 17th was the worst.
- About one third of the German-speaking population died in the Thirty Years' War.
- Until 1630, the grain market in Poland remained favourable.
- The Swedish Deluge thoroughly plundered Poland.
- Epidemics also struck the country.
- Magnates grew ever wealthier.
- Peasants had less and less land, and a lack of tools made it impossible to cultivate.
- The institution of the so-called załoga emerged: the lord lent peasants draught animals.

## III. Exploitation
- The serf labour obligation was initially tied to the size of land used — a set number of days was worked off.
- Peasants worked for the lord on fine days and for themselves on bad-weather days.
- On feast days peasants usually did not work, but those days were not cancelled — they were carried over, so a peasant was perpetually indebted to the landowner for many working days.
- There were so-called gwałty — additional unpaid days of serf labour that were not counted towards the regular obligation.
- Szarwark — a service obligation involving the upkeep of roads and bridges.
- Stróża — a service obligation involving night-time guarding of the village.
- Podwody — a service obligation involving transporting the nobleman's goods.

## IV. Plunder
- Peasants were required to pay rents of 500–750 złoty.
- In-kind tributes were also obligatory.
- The Church was not a defender of peasants but an equally oppressive exploiter — it collected the tithe.
- Peasants were required to attend church and to pay for masses.
- Peasants were the only social group that paid taxes.
- Taxes were collected by the nobleman and spent on his own needs.
- Between 70 and 90% of peasants' income was taken from them.

## V. The law of the whip
- Noblemen beat peasants without restraint.
- One punishment was the gąsior — a type of stocks.
- Peasant women were raped and had no protection against it.
- Patrimonial jurisdiction meant that the nobleman was simultaneously judge, prosecutor, and executioner.
- No prisons were built in Poland, as lords did not want to lose their labour force.
- The nobility held the power of life and death over peasants.
- Peasants very often beat their own families, as it was the only outlet available to them; they also fought among themselves.

## VI. Absolute surveillance
- The nobility exercised absolute control over their peasants.
- Peasants were made to police themselves — this role was fulfilled by the village mayor and other village representatives.
- Villages appointed so-called rugownicy (informers) who spied on their neighbours.
- Dziesiątnicy (tithing men) were also frequently appointed, responsible for the discipline and honesty of ten families.
- The nobility forced widows and widowers to remarry; running a farm independently was not permitted.
- The greed of noblemen led to the ruin of peasant farms.
- Some churches ran schools where reading and writing were taught.

## VII. The tavern
- A village might have no church, but it had to have a tavern.
- Taverns were often leased for such high sums that lessees were forced to cheat peasants.
- One in four innkeepers was Jewish.
- Goods were also sold in the tavern — mainly nails and salt. Salt was used for preserving food and was essential for surviving the winter.
- The nobility pressured the king into selling salt at reduced prices, then resold it to peasants at many times the cost.
- Noblemen charged fees for beer and forbade peasants from drinking at other taverns.
- When grain prices fell, vodka began to be produced from grain, and peasants bought it from the nobility at the tavern.

## VIII. The price of a person
- The Constitution of 3 May did not abolish serfdom.
- Peasants did not take part in the November Uprising, the January Uprising, or the Kościuszko Insurrection.
- A male peasant could be bought for 20,000 złoty; female peasants were valued even lower.
- According to the nobility, peasants descended from Ham — the biblical son of Noah.
- There was also a theory of a Sarmatian conquest of Polish lands, according to which the nobility and peasants were two separate races.
- During conflicts between noblemen, peasants were killed as the property of the opposing lord.

## IX. The apex of misery
- Peasant cottages were built from clay and straw.
- Peasants often lived in a single room together with their animals.

## X. Resistance
- Peasants resisted primarily by fleeing.
- Noblemen punished escapes, yet they themselves stole peasants from one another.
- Peasants also staged uprisings.
- The quality of peasants' work was very low — they deliberately put in minimal effort.
