# Content

## 1. Ada, Countess Lovelace

- Ada Byron was the only legitimate child of the poet George Byron.
- She combined a love of poetry with a passion for mathematics.
- She saw Babbage's difference engine.
- George Byron married for practical reasons, to pay off his debts; he had many affairs, including with his half-sister; he died of a fever while trying to support the Greeks in their fight against the Turks.
- Ada married William King, Earl of Lovelace.
- Successive machines expanded the scope of calculation: Pascal (addition and subtraction), Leibniz (additionally multiplication and division), Babbage (sines and cosines).
- While translating Babbage's text, Ada wrote "Translator's Notes", which contained the first published computer program.

## 2. The computer

- Hollerith used punched cards for a voter census and later founded IBM.
- Bush built an analogue electromechanical computer.
- Characteristics of computers: digital, binary, electronic (valves, transistors, integrated circuits), general-purpose.
- Turing invented the Turing machine.
- Boole's Boolean algebra: values 0 and 1 and the operations "AND", "OR".
- Shannon proposed combining multiple logic gates (AND gate, OR gate, etc.) to perform operations.
- Stibitz built a complex-number calculator using valves.
- Howard Aiken took over the Mark I device.
- Konrad Zuse built a calculating machine in Berlin.
- Atanasoff independently designed a machine in Iowa for solving linear equations.
- Mauchly also worked on a calculating machine and met Atanasoff.
- Mauchly and Eckert created the ENIAC computer, which calculated artillery shell trajectories.
- Colossus was built at Bletchley Park in England — the world's first fully electronic, partially programmable computer.

## 3. Programming

- Grace Hopper wrote the world's first programming manual for the Mark I.
- The word "bug" comes from a moth that flew into the Mark II.
- The successor to ENIAC was EDVAC, featuring von Neumann architecture, operating in binary format and equipped with mercury memory.

## 4. The transistor

- The transistor amplifies electric current and controls its flow; it is to the digital revolution what the steam engine was to the industrial revolution.
- Invented in 1948 at Murray Hill, Bell Labs, by Brattain, Bardeen, and Shockley (a theorist and an experimenter).
- Types of electrical conductors: conductors (e.g. copper), insulators (e.g. sulphur), semiconductors (e.g. silicon — adding a dopant such as boron turns them into conductors).
- Transistors were used in Regency radio receivers.

## 5. The integrated circuit

- The integrated circuit (microchip) was invented independently in two places: Texas Instruments (Jack Kilby) and Fairchild Semiconductor (Noyce).
- A single piece of semiconductor that, through different dopant mixtures, performs various functions: resistor, capacitor, distributed capacitance, and transistor.
- Applications: ballistic missiles, the Apollo programme, hearing aids, and calculators.
- Moore's Law: the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles every year.
- Rock invented the joint venture; he invested in Intel, among others.
- Intel leadership: Noyce (external affairs), Moore (internal affairs), Grove (man of action).
- The microprocessor — many integrated circuits capable of performing universal tasks — was invented at Intel by Ted Hoff.

## 6. Computer games

- MIT had an electronic model railway club; the word "hacker" (someone who plays hacks, i.e. pranks) also originated there.
- The game Spacewar was created by Russell.
- Bushnell created the "Computer Space" arcade machine based on Spacewar and founded Atari; he hired Al Alcorn, who created the game Pong.

## 7. The Internet

- Vannevar Bush led American science during World War II; he advocated cooperation between the military, universities, and private companies, and supported funding for basic research.
- Licklider is considered the father of the Internet; he championed decentralised networks and new interfaces.
- Two models of computer interaction: batch processing (like correspondence by letter) and interactive processing (like a conversation).
- The Internet was born at ARPA to connect academic communities; Taylor was the director, Roberts was the engineer; ARPANET was created.
- Concept of the router: an additional minicomputer responsible for the connection; data transmitted in packets.
- Four sites were connected: UCLA, Stanford, Utah, and the University of California.
- The first connection was made in 1969.
- In 1973, Kahn and Cerf interconnected various existing networks into the Internet; they developed the common Internet Protocol (IP) and TCP (controlling packet order and completeness).

## 8. Personal computers

- Doug Engelbart invented the mouse.
- Xerox PARC had a single purpose: conducting research.
- In 1973, the Xerox Alto computer was created, based on a bitmap (each pixel had one bit of main memory).
- Ed Roberts created the Altair — a personal computer sold as a kit.

## 9. Software

- Paul Allen and Bill Gates met in Seattle; both were passionate about computers.
- Gates's family: father — lawyer, mother — community activist, two sisters.
- Gates played tennis and water-skied and was a scout; he attended a private school that had a Mark II computer terminal.
- Allen attended the private Lakeside School and was two years older than Bill.
- Together they founded the Lakeside Programmers Group.
- Evans died in a climbing accident.
- In his final year of school, Bill worked for an electromagnetic network.
- Gates went to Harvard.
- He and Allen wrote a BASIC interpreter for the Altair.
- Gates dropped out of Harvard and moved to Albuquerque, where the Altair was manufactured; they signed a licensing agreement and founded Microsoft, which later moved to Seattle.
- Wozniak and Jobs met through a mutual friend; they created the Blue Box — a device for making free phone calls.
- After returning from India, Jobs worked at Atari; Wozniak designed calculators by day and worked on Apple I by night; together they created the Apple II.
- Dan Bricklin created VisiCalc — a spreadsheet that became a springboard for the Apple II.
- Microsoft bought QDOS from Paterson for $50,000 and sold the licence rights to IBM.
- Jobs acquired technologies from Xerox PARC: Ethernet (local area network), object-oriented programming, and a desktop GUI (bitmap).
- The Lisa and Macintosh computers were created; Microsoft created Windows.
- Stallman created the GNU system.
- Linus Torvalds, a Finnish student at the University of Helsinki, created Linux — the kernel for GNU.

## 10. Online

- In 1972, Tomlinson invented e-mail.
- Modems modulate and demodulate analogue signals into digital ones; for a long time this was blocked by the monopolist AT&T.
- WELL was created — a forum where users were not anonymous.
- America Online (AOL) was created.
- In 1993, the Internet became public, largely thanks to Al Gore.

## 11. The WWW

- Tim Berners-Lee invented the WWW; after Oxford he joined CERN.
- He created the Enquire programme for communication between departments, using hyperlinks.
- He invented URL, HTTP, and HTML; he collaborated with Cailliau; the WWW was made public in 1991.
- The Mosaic browser was created.
- Justin Hall launched the first blog with personal information.
- Yang and Filo founded Yahoo! — a search engine where bots assist and humans verify at the end.
- Williams created the Blogger programme.
- Wales founded Wikipedia.
- Google was created by Larry Page and Sergey Brin — PhD students at Stanford; the name comes from the number googol (1 followed by 100 zeros), because the search engine processes an enormous number of links.

## 12. Viva Ada!

- The singularity: the moment when computers become so intelligent that they can program superintelligent machines themselves.
- Creativity is rooted in collaboration.
- The most effective teams have a wide range of skills.
- The best results come from combining the sciences and the humanities.
