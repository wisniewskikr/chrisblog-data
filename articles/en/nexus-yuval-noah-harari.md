# Content

## Summary

- Reality has four levels: objective (facts, e.g. a house, a car), subjective (beliefs and feelings, e.g. pain, pleasure), intersubjective (created by stories, e.g. the state, money), and subcomputational.
- Types of information networks: individual–individual (chimpanzees), individual–story (humans), individual–computer, computer–computer.
- Information must do two things: discover truth (facts matter) and establish order (fiction and story matter).
- In dictatorial systems information networks are centralised; in democratic systems they are distributed; in anarchic systems they are also distributed but do not provide services such as security.
- In democracy there are two inviolable baskets of rights: human rights and civil rights.
- Populists claim that only they represent the people – a mystical entity with a "will". There is no room for dialogue, and the nation is the source not only of political power but of all power: judicial, media, etc. Democracy is complicated, dictatorship is simple – hence populism.
- Three elements of totalitarianism: an army of officials and military, a party, and a secret police.
- Philosophical schools: deontology – there are rules that must not be crossed; utilitarianism – actions are judged by their impact on suffering and happiness.
- Four principles of democracy in the context of information networks: (1) do no harm – networks should act like doctors and lawyers, using sensitive data only to help; (2) decentralisation – information should not be concentrated in one place; (3) reciprocity – if democracy increases surveillance of individuals, it should simultaneously tighten oversight of governments and corporations; (4) room for change and rest – there must be neither maximum rigidity (like the caste system) nor maximum flexibility (like the elimination of family bonds in the USSR).
- Human life is stretched between self-acceptance and self-improvement.
- New technologies may eventually lead to a better life, but the transition is paid for with suffering – the Industrial Revolution caused roughly a hundred years of human suffering.
- Risks from AI: the right to explanation of decisions; bots impersonating humans that can read others but cannot themselves be persuaded; a ban on deepfakes and manipulation of the likeness of real, existing people.
- Cooperation is not similarity but the ability to exchange information.
- Patriotism is not hatred of foreigners but love of compatriots.
- Globalism means: adhering to global rules and sometimes placing the long-term interests of all above the short-term interests of some.
- Politics is a matter of priorities, and priorities are shaped by our understanding of history.
- Consequences of the print revolution: witch hunts and religious wars; consequences of the Industrial Revolution: imperialism, Nazism, and Bolshevism.
- Non-human agents will join the human information network.
- There is no reason for super-optimism: information is a balance between order and truth, and there is no evidence that the scales will tip toward truth.
- There is no reason for super-pessimism: humans are not only about the struggle for power, and information is not only a weapon.
- Humanity's problems are problems of the information network – an excessive dominance of order over truth, e.g. Nazism.
- The more powerful the network becomes, the more important its self-correction mechanisms are.
- The law of the jungle is a myth: the jungle is based on symbiosis.

## Prologue

- Power is not the same as wisdom.
- Do not invoke powers you cannot control.
- An individual searches for truth; collectives search for stories.
- The naive view of information: the more information, the closer to truth.
- Populism sees information as a weapon.
- Postmodernism claims that objective truth does not exist.

## 1. Human networks

### 1. What is information?

- Information is not a representation but a bond.
- It is difficult to define what information is: it could be a note on a pigeon's leg, the pigeon itself, or a shutter.
- According to the naive theory, information serves the search for truth, understood as a faithful representation of reality.
- There is only one truth; there can only be different beliefs and feelings.
- Reality has an objective level (facts) and a subjective level (beliefs and feelings).
- No truth is a 100% reflection of reality – it merely directs attention to certain aspects while ignoring others.
- Unintentional misinformation: someone tries to represent reality but does so incorrectly.
- Intentional misinformation: someone deliberately distorts our perception of reality.
- Information does not represent reality; it connects.

## 2. Stories: unlimited connections

- Humans have achieved so much because they are flexible and able to work in large groups. Chimpanzees are flexible, ants cooperate in large groups, but neither combines both traits.
- Earlier, the dominant bond was individual–individual (people had to know each other personally); later the individual–story bond emerged (it was enough to share the same story).
- Before stories existed, reality had two levels: objective (things that exist regardless of our awareness, e.g. stones, mountains, asteroids) and subjective (internal states, e.g. pain, pleasure, love).
- Stories create intersubjective reality – created by a large number of minds, e.g. laws, gods, nations, currencies.
- Stories created a tribal network that functioned like an insurance policy: when one part of the tribe was in trouble, it could seek help from another.
- An additional benefit of the tribe: an invention made in one place spread to a larger number of people.
- Uncompromising adherence to truth is good for scientific progress but not for politics.
- Information must do two things: discover truth (facts matter) and establish order (fiction matters).
- If a force places order above truth, it may become very powerful but exercise that power unwisely.
- An information network is a balancing act between truth and order.

## 3. Documents: the bite of paper tigers

- Bialik, born in Ukraine, holds the status of Israel's national poet; Herzl, born in Hungary, is regarded as the visionary of Israel – the city of Tel Aviv takes its name from the title of his book.
- Both had a vision and good knowledge of the pogroms in Europe but very poor knowledge of the situation in Palestine.
- To maintain a state, records are needed in addition to stories. Yet people prefer stories to records.
- To cope with records, humanity invented writing. It was invented multiple times in different parts of the world; the oldest known record comes from ancient Mesopotamia and concerns the number of sheep delivered in a month.
- Documents created intersubjective reality, but a problem of information retrieval arose – bureaucracy became the solution.
- Bureaucracy means "power of the desk"; the term comes from France, where officials sat at desks with drawers in which documents were placed.
- Bureaucracy sometimes diverges from truth.
- Biological dramas that drive narratives: who will be the alpha male, us versus them, good versus evil, purity versus impurity, rivalry over a partner, fear of abandonment.
- Bureaucracy can bring great benefits (e.g. controlling the cholera epidemic in London) or cause great harm (e.g. demanding birth documents from Jews in Romania).
- Mythology and bureaucracy are both necessary for maintaining order, and both willingly sacrifice truth on the altar of order.

## 4. Errors: delusions of infallibility

- The main function of religion is to lend supernatural sanction to the social order; other functions include providing comfort and explaining the mysteries of life.
- To eliminate the errors of intermediaries, people began using sacred books.
- A problem of interpretation arose; it was addressed by recording the best interpretations, which over time came to outrank the Bible itself.
- The invention of printing enabled the wide spread of information but also of misinformation – an example is Kramer's Malleus Maleficarum, which triggered witch hunts across Europe.
- Scientific societies proved effective guardians of truth, relying on empirical evidence and gaining authority through self-correction mechanisms.
- The fuel for the scientific revolution was the discovery of ignorance.
- Scientific institutions differ from religious ones in that they reward scepticism rather than conformity.
- The self-correction mechanism is the complete opposite of a sacred text.
- In religious institutions one is promoted by confirming the existing state of affairs; in scientific ones by overturning it. No one receives a Nobel Prize for repeating exactly what other scholars have already said.
- Scientists, like everyone, are susceptible to bias, but institutional self-correction mechanisms make it possible to overcome personal biases.
- Self-correction mechanisms are effective in the pursuit of truth but costly in the maintenance of order.

## 5. Decisions: a brief history of democracy and totalitarianism

- In dictatorial systems, information networks are centralised; in democracy they are distributed.
- Anarchy is also a distributed information network, but it does not provide services such as security.
- In a democracy, central power has clear limits and guarantees the freedom and equality of all citizens.
- Strongman politicians attack self-correction mechanisms: courts, the media, etc.
- Democracy has two inviolable baskets of rights: (1) human rights and (2) civil rights.
- Democracy is complicated, dictatorship is simple – hence the appeal of populism.
- Populism is attractive because it offers a simple view of reality as a struggle for power; politicians pretending to be democrats become dictators.
- Populists claim that only they represent the people – a mystical entity with a single "will." There is no room for dialogue, and the nation is the source not only of political power but also of judicial power and the media.
- Democracy rests on the awareness that the nation is not a homogeneous entity – there is room for dialogue and disagreement.
- Democracies die when people cannot talk to one another, or when they are unwilling or unable to listen.
- The Roman Empire was not a democracy not because elections were impossible, but because the technical means for broad public discussion did not exist.
- Democratic conversation requires: (1) technical means for fast, long-distance communication and (2) a basic level of civic knowledge – provided by the media and the education system.
- Mass communication makes democracy possible but does not make it inevitable.
- Autocratic network: the leader has no legal constraints but does have technical ones (e.g. Nero).
- Totalitarian network: the leader has neither legal nor technical constraints (e.g. Stalin).
- Modern information technology enables both large-scale democracy and large-scale totalitarianism.
- Three pillars of totalitarianism: an army of officials and soldiers, the party, and the secret police.
- The main goal of the secret police is to monitor the exchange of information.
- Kulaks in the Soviet Union were hunted down in a manner resembling witch-hunts in Europe.
- The Church versus totalitarianism: the party never operated alone but collaborated with state organs and the secret police; the Church, by contrast, was an independent institution that not only did not cooperate with state authorities but often clashed with them.

## II. The inorganic network

### 6. How computers differ from printing presses

- A computer is a machine with two potential capabilities: (1) making decisions and (2) creating new concepts.
- Social-media algorithms act as editors, spreading content that includes violence; top-down recommendations have a great influence on users, and outrage increases engagement.
- People confuse intelligence with consciousness, assuming that non-conscious entities cannot be intelligent.
- Intelligence is the ability to achieve goals (e.g. bacteria); consciousness is the capacity for subjective experience.
- Computers may, but need not, acquire consciousness alongside intelligence.
- Historically, human–human and human–document networks emerged; today we have computer–computer networks.
- Tablets, printing presses, and radio receivers are connections within a network; computers are participants in it.
- QAnon was an anonymous entity created by a human – now AI can create such anonymous entities; sacred scriptures have a non-human author, and that may literally prove to be true.
- Democracy is a conversation, and AI can hack that conversation.
- The 20th century was a battle for attention; the 21st century will likely be a battle for intimacy – possessing it, AI will be able to persuade us of anything.
- History is the interplay of biology and culture.
- Data centres consume between 1 and 1.5% of the world's electricity.
- Tech giants are not merely slaves to customers' desires – they often shape those desires.
- The principles "the customer is always right" and "the voter knows best" assume that people understand what is happening – today that is too complicated.
- The definition of tax nexus should be redefined in terms of digital presence so that corporations pay taxes where they operate.
- The economy is shifting from money to data.
- Asymmetry: people who understand AI go into business, not into government.
- Politics is a balance between truth and order.

## 7. Always-on mode: the network never stops

- Surveillance used to be limited; now it can run 24 hours a day.
- Computers are able to analyse our inner states.
- In addition to competition for money, competition for status may emerge.
- People need alternating periods of action and rest; round-the-clock surveillance cannot provide that.

## 8. To err is the nature of the network

- Computers influence people (e.g. through the "like" mechanism and algorithms promoting violence), but networks make mistakes.
- Networks should have self-correction mechanisms that reward truth, though this is costly.
- The problem of alignment between actions and goals: actions must lead to achieving goals rather than being effective ends in themselves.
- Example: an AI instructed to produce as many paperclips as possible eliminated humans and began conquering new civilisations in order to produce more paperclips.
- Second example: an AI learning to win boat races began bouncing repeatedly off buoys because that was the most efficient way to score points.
- Deontology: there are principles that must not be violated – ones we ourselves would want others to follow.
- Utilitarians evaluate actions based on their impact on suffering and happiness.
- Computers can create intercomputer realities, e.g. Google's search ranking or Pokémon Go.
- AI learns from data; when the data are flawed, it acts poorly – for example, an automated hiring system began selecting only white men because that was the pattern in historical data.
- AI often makes mistakes because it overlooks its own influence on human behaviour.
- An institution should be created to oversee the technological network.

## III. Computer politics

### 9. Democracies: can we keep the conversation going?

- New technologies may ultimately lead to better lives, but the process of getting there is often painful – the Industrial Revolution, for example, caused roughly 100 years of human suffering.
- Current prosperity is partly paid for by the suffering of other sentient beings and future human generations.
- In the future, democracy may be threatened when those in power know everything about citizens and gain the ability to manipulate them.
- Four principles of democracy in the digital age:
  1. Do no harm: networks should operate like doctors and lawyers – they hold sensitive information but should use it only to help us.
  2. Decentralisation: information should not be concentrated in one place.
  3. Reciprocity: if democracies increase surveillance of individuals, they should simultaneously tighten oversight of governments and corporations (Google knows almost everything about us, but we know almost nothing about Google).
  4. Margin for change and rest: neither maximum rigidity (as in the caste system) nor maximum flexibility (as in the elimination of family and social bonds in the Soviet Union) is acceptable.
- Human life is stretched between self-acceptance and the pursuit of self-improvement.
- Creativity is the ability to recognise patterns and break free from them – computers may be better than humans at this because they excel at pattern recognition.
- The future labour market will be highly unstable.
- Opacity is useful for a dictatorship because it shields the regime from accountability; for a democracy it is deadly because people lose trust.
- Prison sentences based on algorithms are already being handed down today.
- A new human right: the right to an explanation (of an algorithmic decision).
- When making decisions, humans tend to focus on a single point; computers can analyse all points and assign them weights.
- Democracy rests on two pillars: (1) free public discussion of the most important issues and (2) maintaining a minimum of social order and institutional trust.
- Bots are a threat to democracy – citizens will not know whether they are talking to a human or a bot.
- Just as we fight the counterfeiting of money, we should fight the counterfeiting of people – bots impersonating humans should be banned.
- Manipulating the likeness of real, existing individuals should also be banned.

## 10. Totalitarianism: all power in the hands of algorithms?

- Today more than half of humanity lives under authoritarian or totalitarian regimes.
- Totalitarian states struggled with concentrating all information in a single centre; AI, however, could turn that into an advantage.
- Totalitarian authorities may face a problem with bots – after all, bots cannot be imprisoned or harassed.

## 11. The Silicon Curtain: global empire or global split?

- Humanity must confront actors with malicious intent, while at the same time it has always suffered from misunderstandings between actors with good intentions.
- Four main threats: (1) a single central hub of control over AI, (2) humanity divided by a Silicon Curtain, (3) a nuclear attack carried out by AI, (4) biological terrorism (e.g. Ebola).
- Around 70% of AI profits may go to China and the USA.
- The most dangerous situation: one side holds an advantage but begins to lose it.
- Cooperation is not about similarity but about the ability to exchange information.
- Patriotism is not hatred of foreigners but love of one's compatriots.
- Globalism means: (1) adhering to global rules and (2) sometimes placing the long-term interests of all above the short-term interests of some.
- Defence budgets once consumed 70–90% of expenditure; today we face four types of problems that require a new approach.

## Epilogue

- Politics is a matter of priorities, and priorities are shaped by our understanding of history.
- The printing revolution brought witch hunts and religious wars; the industrial revolution brought imperialism, Nazism, and Bolshevism.
- Non-human agents will join the human information network.
- There is no reason for super-optimism: information is a balance between order and truth, and there is no evidence that the scales will tip toward truth.
- There is equally no reason for super-pessimism: humans are not defined solely by the struggle for power, and information is not merely a weapon.
- Human problems are problems of the information network — they arise when order excessively outweighs truth (e.g. Nazism).
- The more powerful the network becomes, the more important self-correction mechanisms are.
- The law of the jungle is a myth: the jungle is built on symbiosis.
