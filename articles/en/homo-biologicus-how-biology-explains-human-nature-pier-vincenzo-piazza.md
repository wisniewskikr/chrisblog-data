# Content

## I. Matter

### 1. The legend of the immaterial nature of man

- Dharmic religions (Buddhism and Hinduism) assume reincarnation.
- The burden of proof lies with whoever claims something exists – existence is proved, not non-existence.
- Milestones in the evolution of life on Earth:
  - 4 billion years ago: single-celled organisms
  - 500 million years ago: multicellular organisms
  - 400 million years ago: fish
  - 300 million years ago: vertebrates
  - 200 million years ago: reptiles
  - 100 million years ago: mammals
  - 3 million years ago: first Homo
  - 150,000 years ago: Homo sapiens
  - 50,000 years ago: first art
  - 15,000 years ago: agriculture
  - 3,000 years ago: writing
- The same matter can take different forms – like water and ice in a glass.

## 2. Biology, like human nature, is extremely variable

- Our genome is not a recording but a musical instrument – it can produce an infinite number of melodies, sounding different in different environments.
- DNA is divided into:
  - coding sequence (3%) – produces proteins
  - regulatory sequence (97%) – determines which proteins are produced, when, and in what quantity
- Humans and mice share an almost identical (99%) coding sequence; the difference is made by the regulatory sequence – the "head chef".
- Emergence: the same object can have different properties depending on the environment (like water: liquid, ice, steam).
- Humans have approximately 25,000 genes.
- Environments that influence gene expression: the cell, the organ, the individual, the external environment.
- General cell structure: cell membrane, cytoplasm, cell nucleus.
- Experiment 1: DBA mice (naturally averse to drugs) began seeking them out after a period of hunger; C57 mice (naturally fond of drugs) began avoiding them under the same conditions.
- Experiment 2: the mother's environment modifies offspring susceptibility to drugs – a nurturing mother reduced susceptibility in DBA mice but had no effect on C57 mice.

## 3. Biology, like the mind, feeds on the environment

- Psychology is part of biology – drugs could not affect the psyche if it were not.
- The external environment influences the biology of the brain.
- Neuron structure: dendrites (receive information), axon (transmits information), synapses (connection points – chemical transmission via neurotransmitters).
- The brain: grey matter (neurons and dendrites) and white matter (axons).
- Physical stimuli are converted by sensory organs into biological signals, reach synapses, which produce transcription factors that act on DNA in the cell nucleus.
- Epigenetic factors can alter gene expression and be passed between generations (e.g., the influence of a nurturing mother).
- Transcription factors act like fingers playing a melody on an instrument.
- Genetic selection takes thousands of years; epigenetic selection allows faster or temporary adaptation.
- Evolution: biology proposes, the environment decides.
- Mutations arise from external factors (e.g., radiation), replication errors, or fertilisation.
- Social animals experience stress when alone; territorial animals experience stress in company.
- Dopamine is linked to the pleasure of the effort of pursuing a goal; enkephalin and anandamide are the true pleasure receptors.
- The time spent pursuing pleasure is many times longer than the time spent experiencing it.
- In stressful situations the body also releases dopamine, to encourage seeking an optimal solution rather than simply fleeing.
- Stressful events are remembered more strongly – cortisol from the adrenal glands activates transcription factors in the brain.
- Cortisol regulates the biological clock; chronic stress disrupts this regulation.
- Learning: tolerance (weakening of responses to irrelevant stimuli) and sensitisation (strengthening of responses to important stimuli).
- Homeostasis – the baseline activity level set by the environment after birth; allostasis – a new equilibrium point established after prolonged environmental change.
- People who have experienced stress are more susceptible to addiction.

## II. Aspirations

### 4. The goal of biology is freedom

- The goal of biology is freedom, but one cannot free oneself from the environment – one can only adapt to it as well as possible.
- Concepts of freedom:
  - antiquity: freedom originates within the individual, is spontaneous, and is aware of its consequences
  - Christianity: free will as the condition of human responsibility for sin
  - modernity: the two pillars are survival and pleasure
- Laws of thermodynamics: first – energy is neither created nor destroyed; second – energy and matter move towards ever greater dispersal (entropy).
- Hierarchy of resource availability: air (we die after 3 minutes), water (after 3 days), food (after 40 days) – the less accessible the resource, the more we store.
- Responses aimed at homeostasis:
  - prestasis – anticipates shortage (air)
  - endostatic – manages shortage in real time (water, food)
  - exostatic – prevents future shortage by accumulating reserves motivated by pleasure (food)
- Hedonic experiences:
  - endostatic (happiness) – restoring balance; we aim to maintain the state with minimal effort
  - exostatic (pleasure) – exceeding balance; we aim to repeat it as often as possible
- About 50,000 years ago humans detached hedonism from its biological function and learned to induce it artificially.

## 5. Biology creates two types of being in the world

- Two poles of personality: spiritualism and materialism.
- Two spheres of action: conservatism and progressivism.
- The spiritualist and conservative relies on the endostatic system; functions better in times of stability.
- The materialist and progressive relies on the exostatic system; functions better in times of uncertainty.
- Evolution equipped us with a mechanism for the attractiveness of novelty – after a while novelty ceases to be interesting, motivating further searching.
- Possible ecological solutions: nuclear fusion (ITER reactor, tritium isotope), increasing energy efficiency (current ~16%, electric motors ~95%), genetic engineering.

## 6. Biology gives life meaning

- Three human aspirations: freedom, development, meaning of life.
- Life is a state of organic matter that causes greater entropy growth in its surroundings than the same matter in any other state – life accelerates entropy and chaos.
- Only humans create entropy for entertainment.
- Pleasure is associated with:
  - rarity (more energy needed to obtain)
  - order (requires effort and causes chaos elsewhere)
  - power (amount of work done per unit of time, regardless of energy used; usually the greater the power, the worse the energy efficiency)
- Historically: we sought rare resources, carefully accumulated food, and valued power as a means of defence.
- Hypothesis: if humans were to modify themselves towards plants (ability to photosynthesise), they would lose complex behaviours – perhaps that is why we have not encountered extraterrestrial civilisations.

## III. Excess

### 7. Norms, normality, defects and diseases

- We treat physical diseases; mental diseases are too often punished.
- Norms are behavioural boundaries that vary across cultures and historical periods.
- Normality in medical terms: what a properly functioning brain does and can do; the human brain has not changed since the invention of writing (~5,000 years ago) and is the same worldwide.
- Neurological diseases: the brain does not do enough of, or does poorly, what it should (e.g., Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease).
- Mental pathologies: the brain usually does too much; diagnosed by assessing the rationality of the cause, the frequency of the behaviour, and the degree to which it disrupts life.
- Neurological diseases are easier to diagnose – absence is easier to notice than excess.
- Addiction is a mental illness that limits freedom – like voluntary enslavement.

## 8. Obesity – a brain sick from the environment

- Obesity is normal biological behaviour in a changed environment.
- Obesity is a disorder of the exostatic system.
- Ways to limit excessive eating:
  - what to eat: less tasty food, with less sugar, salt and fat; one dish instead of three; food kept out of sight
  - when to eat: change mealtimes
  - with whom to eat: alone

## 9. Drug addiction – an increasingly sick brain

- Five categories of drugs: tobacco derivatives, alcoholic beverages, opioids (heroin and morphine), psychostimulants (cocaine and amphetamine), cannabinoids (marijuana).
- Tobacco, opioids, and marijuana substitute for neurotransmitters, acting on receptors in a similar but far more intense way.
- Psychostimulants and alcohol intensify the action of neurotransmitters by increasing the number of signals or the sensitivity of receptors.
- Drugs cause a rise in dopamine: a natural rise is small and falls after the stimulus; an artificial one is large and increases with each subsequent dose.
- Effects of drug use: impaired cognitive abilities, motivation, and motor function; impaired judgment; psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety.
- Addiction rates: tobacco 33%, cocaine 20%, heroin 20%, alcohol 20%, marijuana 10%.
- Marijuana is particularly harmful to young people, hindering memory and motivation; less harmful to adults.
- Relapse rates among addicted individuals reach up to 90%.
- The legality of drugs depends on their social impact: tobacco does not reduce social adaptation; alcohol is destructive only in large doses and is easy to dose; marijuana reduces social adaptation; cocaine makes a person feel that anything is permitted; opioids produce such intense pleasure that a person stops doing anything else.
- Stages of addiction: sporadic use → intensive and regular use → loss of control.
- A definition is unique and allows recognition of something even without prior experience of it; a description is not unique.
- Pathological drug use is characterized by invasiveness (more and more drugs, fewer and fewer other activities) and uncontrollability (inability to stop the behavior).
- Addiction is driven by individual susceptibility: an overactive dopaminergic system (much stronger experience of pleasure, due to genetic or environmental causes) or underdevelopment of the prefrontal cortex.
- Drugs initially impair synaptic plasticity in everyone; most people recover it, but addicts do not — their behavior becomes crystallized.
- The crystallization metaphor: the brain is a tank of water, behaviors are containers submerged in it; when the water freezes, a container cannot be removed — a hole remains, and there is a constant temptation to fill it with the only container still present; only about 10% of people manage to "melt the water back".
- Drug addiction is a disease and should be treated as such.
- Proposed solutions: greater funding for research, treatment of people in the early stages of addiction, legalization — but only when effective treatment is available.

## Epilogue

- Gene: the part of DNA used to create proteins; consists of a coding sequence (approx. 25,000 strands) and a regulatory sequence (accounting for about 93% of the gene).
- Hormones: mediators used by cells to communicate; they modify the activity of specific receptors in the recipient cell.
- Mechanism of hormone action: the hormone binds to the receptor due to its specific shape (key fits the lock) → the receptor changes shape (the key turns) → the cell changes its activity (the door opens).
- A neurotransmitter is another type of mediator.
- Mitochondrion: originated from the merging of a cell with a bacterium and reproduces in the same way; found in almost all cells; converts glucose into ATP — the universal fuel.
- Proteins: the building blocks of the body; based on a chain of 22 amino acids; differences between proteins arise from the sequence of amino acids, which is determined by DNA.
- Types of proteins by function: structural (body shape) and enzymes (the organism's metabolic capabilities).
- Types of proteins by location in the cell: cell membrane proteins, cytoplasmic proteins, nuclear proteins, and mitochondrial proteins.

## 3. Curing bad genes

- Inherited mutations: a single defect (in one parent, e.g. Alzheimer's disease) or a double defect (in both parents, e.g. sickle cell anaemia).
- Other genetic defects include an abnormal number of chromosomes (too many or too few).
- It is already possible to repair some genes using viruses.
- It is also possible to administer proteins that the organism cannot produce on its own.
- The vast majority of biological diseases result from a combination of genes and environment, meaning a great deal depends on our own actions.

## 4. Where do cancers come from?

- Some cancers are caused by viruses that penetrate a cell's DNA and alter it.
- Cancer occurs most often in dividing cells; it is rare in muscles and nerves.
- A damaged cell can send a division signal on its own, from the membrane to the nucleus, dividing repeatedly and without control.
- Three mutations are needed to produce cancer: in a proto-oncogene (the cell divides without permission — "the accelerator gets stuck"), in a suppressor gene (division control is lost — "the brakes fail"), and a mutation causing increased telomerase production (the cell gains nearly unlimited longevity — "the old car is constantly being repaired").
- Mutations can also be triggered by external factors: solar radiation, cigarettes, and food.

## 5. What do we inherit from our parents?

- We inherit 23 chromosomes from each parent.
- Two chromosomes determine sex (determined by the sperm): girls XX, boys XY.
- Genes account for approximately 50% of behavior, and environment for the other 50%.
- Indicators of environmental potential include the mother's level of education and the number of books at home.
- The brain has an innate potential that cannot be increased; to fully realize it, the brain must be properly nourished, free from toxins, and stimulated by the environment.
- Dominant inheritance: the trait needs to appear in only one parent; recessive: the trait must appear in both parents.
