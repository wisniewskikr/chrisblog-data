# Content

## A view from the heights of time's perspective

- We view other species from a human perspective – we compare them to ourselves, though we should not.
- Viewing the pilgrimage from a human perspective: only 40 meetings separate us from a single common ancestor.

## Main prologue

- The past can be studied through: fossils (divided into periods of mass extinction), refreshed relics (e.g. writing or DNA pointing to a primary source), and triangulation.
- The structure of DNA: 4 letters; 64 words made of 3 letters; 21 meanings (20 amino acids and one stop signal); sequences of several hundred meanings; a protein made of several sequences.
- Genes are like meaningful sentences interspersed with nonsensical passages (which are discarded), and even meaningful passages are sometimes ignored as unnecessary remnants.
- Taxonomic hierarchy: species (e.g. panther, lion, tiger) → genus (Panthera) → family (felids) → order (carnivores) → class (mammals) → phylum (chordates).

## Meeting 0. All of humanity

- The common ancestor of all living humans probably lived as recently as 10,000 years ago – not necessarily in Africa.
- That ancestor may have passed on no DNA at all, as it could have been lost along the way.
- Agriculture developed approximately 12,000 years ago.
- It took only 20 years of selective breeding to turn foxes into dog-like animals.
- The lactose-tolerance gene – previously switched off in adults – is an example of a recent genetic modification in humans; similarly, some people cannot tolerate gluten from grasses.
- Around 50,000 years ago a great cultural leap occurred, possibly enabled by the subjunctive mood in language.

## Archaic Homo sapiens

- The female line can be traced through mitochondria; the male line through the Y chromosome.
- Three branches of humanity: Homo sapiens, Neanderthals, Denisovans.
- FOXP2 is probably a gene involved in language.
- Genes consist of exons (translated) and introns (not translated).
- The modern human brain is about 6 times larger than body proportions would predict.
- Upright posture likely evolved to free the hands and allow food to be carried home.
- Humans first began walking upright, and only later did their brains enlarge.

## Meeting 1. Chimpanzees

- Chimpanzees are divided into common chimpanzees and pygmy chimpanzees (bonobos).
- The meeting took place approximately 6 million years ago (Eocene).
- The common ancestor probably used tools.
- A geographical barrier is necessary for a species to split.
- Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes; great apes have 24; one human chromosome was formed by the fusion of two.
- Genetic similarity between humans and chimpanzees is 98–99%.
- About 1–2% of the genome encodes proteins, 8% regulates gene expression, and 90% remains unused.
- Mutation rate: one per 2 billion letters of DNA per year.

## Meeting 2. Gorillas

- The meeting took place approximately 8 million years ago (Eocene).
- Gorillas are vegetarians; males surround themselves with a group of females.
- Apes (gorillas, chimpanzees) are distinguished from monkeys.

## Meeting 3. Orangutans

- The meeting took place approximately 14 million years ago; Asia (Eocene).
- About 20 million years ago, great apes moved from Africa to Asia (giving rise to gibbons and orangutans there), and later some returned to Africa.

## Meeting 4. Gibbons

- The meeting took place approximately 18 million years ago; Asia (Eocene).

## Meeting 5. Old World monkeys

- The meeting took place approximately 25 million years ago (Paleogene).

## Meeting 6. New World monkeys

- The meeting took place approximately 40 million years ago (Paleogene).
- They probably reached South America on vegetation rafts from Africa.
- In the Mesozoic, dinosaurs dominated during the day; mammals lived at night and lost colour vision, only to regain it later.
- Colour vision relies on cones: dichromatic animals (two types), trichromatic (three types), tetrachromatic (four types); humans have three types of cones (blue, green, red).
- The brain distinguishes colours by comparing the speed of signals transmitted by different cones.
- Mechanism of vision: retinal twists opsins; a photon hits opsin, which untwists and sends a signal; retinal then twists opsin again.

## Meeting 7. Tarsiers

- The meeting took place approximately 60 million years ago (Paleogene).
- Two strategies for night vision: large eyes that capture all available photons (as in owls), or a reflective membrane behind the retina that bounces light back for a second chance to capture photons (which is why cats' eyes glow in the dark).

## Meeting 8. Lemurs

- The meeting took place approximately 65 million years ago (Paleogene); Madagascar.

## The great extinction at the end of the Cretaceous

- The K-Pg boundary: approximately 66 million years ago – dinosaurs went extinct and the age of mammals began.
- A meteorite impact was the probable cause.
- Three models of mammal diversification after the extinction: the Big Bang model (only one mammal survived and gave rise to all others), the Delayed Explosion model (many mammals survived and diversified later), and the Non-Explosion model (the extinction event was not decisive; diversification proceeded regardless).
- The Delayed Explosion model is considered most likely.

## Meeting 9 and 10. Colugos and treeshrews

- The meeting took place approximately 70 million years ago (Cretaceous).
- Colugos can glide up to 70 metres.

## Meeting 11. Rodents and lagomorphs

- The meeting took place approximately 75 million years ago (Cretaceous).
- Genes can be compared to a dictionary – the number of words in almost any literary work is similar; what differs is their arrangement, the location of their use in the body, and the timing of their activity.
- A better analogy is subroutines: every cell contains the full set of subroutines and calls them in different sequences.
- Genes are responsible for building proteins, and proteins in turn influence the construction of organs and behaviour.

## Meeting 12. Laurasiatheria

- The meeting took place approximately 85 million years ago (Cretaceous).
- Hippopotamuses are most closely related to cetaceans.
- The sex ratio in biology corresponds to the ratio of offspring-rearing costs.
- In species with harems, males are typically much larger than females because they must compete for access.
- Distribution of human societies: 16% monogamous, 1% polyandrous (one woman – multiple partners), 83% polygynous.
- Gorillas: large bodies, small testes – harem system; chimpanzees: smaller bodies, large testes – sperm competition (one female, multiple partners).
- Humans represent mild polygyny.

## Meeting 13. Xenarthra and Afrotheria

- Continents drift apart from their centres – at approximately 1 cm per year.

## Meeting 14. Marsupials

- The meeting took place approximately 160 million years ago (Cretaceous).
- Mammals are divided into egg-laying monotremes, marsupials, and placental mammals (including humans).
- Marsupials reached Australia from the Americas via Greenland; probably from a single species evolved marsupial equivalents of mice, cats, dogs, and other mammals.

## Meeting 15. Monotremes

- The meeting took place approximately 180 million years ago (Early Jurassic).
- Monotremes lay eggs and have a single opening (cloaca) shared by the digestive, urinary, and reproductive systems.
- The platypus's bill acts as an antenna: when hunting in mud, the animal disables sight, hearing, and smell, relying instead on electrical and mechanical sensors – it calculates the distance to prey in a manner analogous to the gap between lightning and thunder.
- The star-nosed mole "sees" with its nose, which is exquisitely sensitive to touch, much like a human hand.

## Mammal-like reptiles

- Approximately 220 million years ago.
- At the end of the Permian, a mass extinction occurred caused by intense volcanism.

## Meeting 16. Sauropsids

- The meeting took place approximately 320 million years ago (second half of the Carboniferous).
- Includes snakes, swallows, birds, and others.
- Darwin's finches of the Galápagos Islands as an example of adaptive evolution.
- Hypotheses about human traits: lack of fur – to get rid of parasites and signal their absence; bipedalism – became fashionable and females preferred such males; large brain – producing art makes a male attractive.
- The dodo could fly but lost that ability when it became unnecessary.

## Meeting 17. Amphibians

- The meeting took place approximately 340 million years ago (Carboniferous).
- Amphibians are divided into caecilians (limbless), urodeles (salamanders), and anurans (frogs).
- Ring species: populations diverge and then reconnect at the same time.

## Meeting 18. Lungfish

- The meeting took place approximately 415 million years ago (Devonian).
- Lungfish breathe with both gills and lungs and can walk along the bottom.
- Fish moved onto land not to colonise it, but to reach another body of water.
- Spring tides (high) occur when the gravitational forces of the Sun and Moon are aligned and reinforce each other; neap tides (low) occur when they act at right angles and partially cancel each other out.

## Meeting 25. Lancelets

- The meeting took place approximately 540 million years ago.
- It is wrong to speak of animals as "higher" or "lower" – all have had the same amount of time to evolve.
- Fossils are almost certainly not our direct ancestors, but they can show us what those ancestors probably looked like.

## Meeting 26. Protostomes

- The meeting took place approximately 560 million years ago.
- Gastrulation – one of the most important stages of development: an indentation appears in the embryo; animals are divided into protostomes (mouth forms first; about one million species) and deuterostomes (mouth forms second; about 80,000 species, including humans).
- Humans descend from an animal that swam upside down.
- Ants can cultivate fungi on leaves, and also "farm" aphids to collect their honeydew.
- Species is defined by the ability to interbreed; race – by a genetic difference of 6–15%.
- Genetic differences between any two people in the world are most likely smaller than those between any two chimpanzees.

## Meeting 37. Amoebozoa

- They have no fixed shapes.
- From time to time they gather in groups for the purpose of reproduction.
- Chemical reactions leave the atomic nucleus unchanged; nuclear reactions change the atomic nucleus.
- The number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus is usually the same; electrons are disregarded. When there are more neutrons, the atom is called an isotope. Examples: carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons), carbon-13 (6 protons, 7 neutrons).
- Types of atomic decay:
  - Alpha: the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons; mass number decreases by 4, atomic number by 2. Example: uranium-238 → thorium-234.
  - Beta: a neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron; mass number unchanged, atomic number increases by 1. Example: sodium-24 → magnesium-24.
  - A neutron displaces a proton, taking its place; mass number unchanged, atomic number decreases by 1.
  - Electron capture: a proton captures an electron and becomes a neutron; mass number unchanged, atomic number decreases by 1.
- Half-life: the time required for half the atoms to decay; can range from seconds to billions of years.
- Termites are able to digest cellulose and lignin.

## The great historical meeting

- The eukaryotic cell arose through the merger of a cell with bacteria.
- Bacteria provided:
  - Photosynthesis: energy is used to synthesise organic compounds; oxygen is a by-product.
  - Oxidation: oxygen is used to burn organic compounds in order to recover energy.
- It is bacteria that convert light into organic compounds; plants merely provide them with a home.

## Canterbury

- In the beginning there was heredity – everything started from that.
- Fire exhibits reproduction without heredity: its behaviour comes from the environment, not from an ancestor.
- Replicators: DNA, computer viruses, chain letters – yet all of them require infrastructure.
- Oxygen exists because not all organic matter decomposes – some is stored in rocks. Oxygen was at first a poison, then organisms learned to cope with it, and eventually became dependent on it.
- Amino acids can arise without organisms, through chemical reactions alone; proteins are made of amino acids.
- Enzymes catalyse (speed up) biochemical reactions without taking part in them directly.
- Thousands of chemical compounds coexist inside a cell as if in a laboratory; enzymes seek out substrates and convert them into products.
- Enzymes are produced by genes but can be temporarily inactivated.
- Enzymes produce, among other things: sugars, fats, purines and pyrimidines (building blocks of DNA and RNA), and amino acids (building blocks of proteins).
- Protein synthesis:
  - DNA produces mRNA (messenger RNA).
  - A ribosome (enzyme) attaches to the mRNA and moves along the strand.
  - The ribosome takes successive amino acids floating in the cell, delivered by tRNA (transfer RNA) – each tRNA specialises in one amino acid.
  - An amino acid joins the ribosome via a codon–anticodon pair and is added to the growing polypeptide chain.
- Autocatalysis: instead of producing product Z, a catalyst produces more of itself; its number grows geometrically.
- Comparison of replicators:
  - DNA: good replicator, poor enzyme.
  - Proteins: good enzyme, poor replicator.
  - RNA: intermediate properties of both.

## Return of the host

- Factors influencing the possibility of life: gravity, distance from a star, axial tilt of the planet, presence of moons.
- Progress understood as the continuation into the future of trends from the past.
- Landmark events in the history of life:
  - The splitting of RNA functions into DNA and proteins.
  - The emergence of the eukaryotic cell from prokaryotic cells.
  - Sexual reproduction.
  - Multicellularity.
  - Segmentation.
  - The emergence of ant colonies and termite mounds.
  - The emergence of human societies.
- Cell division:
  - Mitosis: ordinary duplication of a cell.
  - Meiosis: the number of chromosomes is halved.
- The genome is the total set of genes in a single individual.
- The gene pool is the total set of genomes of all individuals in a population.
