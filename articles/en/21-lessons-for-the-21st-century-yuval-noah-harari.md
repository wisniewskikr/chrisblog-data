# Content

## Introduction
- The global world surrounds us with countless webs that on one hand restrict our movements, and on the other transmit our actions to distant points.

## I. The technological challenge

### 1. Disillusionment
- People tend to think in stories rather than facts, numbers or equations.
- There were three great stories: fascism, communism and liberalism. Currently there is none.
- People have always found it much easier to invent tools than to use them wisely.
- Freedom is worth little if it is not accompanied by some system of safeguards.
- Abraham Lincoln: "You can fool all the people some of the time, and some of the people all the time, but you cannot fool all the people all the time." – This does not work when artificial crises are constantly created and the media are countered.
- Main problems: global warming and a tremendous technological breakthrough.

## 2. Work
- People have two types of skills: physical and cognitive. In the future, machines may dominate in both areas.
- AI advantages: direct exchange of information and the ability to update.
- The greatest gains from AI are expected in medicine and transport.
- Human advantages: caring for people and creativity – although algorithms may surpass humans even in music.
- New jobs may appear, but they will require high qualifications.
- The chess program AlphaZero defeated Stockfish 8, teaching itself chess without human help – in four hours.
- Universal basic income: the state taxes corporations and the money is allocated to people's basic needs.
- Universal basic services: free education, transport, healthcare, etc.
- It will be necessary to find something to replace work and give life meaning – e.g. sport or religion.

## 3. Freedom
- Freedom in three spheres: political (the voter knows best), economic (the customer is always right) and personal (listen to yourself).
- Referendums and elections are about feelings, not rationality.
- Feelings are not free will – they are lightning-fast calculations we are unaware of.
- An algorithm combining biological and technological knowledge will manage our health for us.
- In the future, algorithms may decide about the choice of university or partner.
- The history of the world is a story of wonderful ideals and far from ideal behaviour.
- An autonomous car will have to decide whether in the event of an accident it sacrifices the owner or children running into the street.
- Dictatorships collapsed because centralised management was worse than distributed – but in the future this may be reversed.
- Possible scenarios: dictators order people to wear biometric bracelets; individual discrimination driven by algorithms emerges.
- We confuse intelligence with consciousness: intelligence is the ability to solve problems, consciousness is the capacity to feel.
- For every dollar invested in AI, we should invest a dollar in research on consciousness.

## 4. Equality
- Current inequality is enormous: the 100 richest people own as much as the poorest 4 billion.
- Inequality may deepen: the wealthiest will be able to buy health, beauty and intelligence, leading to a split between superhumans and useless Homo sapiens.
- The history of the concentration of power: land → machines → data.
- It is necessary to determine as soon as possible who owns data.

## II. The political challenge

### 5. Community
- People are increasingly lonely.
- Facebook is trying to use AI to build social networks.
- Online activity very often comes at the expense of offline contact.
- Perhaps instead of sharing experiences with others, one must first connect with one's own experiences.

## 6. Civilisation
- There is only one civilisation in the world – there is no clash of civilisations; civilisations change (e.g. Germany).
- Historically, people unite into ever larger groups; animals cannot do this – once they split into species, they do not reunite.
- Today states are unified: all have an anthem, a flag, etc. In the Middle Ages this would have been impossible.
- In practical matters (economy, hospitals, weapons) everything is the same everywhere, despite differences in religions and national identities.

## 7. Nationalism
- Nationalism has two aspects: the easy one (preferring similar people over foreigners) and the difficult one (preferring strangers over relatives).
- Mild nationalism (walled off but cooperative) has never succeeded in history.
- Strong nationalism (only our country matters) is not possible in the era of a global economy.
- Nationalism cannot defend against nuclear annihilation or ecological threats.
- Nationalism does not think at the level of technological challenges.
- We have a global ecology, a global economy and global science, but we are still stuck in national politics.

## 8. Religion
- Three types of problems: technical (e.g. how to obtain water?), political (e.g. how to prevent global warming?) and identity-related (e.g. should we care about problems on the other side of the world?).
- Priests once dealt with agriculture and medicine, but mainly justified failures while demanding faith in God.
- Today's religious leaders turn first to scientists and then seek justification in the holy book.
- Religions divide people; small differences in history and politics can change a great deal.
- Religions unite people into groups and combine with modern technology.
- Religions appear to be part of the problem, not the solution: they have a lot of power but contribute nothing new and serve mainly the interests of individual states.
- We are between a rock and a hard place: nationalism and religion on one side, global problems on the other.

## 9. Immigration
- Three conditions of immigration: the host country allows immigrants in; immigrants must accept the basic norms of the host country; if they assimilate, they become part of it.
- Supporters of immigration say it is a moral obligation; opponents say you can accept people but you can also choose.
- If immigrants are intolerant, it will give ammunition to opponents of immigration.
- The lack of a single definition of a European is a problem.
- Open questions: how long should assimilation take? When can the arrangement be considered broken?
- We now have cultural racism: individuals are very often judged on the basis of their culture, which is wrong because a person consists of culture, genes and personal history.

## III. Despair and hope

### 10. Terrorism
- Annual statistics: terrorists kill approximately 25,000 people (mainly in Afghanistan, Pakistan, etc.); road accidents – 1.25 million; diabetes – 3.5 million; air pollution – 7 million.
- Terrorists are like a fly that wants to destroy a china shop: it can do nothing by itself, but tries to fly into an elephant's ear so that the elephant destroys the china – because flies thrive on destruction.
- Terrorism is not military action, it is theatre.
- Terrorists are gamblers with weak cards: they have nothing to lose from reshuffling and everything to gain.
- Terrorism can make a lot of noise because political violence has been eliminated in Europe.
- How to counter it: quiet intelligence operations, not amplifying events in the media, freeing one's own imagination and not overreacting.
- When terrorists get hold of nuclear weapons, the situation will look completely different.

## 11. War
- In the 21st century the best strategy is to avoid getting involved in any conflicts.
- Russia also tends to avoid conflicts – it is not strong enough for a global conflict and lacks the latest technologies.
- In the past, economic assets were mainly material, so victory brought profits; today they are mostly immaterial, so one can lose a lot and gain little.
- Nuclear bombs raise the stakes.
- Cyber warfare may shift conflicts to new domains.
- One must never underestimate human stupidity – there are madmen, and even rational people often act very foolishly.

## 12. Humility
- Every nation considers itself the most important.
- Freud had a great influence on psychology, and his mother had a great influence on Freud – yet nobody talks about her.
- Morality is built into social groups.
- Monotheism is a bad invention: many people became much more intolerant because of it.
- Jews began to achieve success in science only in the 20th century for several reasons: moving away from orthodoxy, a strong emphasis on education, a desire to prove their worth, and other paths being closed off.

## 13. God
- People use the word "God" when they cannot explain something, and then expand the concept.
- Ethics has nothing to do with God – it is natural. Morality means reducing suffering.

## 14. Secularism

- Secularism holds that morality does not come from God but from biology.
- Secular values: truth, compassion, equality, freedom, courage, responsibility.
- Truth is important, but it should not be confused with belief.
- Compassion is the engine of ethics.
- Equality follows from truth and compassion, since both apply equally to the poor and the rich.
- Courage means being willing to admit ignorance and venture into the unknown; fear of the unknown can paralyse us more than many a tyrant.
- Responsibility rests with people.
- Stalin was secular in the narrow sense (he did not believe in God) but not in the broad sense – he did not adhere to principles.
- Human rights are not natural – they are a dogma.
- Every ideology has its dark history (Christianity, capitalism, communism); secularism's advantage is that it admits to its own.

## IV. Truth

- We have an illusion of knowledge; an example is asking people how a zip fastener works.
- Most views are shaped by group thinking, not rational deliberation.
- Power has no time for knowledge – it changes reality rather than trying to understand it.

## 16. Justice

- Our sense of justice evolved in small hunter-gatherer groups and may not work in today's world, where causal links are not clearly visible.
- Moral problems used to concern small groups; now they concern everyone, so all perspectives must be taken into account.
- Ways of coping with complex moral problems: minimising them, focusing on a single story, conspiracy theories, creating a dogma.

## 17. Post-truth

- Deception on the internet is widespread, but deception itself has a long history.
- People have always created fiction in order to unite.
- Truth has never been high on Homo sapiens' list of priorities; the power of cooperation depends on a balance between truth and fiction.
- We learn to respect sacred books in the same way we learn to respect banknotes.
- Truth and power will always diverge at some point.
- It is our responsibility to take the time and make the effort to uncover our own biases and verify information.
- Pay for high-quality information – free content takes your most valuable assets: attention and data.
- If something seems important, consult the expert literature.

## 18. Science fiction

- Science-fiction films are the most important source of scientific knowledge for many people and should therefore be responsible.
- From a young age we are fed films that are fiction; they cannot, however, be erased from the mind.
- The film *Inside Out* shows what happens when only happiness matters.

## V. Resilience

- Schools currently focus too much on transmitting information; this once made sense, but no longer does.
- The ability to distinguish what is important is now crucial.
- Experts argue that schools should shift to teaching the "four Cs": critical thinking, communication, cooperation, creativity.
- Key life skills: coping with change, learning new things, and maintaining mental balance in unfamiliar situations.
- In the future, periods of learning and applying knowledge will constantly overlap.
- Technology may take control over people.
- Listening to your inner self is risky, because it reflects what you have absorbed from outside.
- You need to know yourself, but corporations are doing the same at the same time; if they can do it better than you, power will shift to them.

## 20. Meaning

- Stories used to be cyclical (e.g. Arjuna, The Lion King).
- A good story concerns me and extends over a longer horizon than my own life.
- When a person believes in a story, they become absorbed in its details and lose interest in the world around them.
- People who do not believe in a soul want to leave something tangible behind: cultural (e.g. a poem) or biological (children).
- Perhaps the answer is goodness, or perhaps it is love.
- Once we have adopted a story, it is hard to change it.
- People believe in fictional stories through rituals and through suffering in the name of those stories.
- The word "hocus-pocus" comes from the Latin "Hoc est corpus!" – "This is the body!"
- If you are interested in truth, rituals are obstacles; if you are interested in stability, rituals are allies.
- A person is a mixture of many identities.
- The problem with evil is that it often looks attractive (e.g. fascism: what is bad is what is bad for the state).
- The universe does not give me meaning – I give meaning to the universe.
- We have no freedom to choose what we desire, so we have no free will.
- The Buddha taught three principles: everything changes, nothing is permanent, nothing can fully satisfy us; suffering arises from seeking God, nation, self, or love.
- The great question is: "How do we get rid of suffering?"
- When someone presents an idea, it is worth asking: "Does this idea suffer?"
- Be wary of the words: "sacrifice", "eternity", "purity" and "redemption".

## 21. Meditation

- Vipassana retreats: the content of the mind is closely linked to sensations in the body; if you want to know what anger is, observe the body.
- The real mystery is not what happens to a person after death, but what happens before death.
- Meditation is not a solution to problems – one must act, and in an organised way.
- For now, studying the brain and studying the mind are two different things.
- Meditation is a method of observing one's own mind.
- Destabilisation is growing more dangerous thanks to weapons, deception is growing easier thanks to television, and in the future things may be much worse.
