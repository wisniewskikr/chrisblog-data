# Content

## 1. The gap instinct

- This is an outdated instinct that sees conflict where there is agreement, and difference where there is consistency.
- Countries cannot be divided simply into poor and rich – that is already outdated.
- Currently 85% of countries have few children, and their child survival rate exceeds 95%.
- Low-income countries are far more developed than they appear; far fewer people live there too – only 9% of the population.
- Division of people by income: level 1 – 1 billion, level 2 – 3 billion, level 3 – 2 billion, level 4 – 1 billion.
- Warning signs of the gap instinct:
  - Comparing averages: you need to look not only at averages but also at the spread of data.
  - Comparing extremes: we like extremes because they are memorable, but most often it is average data that describes reality.
  - The view from above: looking from the top, it is very hard to notice details at the bottom, but looking from the bottom, every dollar makes a difference.

## 2. The negativity instinct

- This is the tendency to notice bad things more often than good ones.
- We tend to forget the bad things of the past, which leads us to idealise it and think things are worse now.
- Improvements happen more often than it seems, but information about them is harder to come by.

## 3. The straight line instinct

- World population over time: 5 million in 8000 BCE, 1 billion in 1800, 7.6 billion today, 10–12 billion in 2100 and then stabilisation.
- To reduce the number of children, poverty must be fought – then there is no need to guard against child deaths, no need for more hands to work, contraception can be used, and people want a better life for their children.
- Data does not always follow a straight line – types of lines:
  - Straight: e.g. income and education.
  - S-curve: e.g. literacy – nobody can read, rapid progress, everyone can read.
  - Reversed S: e.g. income and number of children – high number, sharp drop, small stable number.
  - Hump (normal distribution): e.g. number of road accidents – small, rises, then falls again.
  - Doubling: e.g. income and travel distance.

## 4. The fear instinct

- Two things must be distinguished: frightening – something that makes you feel afraid; dangerous – something that actually poses a threat.
- Risk = danger × exposure.

## 5. The size instinct

- To counter this instinct, the 80/20 rule can be applied: most often 2–3 factors account for 80% of results, and those are the ones to look for.
- A single number should trigger caution – it should be compared with others and divided by the total.

## 6. The generalisation instinct

- The gap instinct divides into "us" and "them," while the generalisation instinct lumps them all into one category.
- How to challenge this instinct:
  - Look for differences within a group.
  - Be wary of "the majority" – it covers both 51% and 99%.
  - Avoid exceptions.
  - Assume your experience is not the norm – if something looks strange, ask yourself why.
  - Be careful about generalising from one group to another.

## 7. The destiny instinct

- This is the belief that innate characteristics determine the fate of people, nations, or cultures.
- Such immutability was once true; it is no longer.
- Even a small change over a long period amounts to a large change.
- Knowledge needs to be updated regularly.

## 8. The single perspective instinct

- Simple ideas are attractive but most often untrue.
- You should think humbly about your current knowledge and approach information that contradicts it with curiosity.
- It is worth relying on expert knowledge, while remembering that experts are only experts in their own field.
- The world cannot be understood without numbers, but neither can it be understood through numbers alone.
- The USA spends more on healthcare than any other country in the world, yet its healthcare is worse than that of many level-4 countries – due to the lack of basic health insurance.
- Appreciate complexity, seek compromise.

## 9. The blame instinct

- We like to find someone to blame for a situation because it gives us a clear picture of it.
- Independence is not the same as representativeness – journalists choose their topics in some way.
- Look for causes, not culprits – when looking for a scapegoat, you often stop there and go no further.

## 10. The urgency instinct

- When we feel fear and time pressure, we can make truly bad decisions.
- Five global threats: a global pandemic, a financial crisis, a world war, a climate crisis, extreme poverty.
- Eliminating extreme poverty requires: peace, education, healthcare, electricity, clean water, toilets, contraception, and microcredits.
