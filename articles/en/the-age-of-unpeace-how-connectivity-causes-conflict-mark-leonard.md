# Content

## Connectivity: connection can cause:

- Cooperation
- Conflict (the Greek word *pharmakon* means both a remedy and a poison)

Current options:
- Open
- Closed

## Part one. Opportunity

### 1. The great convergence

- China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001
- 2001: China's economy was 1/10 of the USA's; 2019: China's economy was 2/3 of the USA's
- China's Sputnik moment: 2016, when AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol at Go

Chinese equivalents of Western platforms:
- Google – Baidu
- Amazon – Alibaba
- WhatsApp – WeChat
- Twitter – Weibo
- Uber – Didi Chuxing

## Part two. Reasons

### 2. The connected man

- Tinder was created as a copy of Grindr, an app for gay men
- The internet leads to polarization: people, given the choice, seek out those similar to themselves
- The intimacy and segregation of the internet lead to a regression in behaviour
- Belonging to a group is an essential element of identity
- The problem with artificial intelligence: imprecisely defined conditions can lead a system to make undesirable decisions

Algorithms:
- Transmit biases
- Reinforce biases
- Shape partisan attitudes

Sovereigns across the ages:
- Pre-modern era: God
- Modern era: the State
- Liberalism: the Individual
- The future: the Algorithm

## 3. National cultures of unpeace

- Distribution of wealth: in 1979 the top 1% held 12% and the bottom 20% held 13%; in 2016 the top 1% held 20% and the bottom 20% held only 13%
- Two groups in public debate: knowledge and emotions

## 4. The geopolitics of connectivity

- Interdependence both provokes wars and restrains them

Stages:
- War
- Perpetual conflict (like tensions at the holidays)
- Peace

Hybrid warfare tools:
- Electoral interference
- Disinformation
- Cyberattacks
- Drones
- Finance

- Muslim rivalry: Iran (Shia) versus Saudi Arabia (Sunni)

## III. Weapons and warriors

### 5. The anatomy of unpeace

- Syria: Putin backed Assad, Erdoğan (Turkey) backed the rebels

State approaches to migration:
- Generators: generate migration
- New colonialists: encourage their own citizens to emigrate (e.g. China and India)
- Brokers: benefit from their position between migrants and their destination
- Integrators: accept migrants and benefit from doing so

Status tendencies:
- Power over law
- Politics over science
- Nationalism over internationalism

- Since 1988 CO₂ emissions have risen by 40%
- Since the 1960s more than half of the rainforests have been cut down

## 6. The new topography of power

Centuries:
- 19th century: unification (e.g. the standardization of time)
- 20th century: division (e.g. the dissolution of the Soviet Union)

Structures:
- Hierarchy
- Network: strength is determined by the weakest link (e.g. semiconductor shortages); networks are flexible but spread infections quickly; well-connected networks grow stronger still – the rich get richer

- In politics, the shift must go from the chessboard to the network, from divisions to building relationships

Seven strategies of leverage:
1. Central position: others need you more than you need them
2. Acting as a gatekeeper: deciding who is in the network and who is not
3. Data extraction: spying on others
4. Subversion: penetrating the systems of others
5. Infiltration: changing other countries from within, e.g. through the diaspora
6. Setting the rules: creating norms and rules for the whole network
7. Pursuing independence: minimizing one's own dependence

## 7. Empires of connectivity

Main empires:
- USA
- China
- Europe
- The rest of the world

## Conclusion

- Humanity needs therapists, not architects

Steps:
1. Face the problem

4. Take care of yourself
