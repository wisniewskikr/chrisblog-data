# Content

## Introduction

- 19th century: physical revolution – the atom, transistors, lasers.
- 20th century: information revolution – the microprocessor, the internet.
- 21st century: biological revolution – the genetic code.

## Part I. The origins of life

### Chapter 1. Hilo

- Jennifer Doudna was born in Washington in 1964 but grew up in Hawaii.
- Because of her blonde hair she was called Haole – a person from outside the islands.
- She was the firstborn child and has two younger sisters.
- Her father wrote speeches for the Pentagon and later lectured in literature.
- Other children bullied her; she sought refuge in books and befriended Lisa Hinkley.
- She developed a fascination with biology and played wing on a soccer team.
- After reading Watson's "The Double Helix" she decided to become a scientist.

## Chapter 2. The gene

- Watson and Crick discovered the structure of DNA.
- Darwin: the voyage of the Beagle and observation of finches.
- Mendel in Brno (Czech Republic): wanted to become a teacher but failed his exams; by cross-breeding peas he discovered dominant and recessive traits.

## Chapter 3. DNA

- It was initially thought that proteins carried life's information; later it was discovered that nucleic acids do.
- Nucleic acids consist of sugars, phosphates, and four bases.
- Types: RNA (ribonucleic acid) and DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid – one fewer oxygen atom).
- James Watson: a middle-class family from Chicago, very talented, enjoyed birdwatching; studied in Chicago, earned his doctorate in Indiana, researched bacteriophages; moved to Copenhagen and then to Cambridge.
- Francis Crick: a biochemist who served in the military during World War II.
- Wilkins and Franklin quarrelled over priority; Watson and Crick were forbidden from working on DNA.
- American Pauling was close to discovering the structure of DNA but was stopped by the FBI at the airport because of his pacifist views.
- Wilkins showed Watson photograph No. 51 without Franklin's consent – it confirmed Watson's hypotheses.
- Watson and Crick established: two sugar-phosphate chains from which four nitrogenous bases project – adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine.
- In the last weekend of March 1953 they completed the article describing the structure of DNA.

## Chapter 4. A biochemist's education

- Doudna was admitted to Pomona College in California to study chemistry.
- She completed summer internships at the University of Hawaii under Hemmes.
- She pursued doctoral studies at Harvard, completed her doctorate under Szostak, and they began working on RNA.

## Chapter 5. The human genome

- The Human Genome Project began in 1990, led by Watson (whose son had schizophrenia) and later by Collins.
- Venter invented an alternative, faster, and cheaper sequencing method, sparking a rivalry.
- In 2000, at Clinton's request, the two groups joined forces; the project cost 3 billion dollars.

## Chapter 6. RNA

- DNA stays in the cell nucleus and stores information; RNA travels and builds proteins.
- Types of proteins: fibrous (structural), membrane (information transfer), and enzymes (initiating and regulating processes).
- Ribozymes: RNA molecules that act like enzymes.

## Chapter 7. Twists and folds

- Doudna married Tom Griffin, a medical school student; they divorced after a few years.
- She moved to the University of Colorado under Tom Cech to study the structure of RNA, specifically ribozymes.
- RNA is built from only 4 elements (proteins from 20); the function of RNA is determined by its folding.
- An accidentally broken incubator thermostat revealed that a higher temperature was needed.
- Doudna moved to Yale, where molecules were cooled with liquid nitrogen.
- During this period her father died.

## Chapter 8. Berkeley

- In 2000 Doudna married Jamie Cate, also a researcher; two years later their son Andrew was born.
- They moved to Berkeley, California.
- In 2002 a SARS-CoV epidemic broke out in China – a coronavirus built from RNA, not DNA.
- RNA interference: interference with RNA while it carries data from DNA; this is carried out by the enzyme Dicer.

## Part II. CRISPR

### Chapter 9. Clustered repeated sequences

- Mojica discovered repeating sequences in DNA and coined the name CRISPR.
- He discovered that this is how bacteria remember viruses (phages) that attacked them.
- Phages are the most abundant entities on Earth – one trillion per grain of sand.
- Bacteria and phages have been fighting for approximately 3 billion years.

## Chapter 10. Free Speech Movement Cafe

- Jillian Banfield from Australia – a microbiologist studying bacteria in saline waters.

## Chapter 11. Getting to work

- Blake Wiedenheft joined Doudna's lab as a postdoctoral researcher; he had graduated from the University of Montana and spent 3 years travelling the world.
- Martin Jinek – another team member, from the Czech Republic, a crystallographer.
- Cas1 is an enzyme found in the CRISPR system.

## Chapter 12. The yogurt makers

- Basic science driven by curiosity gives rise to inventions; sometimes the relationship is reversed (e.g. steam engines, aeroplanes).
- Danish company Danisco used CRISPR to protect yogurt bacteria from viruses.

## Chapter 13. Genentech

- Genentech was the first biotechnology company; it created synthetic insulin.
- Doudna worked there for 2 months.

## Chapter 14. The laboratory

- Doudna returned to Berkeley.

## Chapter 15. Caribou

- Doudna and Haurwitz, her former doctoral student, founded the company Caribou.
- The success of companies in the USA rests on collaboration between government, business, and academic institutions.

## Chapter 16. Emmanuelle Charpentier

- How CRISPR works: crRNA guides the enzymes to their target and contains a fragment of the virus's DNA; the Cas9 enzyme cuts genes; tracrRNA generates crRNA and serves as a scaffold for crRNA and Cas9 when they attach to the virus.
- The team that discovered CRISPR-Cas9: Emmanuelle Charpentier, Jennifer Doudna, Martin Jinek, and Krzysztof Chyliński.

## Chapter 17. CRISPR-Cas9

- sgRNA: a fusion of crRNA and tracrRNA into a single molecule.

## Chapter 18. Science, 2012

- The team submitted an article describing CRISPR-Cas9 to the journal Science.

## Chapter 19. The presentation duel

- Lithuanian scientist Siksnys was very close to independently publishing similar results.

## Part III. Gene editing

### Chapter 20. A human tool

- In 1972 Berg of Stanford cut and spliced a fragment of a virus – a pioneering step in genetic engineering.
- In 1990 the first clinical trial of biological modification on a human was conducted: a young girl's immune system was corrected.

## Chapter 21. The race

- In 2012 a race began to prove that CRISPR could edit human genes.
- Competitors: Jennifer Doudna, Feng Zhang of the Broad Institute, and George Church of Harvard.

## Chapter 22. Feng Zhang

- Zhang was first fascinated by computers, then switched to biotechnology; he was a doctoral student of Church.

## Chapter 23. George Church

- Church was expelled from Duke for spending whole days in the laboratory instead of attending lectures, but was accepted by Harvard.

## Chapter 24. Zhang takes on CRISPR

- There is no evidence that Zhang succeeded with CRISPR before the publication of Doudna's article.

## Chapter 25. Doudna enters the race

- Aleksandra East, who had experience with human cells, joined the team.

## Chapter 26. The photoelectric cell

- Church and Zhang published their articles in the same issue of Science.

## Chapter 27. Doudna's home stretch

- Doudna published her results in the journal eLife.

## Chapter 28. Starting companies

- Editas Medicine was founded with Doudna, Church, and Zhang; Doudna soon left.
- Three companies were established: CRISPR Therapeutics (Charpentier and Nowak), Editas Medicine (Zhang and Church), and Intellia Therapeutics (Doudna and Haurwitz).

## Chapter 29. Mon amie

- Doudna and Charpentier won three awards: the Breakthrough Prize, the Gairdner Award, and the Kavli Prize.

## Chapter 30. The heroes of CRISPR

- Eric Lander published an article in Cell that overlooked Doudna's role.

## Chapter 31. Patents

- Patents were invented in 1474 in the Republic of Venice.
- Zhang removed Marraffini from the patent application and thereby lost the European patent.

## Part Four. CRISPR in action

### Chapter 32. Therapies

- Victoria Gray was the first person in the USA treated with CRISPR – her sickle cell anaemia was switched off; the cost of therapy was 1 million dollars, which Doudna wanted to change.
- Treatment methods: ex vivo (cells extracted, edited, and reinjected) and in vivo (CRISPR components delivered directly into cells).
- Other applications: cancer treatment (China is a pioneer) and treatment of congenital blindness.

## Chapter 33. Biohackers

- Biohackers want people to do biology at home, just as they once did with computers.

## Chapter 34. DARPA and anti-CRISPR

- DARPA – the US Department of Defense agency – began funding research into anti-CRISPR systems.
- Some viruses possess their own anti-CRISPR systems.

## V. The scientist as a public figure

### 35. The highway code

- Preimplantation genetic diagnosis: eggs are fertilised in a dish, the embryos are tested, and the best one is selected.
- The film Gattaca – the title is a combination of the letters G, T, A, and C; a fake advertisement placed as a promotion generated 50,000 phone calls in two days.
- Types of genetic changes: somatic (non-heritable) and germline (heritable).

## 36. Doudna takes action

- A conference in the Napa Valley addressed CRISPR and ethics.
- Chinese experiments editing human embryonic cells were revealed.

## Part VI. The CRISPR children

### 37. He Jiankui

- He Jiankui in China modified the CCR5 gene so that the children of an HIV-positive father would be resistant to the virus.
- His US supervisor, Deem, knew about the experiment.
- Two girls were born: Nana and Lulu.

## 38. The Hong Kong summit

- He Jiankui made a public appearance at a conference in Hong Kong.

## 39. Acceptance

- He Jiankui was sentenced to 3 years in prison, a fine of 430,000 dollars, and a lifetime ban on conducting reproductive experiments.

## VII. Ethical challenges

### 40. Uncrossable lines

- Three categories of genetic modification: treatment, enhancement, and prevention.

## 41. Thought experiments

- Diseases considered in ethical discussions: Huntington's disease and sickle cell anaemia.
- A deaf lesbian couple deliberately sought a donor so that their child would also be deaf.
- Eero Mäntyranta – Olympic champion in cross-country skiing – has a rare mutation giving him 25% more red blood cells.
- Absolute benefits: everyone gains (e.g. resistance to viruses); positional benefits: everyone improves but no one gains an advantage (e.g. height).

## 42. Who should make the decision?

- The dilemma: what matters more – the individual or society? Schizophrenia, which affected many great artists, is cited as an example.

## 43. Doudna's ethical path

- Doudna advocates for modifications that are strictly necessary from a medical point of view.

### VIII. Dispatches from the front

## 44. Quebec

- Sternberg and Zhang published articles on jumping genes at the same time.

## 45. Learning to edit

- The difficulty of delivering CRISPR into the cell nucleus, where DNA is located.

## 46. Watson once more

- Watson advocated improving human intelligence and appearance.
- He questioned the intelligence of Africans, which led to his social exile.
- Watson proposed abandoning patents in biotechnology — results would be slower, but cheaper and fairer.

## 47. Doudna's visit

- Doudna visited Watson, whose book had a profound influence on her.

### IX. Coronavirus

## 48. To arms

- The first official Covid-19 death was recorded on 9 January 2020.
- On the same day, the Chinese published the genetic sequence of the virus.

## 49. Tests

- Tests created by the USA were defective; tests offered by the WHO were also not adopted.
- Due to bureaucracy, the FDA took a very long time to approve new tests.

## 50. Diagnostic laboratory in Berkeley

- Doudna established a laboratory for testing samples for the presence of Covid-19.

## 51. Mammoth and Sherlock

- Doudna discovered that Cas12, and Zhang that Cas13, cut and then shred RNA.
- On this basis, they created the diagnostic tools Mammoth and Sherlock.

## 52. Coronavirus tests

- Doudna and Zhang developed home tests for the coronavirus.

## 53. Vaccines

- Traditional vaccines work by injecting an inactivated virus, after which the immune system produces antibodies.
- English physician Jenner is considered a pioneer of vaccination: he noticed that milkmaids who had contracted cowpox did not get smallpox; he tested this on a gardener's son.
- Modern vaccines deliver DNA or RNA into a cell, which then produces the antigen itself.
- An mRNA vaccine does not need to enter the cell nucleus, unlike a DNA vaccine.

## 54. CRISPR medicines

- Doudna is working on new CRISPR-based medicines — not vaccines, but drugs.

## 55. Virtual Cold Spring Harbor

- Due to the Covid-19 pandemic, the annual meeting at Cold Spring Harbor was held virtually.

## 56. The Nobel Prize

- In October 2020, Doudna and Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- By 2020, only 5 of the 184 Nobel Prizes in Chemistry had been awarded to women.
