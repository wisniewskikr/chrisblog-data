# Content

## Introduction
- Talking to strangers wonderfully disrupts the predictable narrative of everyday life.
- As a citizen of the street, you have two duties:
  - Be kind and approach others with respect.
  - Protest loudly in the face of aggressive verbal and physical attacks – as long as your intervention will not make things worse.

## 1. Who is a stranger?
- We categorize people, and sometimes we do something worse – we stereotype.
- Stereotyping is fast and easy; paying attention is difficult and requires effort – it needs to be practiced.

## 2. Fleeting intimacy
- It is worth talking to strangers – we wake up to life.
- It is important to let someone know that they have been noticed.
- We are constantly negotiating: to open up or not. If you open up, you risk being hurt; if you don't, you won't wake up to life.

### Listening
- Research shows that openness leads to openness.
- If we are interested in someone, we mirror their movements; if not – we don't.

## 3. A world full of strangers
- Fear is easier than taking risks, but it leads to restrictions. Talking to strangers is a remedy for fear.
- The more we get used to something, the better we feel – the mere exposure effect.

### Cosmopolitanism
- We are citizens of the world, not of a state, but being a cosmopolitan requires empathy.
- Empathy depends on stress levels (the less, the better), a sense of connection, and early childhood – it is not innate but develops or fades during that period.
- Cosmopolitanism is an obligation for the privileged, but its absence may be a necessity for the poor.

### Contact and prejudice
- The contact hypothesis: a positive contact with one person transfers to the entire group.
- Negative experiences are much stronger than positive ones.

## 4. The mechanics of interaction
- There are unwritten rules about what is and is not acceptable in public space.

### Civil inattention
- The dilemma: how to behave around a stranger – to greet them or not?

### The gaze
- We use our gaze to signal whether we want to enter into closer interaction – we look longer if we do.

### Greeting
- One needs to know when to greet and when not to.
- We are more inclined to greet when we are in the minority – e.g., Europeans in Vietnam.
- We do not greet when we are in a hurry – an example from Gambia.

### This is the place
- Some places are more open to interaction than others, e.g., a sports hall.

### Exit strategy
- In theory, the person who initiated the interaction has the right to end it first.

## Expeditions
- Watching people.
- Say hello to everyone.
- Let's go get lost.
- Ask a question, e.g.: what are you afraid of?
- This is not your place.
