# Content

## Preface

- Networks and hierarchies interact with and influence each other.

## I. Introduction: networks and hierarchies

### 1. The mystery of the Illuminati

- The Order of the Illuminati, founded in Germany, aimed at reliability; members used code names.
- Three levels of initiation: Novice, Minerva, and Illuminated Minerva.
- The Bavarian government banned the order.

## 2. Our networked age

- We live in the age of networks.
- Networks also existed in the past.

## 3. Networks everywhere you look

- Networks are ubiquitous in nature.
- Humans are born for social networks.
- Yet for most of history, people lived within hierarchies.

## 4. Why we need hierarchies

- Hierarchies originated in family and tribal clans.
- Hierarchies proved effective.
- Their weakness is difficulty in handling complexity.

## 5. From seven bridges to six degrees

- Graph theory began in Königsberg with the problem of seven bridges – it was impossible to cross each bridge exactly once.
- Homophily: the tendency to associate with people similar to ourselves.
- Degree centrality: the number of connections a node has.
- Betweenness centrality: the intensity of a node's connections.
- Closeness centrality: the average number of steps between nodes.
- Any person on Earth can be reached through at most five intermediaries.

## 6. Weak ties and virals

- The difference between a small world and a large one is sometimes just a few edges.
- The utility of a network grows proportionally to the square of the number of connected devices.
- A meme is a spreading idea – to go viral, it must originate in the right node.

## 7. The variety of networks

- Networks typically have one highly connected node, followed by moderately connected nodes, and finally the least connected ones.

## 8. When networks meet

- Networks are frequently attacked by hierarchical structures.

## 9. Seven assumptions

- No one is an island.
- Birds of a feather flock together.
- Weak ties are actually strong.
- Structure determines how virals emerge.
- Networks never sleep.
- Networks connect with one another into networks.
- The rich get richer.

## 10. The illumination of the Illuminati

- The order was founded by the academic Weishaupt.
- He worked at a Jesuit university while holding Enlightenment convictions.
- The Illuminati infiltrated the Freemasons.
- They admitted Baron Knigge, who revolutionised the order.
- They were accused of causing the French Revolution, but ideas flowed in the opposite direction: from France to Bavaria.

## II. Emperors and conquerors

### 11. A brief history of hierarchies

- Hierarchies preceded networks, being more natural – based on physical strength or intellectual ability.
- The Roman Empire was overcome partly by religious networks: Christian and Muslim.

## 12. The first age of networking

- The Black Death struck Europe.
- In the west of the continent, hierarchies were less firmly established.

## 13. The art of doing business in the Renaissance

- Cotrugli, in his work on the art of trade, argued that a merchant should be well-rounded.

## 14. The explorers

- The age of geographical discovery was initiated by King Henry the Navigator.
- The Chinese initially repelled the Portuguese.
- They eventually opened up to the Portuguese after the latter helped them deal with pirates and the Dutch.

## 15. Zarro and the Incas

- The Portuguese expanded eastward, the Spanish westward – with the exception of Brazil.
- The conquistadors triumphed mainly through disease.
- The Incas were weakened by internal conflicts.
- The Spanish married into Inca aristocracy.

## 16. When Gutenberg met Luther

- Gutenberg's first printing press was produced in 1446.
- Without Gutenberg, Luther might have remained an anonymous burned heretic.
- Luther's 95 Theses launched Protestantism.
- A thirty-year religious war followed.

## III. Letters and lodges

### 17. The economic consequences of the Reformation

- The Reformation contributed to the secularisation of Europe.
- The price of books fell by 90%.

## 18. The trade in ideas

- In earlier times, the most important academic centre was Padua, centrally located in Italy.

## 19. The networks of the Enlightenment

- The main figures of Enlightenment networks were Voltaire, Rousseau, and d'Alembert (editor of the Encyclopédie).
- These were, however, largely national networks: French, Scottish, and English.

## 20. The networks of revolution

- In the USA, Revere warned of British troop movements.
- Freemasonry originated in Scotland; Freemasons believed that the first mason was Adam, then Moses, and that they had built Solomon's Temple.
- Americans had a particular fondness for associations.

## IV. The restoration of hierarchy

### 21. Red and black

- At the Congress of Vienna in 1815, rule by the great five powers was established: England, France, Austria, Prussia, and Russia – the so-called Pentarchy.

## 22. From mob to tyranny

- Revolution leads to anarchy, and anarchy to tyranny.
- Napoleon worked up to 16 hours a day and had a passion for detail.
- Napoleon slept only in short naps.

## 23. Restored order

- The years 1815–1914 were a period of relative peace in Europe, thanks to the rule of the Pentarchy.

## 24. The House of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha

- The dynasty traced its origins to Augusta; her son Leopold became King of Belgium, and his descendants included Queen Victoria.

## 25. The Rothschild family

- The Rothschilds came from the Frankfurt ghetto.
- They introduced financial innovations.
- They married within the family.
- They made government officials financially dependent on them.
- They maintained a network of private couriers.

## 26. Industrial networks

- Nathan Rothschild began as a textile merchant in Britain, exporting textiles to Germany.
- He profited from raw materials, dyes, and finished products.
- England avoided revolution thanks to the petition system and political flexibility (e.g. Wilberforce and the anti-slavery movement).

## 27. From pentarchy to hegemony

- By 1900, eleven Western powers controlled 60% of the Earth's surface.
- England governed its colonies with a minimal number of soldiers and officials.

## V. The Knights of the Round Table

### 28. Life in the empire

- Buchan, alongside Kipling, was a writer depicting British imperialism (novel "The Thirty-Nine Steps").
- He belonged to an association known as the "Kindergarten" or "Round Table".

## 29. Empire

- England governed by reaching agreements with local authorities.
- An informal hierarchy of titles: "Call Me God", "Kindly Call Me God", and "God Calls Me God".
- Railways and the telegraph enabled the empire's development.
- The Scot Pender founded the Eastern Telegraph Company, which controlled one-third of global telegraph connections.
- Concentration intensified: fewer and fewer but ever larger companies emerged.

## 30. The Taiping Rebellion

- Qing dynasty China was a bureaucratic monarchy governed not by aristocrats but by talented officials.
- The years 1815–1914 were peaceful in Europe but extremely bloody in China – the Taiping Rebellion was led by the Heavenly King, who preached a mutated form of Christianity.
- The British supported the Qing dynasty to preserve the status quo.

## 31. Out with the Chinese

- Local unrest, prosperity elsewhere, and cheap transport triggered mass migrations.
- An exodus of Europeans to the Americas.
- An exodus of Chinese to India and Australia.
- An exodus of Russians to Manchuria, Siberia, and Central Asia.
- The USA introduced restrictions on immigration from China.

## 32. The Union of South Africa

- Socialist movements emerged.
- In Africa, the Boers were defeated and interned in concentration camps.

## 33. The Apostles

- Oxford was seen as strongly masculine, Cambridge as effeminate.
- The Conversazione Society at Cambridge was an exclusive philosophical circle; students held the title of "apostles" and former members were known as "angels".

## 34. Armageddon

- The Pentarchy became unstable once England and France allied against Germany.
- Disputes were aggravated rather than eased.
- British Foreign Secretary Grey may have been partly responsible for World War I by acting too late.
- The war broke out because England failed to maintain a balance between Germany and Austria-Hungary on one side and France and Russia on the other.

## VI. Epidemics and pipers

### 35. Greenmantle

- Towards the end of World War I, epidemics raged: influenza, Marxism, nationalism, and hyperinflation.
- Neutral countries in Europe: the Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland, and the Scandinavian states.
- Germany sought to turn Islam against England.

## 36. Epidemic

- Germany sent Lenin to Russia; he had been living in exile in Switzerland.
- The Russians did not arrest him or his associates after their arrival by train.
- After the failed July coup, the Bolsheviks received an amnesty.
- In October, the Bolsheviks seized the weapons stores in the Peter and Paul Fortress.
- Soldiers defected to the Bolsheviks en masse.
- Lenin declared that a revolution without a firing squad is pointless.
- In August, Fanny Kaplan attempted to assassinate Lenin.
- In response, the Cheka was established under Dzerzhinsky.
- The Gulag system was created – 18 million people passed through it (approximately 15% of the population).
- Stalin became party secretary; his power rested on bureaucracy, communications, and the police.

## 37. The leadership principle

- Fascism was fuelled by the Great Depression.
- The Weimar Republic suffered from conflict between workers and employers and from fragmentation into small parties serving narrow interests.
- Other parties had interests; the Nazis had spirit.
- Stalin preferred control; Hitler preferred chaos and internal rivalry.

## 38. The fall of the golden internationale

- Hitler simultaneously spoke of a great Jewish conspiracy and of Jewish weakness.
- Jews made up 1% of Germany's population.
- Jews accounted for one-fifth of millionaires.
- Jews held 16% of executive positions in companies.
- Of the Jews living in Germany at the outbreak of the war, only one in ten survived.

## 39. The Cambridge spy ring

- The communist spies known as the Cambridge Five actually numbered around ten people.
- During World War II, British intelligence was completely penetrated by the Soviets.
- The cause was poor counter-intelligence operations.
- The network was exposed after the defection of cipher clerk Gouzenko in Ottawa.
- None of the Five was ever tried or convicted.

## 40. A brief encounter in the Soviet Union

- The meeting of Berlin with Akhmatova.

## 41. Ella in the reformatory

- 110 million people served in World War II; 30 million died.
- The Mafia was a hierarchical organisation; it was broken up once it adopted a clear hierarchical structure.
- Alcoholics Anonymous was founded in 1935 in Akron by stockbroker Bill W. and physician Dr. Bob.

## VII. Taking command of the jungle

### 42. The long peace

- There was no direct war between East and West thanks to nuclear weapons.
- After World War II, peace existed only in Europe; on other continents, states fought through proxies.

## 43. The general

- Forester's novel "The General" depicts the blind following of orders.
- In jungle conditions, however, originality and initiative proved more important.
- Walter Walker developed the concept of counter-insurgency warfare.

## 44. The crisis of complexity

- Central planning works well in wartime but not in trade.

## 45. Henry Kissinger's power network

- Kissinger served as Nixon's adviser.
- Kissinger was distinguished by his exceptional ability to build networks of contacts.

## 46. March to the valley

- The internet was born when the government sought a way to protect communications from a nuclear attack
- The theory was developed by Baran
- The first electronic message was sent between Stanford University and the University of California
- In 1983 ARPANET switched to TCP/IP
- In 1984 the first DNS was created
- In 1989 Berners-Lee, a scientist at CERN, developed the WWW – HTTP, HTML, and URL

## 47. The fall of the Soviet empire

- In Poland the Solidarity network was formed: intellectuals, the church, and workers
- In 1989 free elections were held in Poland
- Free elections in Hungary
- In 1991 the Soviet Union collapsed
- In 1989 protests in China were suppressed

## 48. The triumph of the man from Davos

- Schwab organised meetings in Davos, Switzerland
- Mandela was imprisoned for a long time on Robben Island
